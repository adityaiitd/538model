# C1 v2: Islanded prime power blocks for mid-size AI sites (redesign after round one)

## What changed and why
Round one killed v1 on five points. This version answers them as follows.

| Round-one objection | Change in v2 |
|---|---|
| No rule in force grants earlier grid energization for a battery commitment | The product no longer depends on the utility. The plant runs islanded behind the meter. |
| The utility holds the breaker and contracts only with the customer | No utility obligation is held. Grid service, when it arrives, is the customer's own. |
| Four hours of battery does not cover real events | Gas engines run continuously. The battery only follows AI load swings and covers engine trips. |
| Real cost $68-98M per 50 MW battery block, 6-8% equity return at the clearing price | Asset is gas engines plus a small battery at about $1.45M per MW, priced at the gas market rate. |
| Creditworthy buyers self-supply; only weak credits sign | Target the weak-credit buyers on purpose, with short terms, cash security and redeployable assets instead of tenant credit. |

## What is sold
Prime power for AI sites of 25-150 MW whose tenants or GPUs are waiting for power. A standard block is 25 MW net of natural-gas reciprocating gensets with spare units (N+2), a 10 MW / 20 MWh grid-forming battery for load-following, switchgear, controls, and operations and maintenance. Blocks are skid-mounted and designed to move to another site at the end of the term. When grid service arrives the customer can keep the plant as backup at a lower fee or release it.

## Standard unit
One firm megawatt for one month at 99.9% contractual availability.

## Price and contract
- $38,000 per MW-month capacity fee, fuel passed through at cost. Reference: derived from Solaris backlog in the research notes.
- Seven-year take-or-pay, against 11-15 years asked by the large providers. Customer may release the block after year four on payment of 12 months of fees.
- Security for sub-investment-grade customers: six months of fees held in cash or letter of credit, plus a direct agreement with the end tenant where the customer is a colocation or neocloud landlord.
- Availability guarantee backed by the spare units and an engine-maker service agreement. Liquidated damages capped at three months of fees.

## Equipment and delivery (the assumption the founder most wants tested)
Incumbent engines (Caterpillar, Jenbacher, Wartsila) are sold into 2028. The founder proposes to source from makers with open capacity: Korean and Japanese medium-speed gas engine makers that serve marine and island power markets, second-tier European makers, refurbished units, and fuel cells where gas quality allows. Claimed delivery: 12-15 months from deposit. Claimed installed cost: $1.45M per MW including the battery.

## Economics as the founder sees them
- Revenue per block: 25 MW x $38,000 x 12 = $11.4M a year.
- Cash margin $250,000-375,000 per MW-year (ProPetro's former disclosure), so $6.3-9.4M per block on $36M of cost. Payback four to six years.
- Financing: 55% debt at financial close, 45% equity from an infrastructure fund that buys blocks at financial close. The company keeps a 10-20% carried interest plus development and operating fees. The company's own $5-10M pays for engineering, permits, gas interconnect studies and equipment deposits on the first two blocks.

## Why a customer buys today
Operators with GPUs waiting value a month at $350,000-830,000 per MW (round-one demand panel). The large behind-the-meter providers chase gigawatt contracts with investment-grade buyers and 15-year terms. Sites of 25-150 MW with weaker credit are underserved.

## Growth plan
Fleet of standard blocks, then fleet-level financing, then permanent plants, then a private utility for compute.

## Known weak points the founder wants tested
- Is non-incumbent equipment really available in 12-15 months, and will buyers and lenders accept it?
- Air permits and gas interconnects at 25-150 MW: how long, and who has already locked the good sites?
- Crews: who operates these plants?
- With six months of security and redeployable assets, will any lender or fund finance blocks leased to B-rated tenants?
- What stops the oilfield-service providers, who have 2.6 GW of secured equipment and unused capacity, from serving these sites at a lower price?
- Does this ever become more than a small leasing company?
