Method and labels. This is an adversarial second pass on candidates C9a, C9b, C12 and C13. I read the first-round notes (housing_buildings.md, consumer_goods.md, aviation_space.md, skeptic_red_team.md, historical_analogs_framework.md) and then ran fresh checks on the load-bearing or weak figures. Web search worked in this session, but several primary pages returned 403 (Pew, Stateline), so some items are "via search summary" and say so. Tags used throughout: **[M]** measured or reported fact, **[C]** company or vendor claim, **[E]** my own estimate or inference, **[U]** unverified recollection that I could not source this session and that should not be quoted without a check. Headline result. None of the four is a Model T. ## C9a. Direct-to-homeowner factory-built backyard homes (ADUs) in California and copycat states

### Takeaway
The demand is real and legal (about 30,000 California permits in 2024 and about 61,700 nationally in 2025), but the factory is not the bottleneck: prefab ADU companies charge about $230k-$400k installed against a 2021 statewide median of $150k, the box is under half of the installed price, and every venture-backed player is still small (roughly 100-300 units a year) after $25M-$75M+ of funding. A founder should treat this as a site-work, permitting, sales and financing business that buys boxes, and build no factory until an installed price under about $200k is proven on real lots.

### Inferences

**1. Thesis and assumptions.** Thesis: California legalized backyard homes and permits rose from about 1,000 to 30,000 a year, so a producer that delivers a finished unit far cheaper and faster, sold direct to homeowners with equity, unlocks a multiple of today's volume in California and the 21 other states with ADU laws. The legal gate is open, the site scope is small and there is no builder or union in the loop.

Market-share check (my calc, [E]): Samara's $100M of project value at $250k-$300k each is about 330-400 projects in roughly three years, or 110-135 a year, about 0.4% of California's 30,231 permits. Villa's 500+ over about 3.5 years is about 150 a year, 0.5%. All venture-backed prefab ADU firms together are probably under 3% of California ADU permits. The other 97% are garage conversions and site-built units by small contractors. That is revealed preference against the premium prefab product at current prices.

**2. Actor simulation, 2027-2032.**
- (a) Consumers. Three segments. Cash-rich owners in expensive coastal zip codes (the 53% who paid cash) buy on design and convenience and are price-insensitive; they are who Samara, Abodu and Cover serve now, and the segment is thin. Return-seeking owners need the numbers to work: at $300k financed on a 7.49% HELOC the interest alone is $22.5k a year against $24k of median rent (2021 rent, my calc), so they do not buy; at $150k the interest is $11.2k and they do. Family-use buyers (seniors, adult children, 31% of use) buy on need and will stretch. Most likely response to a new entrant priced at $250k+: the same few hundred units a year as existing players. At $180k all-in the return-seeking segment opens.
- (b) Business buyers. Multifamily owners using SB 1211 (up to eight detached ADUs per lot) buy in tens per deal (Prometheus: 50+). They negotiate hard and will dual-source. This is where volume is in 2027-2029, and it is a B2B sale to a few dozen owners, not direct-to-homeowner.
- (c) Incumbents. Local general contractors price to the market and keep the conversion and custom share. Champion, Cavco and Clayton already build ADU-sized HUD-code and modular units and sell them through retailers such as Villa; with backlogs of only 5-6 weeks (March 2026) they have idle capacity and will supply any retailer who brings an order. They will not go direct in backyards, because site work is not their business, but they cap what an entrant can charge for a box. Expect box prices to stay near HUD-code levels ($87 per sq ft, 2023) for anyone willing to accept HUD-code aesthetics.
- (d) Regulators. California keeps tightening state preemption and HCD keeps writing letters; cities respond with utility connection requirements, fire and coastal rules, and unit caps. Other states pass laws, but permit response is about a third of California's rate. HUD chassis-free rules (see C9b) could make a HUD-code ADU look like a normal house from about 2028-2029.
- (e) Labor and local politics. No union in single-family backyards. Neighbors object to multi-ADU projects (a San Diego group is suing over a 136-unit project). Inspectors re-inspect factory work in some jurisdictions (Terner 2026).
- (f) Financiers. Home-equity rates near 7.5% hold demand down through 2027. Appraisals undercount ADU value. Venture money is available in $20-40M rounds (Samara 2025, Villa 2025, Reframe's reported $40M in 2026) but not at Katerra scale, and Boxabl's public numbers will make later rounds harder.
- (g) Insurers and liability. Deposit and warranty risk sits with the seller; one failed installer (Mighty Buildings, 2025) makes buyers and lenders wary of prepaying a startup.
- (h) Installers. Foundation, trenching, utility connection and crane crews are the real constraint and the real cost. A firm that standardizes them (screw piles, one-day set, fixed-price utility runs) gains more than one that automates the factory.

| Year | Most likely course for a new entrant | Effect on price, volume, timing |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | Rates still about 7%; California permits flat at 30-33k; entrant sells 20-60 units using bought boxes | Installed price $230-300k; no price advantage yet |
| 2028 | Multifamily ADU deals grow; two or three more states pass laws; one existing prefab ADU firm fails or is bought | Volume 100-200; price pressure from HUD-code retailers |
| 2029-2030 | Chassis-free HUD-code units reach the market; incumbents supply them to any retailer | Box price falls for everyone; advantage shifts wholly to site and sales process |
| 2031-2032 | National market 70-90k permits a year if rates ease [E]; three to five regional installers consolidate | Entrant holds 0.5-3% share; margin depends on site-cost control |

**3. Market arithmetic.**
- Size today: California 30,231 permits (2024) at $150k-$200k is $4.5-6.0B a year; national 61,655 permits (2025) at an assumed $175k average is about $10.8B [E; the average price is my assumption].
- Share the factory can touch: about 45% of installed price (box, plans, shipping). Factory methods have delivered 0-20% savings on that portion (first-round finding), so 0-9% of installed price. Even a 50% cheaper box cuts the installed price only about 22% (my calc: 0.5 x 45%).
- Elasticity: general housing demand is below 1. ADUs are a discretionary investment with a yield threshold, so I assume -1.0 to -1.5 on installed price; this is an assumption with no measured source.
- At 30% lower installed price: units x1.43 (at -1.0) to x1.71 (at -1.5). California 30k becomes 43-51k; national 62k becomes 88-105k. Dollar market flat to +20%.
- At 50% lower installed price: units x2.0 to x2.8. California 60-85k; national 123-174k. Dollar market flat to +41%.
- A 30% cut requires the box to be about 50% cheaper and site and soft costs about 15% cheaper together; a 50% cut cannot come from the factory at all without a matching halving of site, permit and sales cost.

**4. Bear, base, bull to 2032 (a new entrant starting in 2027).**
- Bear: peak revenue $5-15M a year, $40-60M consumed, wound down or sold for parts by 2030. Reason: installed price no lower than a local contractor, high acquisition cost, rates stay high.
- Base: $60-100M revenue in 2032 (250-400 units at about $250k), $80-120M consumed, 15-25% gross margin, near break-even. Reason: matches the Samara and Villa trajectories with better site process.
- Bull: $300-500M revenue (1,500-2,500 units at about $200k, 2-3% of a 70-90k national market), $200-300M consumed. Reason: installed price truly under $200k, a financing product that counts rent, multifamily owners as anchor buyers, expansion to Washington, Oregon, Arizona and Colorado.

**5. Kill criteria and cheapest 90-day test.**
- Kill if, after the first 50 units: median all-in price is above $220k; site, utility and permit cost is above 45% of contract value or varies by more than plus or minus 40% lot to lot; acquisition cost is above $12k per signed contract; gross margin is under 15%; contract-to-keys is over six months.
- 90-day test (about $150-300k, no factory): in two California cities with pre-approved plan programs, offer a fixed all-in price of $179k for a 500 sq ft unit using a box bought from an existing HUD-code or modular plant. Take $5k non-refundable deposits. In parallel, get firm site-work and utility quotes on 30 real lots from the deposit-payers. Pass marks: at least 10 deposits, at least 60% of lots quotable under $50k of site work, and at least half of deposit-payers pre-qualifying for financing.

**6. Verdict.**

Judgement: the first round ranked this first in housing, and the demand and legal evidence deserve that. The production thesis does not. Existing prefab ADU firms are the expensive option, not the cheap one, and none has shown scale. The buildable business here is an asset-light installer and seller that standardizes site work and financing and buys boxes from factories with idle capacity. That can be a $60-100M regional company with thin margins and little moat. It becomes interesting only if the 90-day test shows an installed price under $200k is repeatable.

### Gaps
- No company discloses units per year, gross margin or acquisition cost. The share estimates above are my arithmetic on press-release figures.
- California's 2025 ADU permit total and completion (not permit) counts were not retrieved; the "growth is slowing" reading rests on 2022-2024 only.
- No measured price elasticity for ADUs exists in anything I found; the -1.0 to -1.5 range is an assumption.
- Samara's current installed price, factory location and capacity are not published ([U]: I recall its plant is in Mexico, which would add tariff exposure; not verified).
- The cost breakdowns come from contractor marketing pages, not audited data.

## C9b. Chassis-less, multi-story HUD-code infill home timed to July 2027; and volumetric bathroom, kitchen and mechanical pods sold to builders

### Takeaway
The timing premise is wrong and the "slow incumbents" premise is wrong. July 2027 is a state paperwork deadline; the HUD construction standard for chassis-free homes had not even been proposed as of October 2026, and Champion's CFO expects "a year-plus". Cavco has already unveiled a chassis-free model, Clayton has shown two-story chassis-free concepts, and both Cavco and Champion sell HUD-code duplexes. A startup factory cannot beat plants that run at 22-26% gross margin with idle capacity and captive lenders; the only defensible role is developer and retailer of infill product built in incumbents' plants. Pods are a real but tiny, cost-neutral, project-sales business.

### Cited Findings

**Status of the law and the HUD standards (late 2026)**
- [M] The ROAD to Housing Act became law on 11 July 2026; Section 301 removes the permanent chassis requirement and directs HUD, with the Manufactured Housing Consensus Committee (MHCC), to set revised standards — [HUD, Next Generation Manufactured Housing Action Plan, Oct 2026](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/Main/documents/Next-Generation-Manufactured-Housing-Action-Plan.pdf) (PDF read in full)
- [M] HUD's plan lists seven actions, including updating 24 CFR 3280 for chassis-free construction and 24 CFR 3285 for foundations and installation. On both, the wording is that HUD "will review the MHCC's future recommendations for proposed rulemaking". No proposed rule, date or deadline is given. The MHCC met on 23 July 2026 and reaffirmed energy recommendations — [HUD action plan](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/Main/documents/Next-Generation-Manufactured-Housing-Action-Plan.pdf)
- [M] The same plan proposes a "Manufacturer On-Ramp" to help existing modular and off-site makers enter the HUD system, a multi-module pathway, and a federal performance code for buildings of five or more units — [HUD action plan](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/Main/documents/Next-Generation-Manufactured-Housing-Action-Plan.pdf)
- [M] Before the Act, HUD proposed (12 June 2026) to drop the chassis only for upper floors of multistory homes while keeping it for the lowest floor; comments closed 11 Aug 2026; HUD put chassis cost in multistory homes at $4,800-$6,700 per unit — [Orrick, June 2026](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2026-06-18/hud-proposes-changes-to-manufactured-housing-definition-to-promote-multistory-construction/); [HUD release 26-049](https://hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-049) (both via search summary)
- [M] States must certify by 11 July 2027 (2028 where legislatures meet every two years) that they treat homes with and without a chassis alike. Pew's researcher estimates about 30 states need changes and that it "may not be as easy as striking a few words". North Carolina, Tennessee and Texas already track HUD's definition automatically — [Stateline, 20 Oct 2026](https://stateline.org/2026/10/20/cheaper-manufactured-homes-could-be-having-a-moment-if-only-states-can-catch-up/) (via search summary; page returned 403)
- [M] HUD's September 2024 rule already allows up to four units per structure and multi-story designs — [Niskanen Center](https://www.niskanencenter.org/two-big-developments-in-manufactured-housing-reform-new-rules-new-bill/) (first-round note)
- [M] Zoning stays local; the Act did not preempt it — [Bay News 9, Sept 2026](https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/news/2026/09/12/new-federal-housing-law-could-reshape-manufactured-homes-in-florida) (first-round note)

**What the incumbents have already done and said**

- [M] Cavco unveiled "Grande Arbor", its first chassis-free manufactured home, on the National Mall, pitched at infill sites; it cannot be sold at volume until HUD finalizes rules — [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/cavco-chassis-free-manufactured-home-model/) (via search summary)
- [M] Clayton published two-story CrossMod concepts without a chassis between floors, marked "not available for sale at this time" — [Clayton, 14 July 2026](https://www.claytonhomes.com/newsroom/press-releases/clayton-commends-enactment-of-landmark-housing-legislation-modernizing-manufactured-housing-standards-with-chassis-optionality) (via search summary)

### Inferences

**1. Thesis and assumptions.** Thesis: the HUD code is the only US building regime with national preemption and a proven 35-50% cost advantage, and removing the chassis lets that product become a normal-looking two-story or duplex infill home; a new producer that designs for this from scratch and is ready when states conform in July 2027 takes the urban market incumbents have never served. Separately, factory-made bathroom, kitchen and mechanical pods sold to builders capture the densest, most trade-intensive part of a building without the whole-building risk.

| # | Assumption | For | Against | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | HUD standards for chassis-free homes are usable by mid-2027 | Statute passed July 2026; HUD plan published | No proposed rule for Part 3280 or 3285 as of Oct 2026; MHCC must recommend first; Champion: "a year-plus"; Cavco cannot sell at volume yet | **Fails** for 2027; realistic 2028-2029 [E] |
| B2 | States conform by July 2027 and title the homes as real property | Three states auto-align | About 30 states need statute changes; biennial legislatures have until 2028 | **Shaky** |
| B3 | The product is much cheaper than site-built infill | Double-section 60-64% of site-built | CrossMod 73-80%; chassis saving $4,800-$10,000 offset by structure and cranes per Cavco; infill adds land, site and utility cost | **Shaky** (10-25% cheaper, not 50%) |
| B4 | Cities will let it be placed on infill lots | HUD code preempts building codes; some state zoning reform | Zoning and siting stay local; bias against "mobile homes" persists | **Shaky** |
| B5 | Incumbents are slow and leave a window | They framed it as "over time" | Cavco chassis-free model shown; Clayton two-story concept; Cavco and Champion duplexes on sale; 5-6 week backlogs mean spare capacity; HUD is building an on-ramp for modular makers too | **Fails** |

| B8 | Builders buy pods at volume if they are cheaper | Hotels and student housing do buy | Leader: about 20,000 pods in roughly 20 years; value is schedule, not price; decision must be made at design stage | **Shaky** |

**2. Actor simulation, 2027-2032.**
- (a) Consumers. Revealed behavior is the key fact: a product at about half the cost per square foot holds 9-10% of new single-family supply. Buyers who can qualify for a mortgage choose site-built for resale value, lender acceptance and status. A two-story chassis-free home that appraises and finances like a house removes two of those three objections; it does not remove the third until there are resale comparables, which takes years. Expect early buyers to be first-time buyers in the Sun Belt and Mountain West and small infill investors.
- (b) Business buyers. Build-to-rent operators, community owners and small infill developers are the natural volume buyers and they already buy from Clayton, Champion and Cavco. Tract builders did not commit volume to Katerra, Veev or Entekra and will not to a startup; Clayton has owned site builders since 2015 and can sell to itself.
- (c) Incumbents. Most likely response: add chassis-free and two-story models as configurations in existing plants in 2028-2029, price them as a premium "innovation" line (Cavco says explicitly that this is not a cost play), finance through captive lenders, and distribute through 350+ Clayton outlets and independent retailers. If an entrant gains traction, they match the product within a model year and undercut on financing. They lobby through MHI, which already says "the industry is ready".
- (d) Regulators. HUD: MHCC recommendations in 2027, proposed rule 2027-2028, final rule 2028-2029 [E, based on "a year-plus" and no NPRM yet]. States: a first wave conforms in 2027, the rest in 2028. Cities: zoning admission is place by place; expect design standards (roof pitch, foundation, facade) used to exclude.
- (e) Labor and local politics. Little union exposure in single-family infill; neighbors object to anything labeled manufactured. Crane sets in tight infill lots need street closures and local permits.
- (f) Financiers. The largest lever is financing parity, not factory cost: on $130,000 over 23 years at 8.6% the payment is about $1,082 a month; over 30 years at 4.9% it is about $690, 36% lower (my calc on 2019 rates). The incumbents own the lending. Venture investors remember Katerra ($2B+), Veev ($600M), Entekra and Blu; a factory-first pitch will struggle to raise beyond a seed.
- (g) Insurers and surety. Lenders and insurers ask who pays if the factory fails mid-project (Terner 2026); a startup manufacturer is exactly that risk.
- (h) Installers. Chassis-free means crane sets, engineered foundations and two-story stitching on site. Set crews are scarce and the cost moves from factory to site, as Cavco says.

| Year | Most likely course | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | States amend statutes; HUD and MHCC draft; incumbents show prototypes; nothing chassis-free sold at volume | Entrant with a factory burns cash with no legal product |
| 2028 | Proposed or final HUD standard; first labeled chassis-free homes from Cavco, Clayton, Champion | Incumbents first to market |
| 2029-2030 | Real-property titling and conventional mortgages available in 20-30 states; early infill projects | 5,000-15,000 chassis-free homes a year industry-wide [E] |
| 2031-2032 | Resale comparables appear; zoning fights case by case | 20,000-50,000 a year [E], 80-90% from incumbents |

**3. Market arithmetic.**
- Size today: 102,737 homes (2025) at about $131,500 average (Oct 2025) is about $13.5B at retail (my calc). Single-family starts were about 940,000 (2025).
- Share the factory can touch: the home is 100% factory-built but on an infill lot it is roughly 40-60% of non-land cost and under 35% of delivered price including metro land [E]. The chassis itself is 4-8% of the home price ($5,000-$10,000 on $131,500) and Cavco says that saving is offset.
- Elasticity: housing demand below 1; I use -0.5. The product is already 20-40% below site-built and holds 10%, so price is not the variable that moves share.
- At 30% lower delivered price than today's HUD-code product: units +20% at -0.5 (0.7^-0.5 = 1.195), about 123,000 homes; dollars fall 16%. At 50% lower: units +41%, about 145,000 homes; dollars fall 29%. Neither is reachable by a factory, since the box is already near the materials floor.
- The real expansion path is access: if chassis-free homes reach 2-5% of single-family and small multifamily starts in conforming states by 2032, that is 20,000-50,000 extra homes a year, a 20-50% lift for the industry ($3-7B), with incumbents taking most of it [E].
- Pods: about 416,000 multifamily starts (2025) at roughly 1.5 bathrooms is about 620,000 bathrooms a year. SurePods' 20,000 pods over roughly 20 years is on the order of 1,000 a year, well under 1% of that. A bathroom is perhaps 3-5% of unit hard cost [U], so a pod 30% cheaper saves a developer about 1-1.5% and does not change behavior; adoption responds to schedule certainty. My assumption: 30% cheaper pods move share from under 1% to 3-5% (19,000-31,000 pods, roughly $200-400M at an assumed $12,000 each); 50% cheaper to 5-10%.

**4. Bear, base, bull to 2032.**
- HUD-code manufacturer startup. Bear: under 100 homes, $30-60M consumed, closed by 2030 because the standard slips to 2029 and incumbents launch first. Base: 200-500 homes a year from one plant, $30-70M revenue, $60-100M consumed, sub-scale margin. Bull: 2,000 homes a year, $300M revenue, $200M+ consumed; requires an owned pipeline and a lender partner.
- Developer-retailer buying incumbents' boxes (the version I think is buildable). Bear: 20-50 homes a year, $20-30M consumed, zoning and appraisal stall. Base: 150-400 homes a year at $200-300k including land, $40-100M revenue, $30-60M equity plus project debt, low-teens gross margin. Bull: 1,500-3,000 homes a year, $400-700M revenue, $150-300M equity plus land debt.
- Pods. Bear: two or three projects, $10-20M consumed. Base: $15-40M revenue, lumpy, 15-25% gross margin. Bull: $100-150M revenue with two or three national hotel or student-housing programs.

**5. Kill criteria and cheapest 90-day test.**
- Kill the manufacturer version now unless a named incumbent refuses to build your design under contract. Kill the developer version if: no HUD proposed rule for Part 3280 by end-2027; your target state has not conformed by its deadline; two of three target cities will not confirm by-right placement in writing; no lender will quote a 30-year mortgage on the product.
- 90-day test (about $50-100k): pick three metros in already-aligned states (North Carolina, Tennessee, Texas). Obtain (i) a written zoning determination for a two-story HUD-code home or duplex on three specific infill lots, (ii) a term sheet from one mortgage lender and one appraiser's opinion of value, (iii) a contract-build quote from two incumbent plants for your design, and (iv) five signed purchase reservations with $2,500 deposits at the resulting price.
- Pods: before any plant, obtain one design-stage commitment from a hotel or student-housing developer for 200+ identical pods with a deposit. Kill if three developers decline at a 10% discount to their site-built bathroom cost.

Judgement: the first round described incumbents as "slow and innovation-shy" and the window as 2027-2028. Both readings fail on fresh evidence. The law is real and may add tens of thousands of homes a year by the early 2030s, but that volume will be made in existing plants and financed by existing captive lenders. A founder can participate as the party that owns land, entitlement, design and the buyer, using the incumbents as contract factories, which is what Villa is already doing. Pods are a sound small business with no expansion story.

**1. In the air, MOSAIC removed the type certificate for four-seat aircraft in July 2026, so a producer who builds at automotive-style cost can sell a $100-150k personal aircraft into a market that once bought 17,800 a year.

**2. Actor simulation, 2027-2032.**
- (a) Consumers. Vehicle: three segments. EV enthusiasts and second-vehicle households convert first (a few tens of thousands). Payment-constrained buyers compare a $26-31k two-seat EV with a three-year-old used compact SUV or a $22k Trax and mostly choose those. Small fleets (parks, campuses, trades) buy on total cost and are the steadier pool. Applying 5-16% conversion to 180,000 reservations gives 9,000-29,000 sales (my calc), against a claimed break-even of 80,000 a year. Aircraft: buyers are existing pilots, median age high, who can buy a used 172 or SR22 for $200-260k; a new $150k four-seat would sell to them and to flight schools, but the pilot population, not price, caps volume.
- (b) Business buyers. Fleet "orders" for new vehicle makers have not converted (Canoo, Arrival). Flight schools are real buyers of two-seat trainers and are price-sensitive; they are the best aircraft segment and are already served by Van's, Sling, Tecnam and Textron's Pipistrel.
- (c) Incumbents. Vehicle: Ford, GM and Toyota respond with one-off cheap models when it suits them (the under-$30k Bolt runs January 2026 to mid-2027) and with incentives (6.8% of price, Oct 2026); captive finance arms can subsidize rates, which a startup cannot. They will not start a price war in a segment with thin margins; they wait for the entrant to run out of cash. BYD stays outside the wall but sets the price that policy change would bring. Aircraft: Textron and Cirrus move upmarket and leave the low end; the low end is contested by a dozen small light-sport makers with 20 years of experience and existing dealer and maintenance networks.
- (d) Regulators. NHTSA: no microcar class before 2029 at the earliest [E]. Direct sales face state franchise laws (not researched this session). FAA: MOSAIC works as written; the consensus-standard route is open to everyone, so it is no moat. CPSC not relevant.
- (e) Labor and politics. UAW and state governments favor a US plant; this helps with incentives, not demand.
- (f) Financiers. Vehicle: equity only after Slate's first-year numbers; debt programs for auto plants were cut in 2025. Aircraft: fundable at $20-60M as a niche; lenders finance aircraft purchases at higher rates and shorter terms than cars.
- (g) Insurers. Vehicle: a recall on early units is existential for a new brand (all 209 Nikola trucks were recalled). Aircraft: product liability is the least-known large cost; a high-volume four-seat flown by low-time sport pilots is the worst risk profile an underwriter could be shown.
- (h) Service. Vehicle: no dealer network means mobile service and third-party shops; parts and service are 48% of dealer gross profit, which the entrant gives up. Aircraft: every airframe needs annual inspections by scarce mechanics.

**3. Market arithmetic.**
- Vehicle size today: about 16M new light vehicles a year [U, not fetched] at $51,109 (Oct 2026) is roughly $800B. Market-level elasticity -0.34 to -0.5.
- At 30% lower prices market-wide: units +13% to +20% (0.7^-0.34 = 1.13; 0.7^-0.5 = 1.20), about 18-19M; dollars fall 16-21%. At 50% lower: units +27% to +41%, about 20-23M; dollars fall 29-37%. A cheaper car shrinks the dollar market.
- For one entrant the relevant number is share: 0.3-1.0% of the market is 50,000-160,000 units, $1.3-4.5B at $26-28k. That is share taken from used cars and cheap crossovers, not new demand.
- Share the factory can touch: maker cost is about 85-95% of price (dealer about 5%), but purchased materials and components dominate; assembly labor and plant overhead are perhaps 15-25% of cost [U]. BYD's edge is supplier integration.
- Aircraft size today: 1,782 piston airplanes worldwide (2025); at an assumed $500k average that is about $0.9B [E]. The first-round estimate of 10,000-30,000 a year at $100k implies, from a $450k base, a volume multiple of 6-17 for a 4.5x price cut: elasticity of 1.2-1.9 (my calc: ln 6 / ln 4.5 = 1.19; ln 17 / ln 4.5 = 1.88). That is above anything measured for durable goods at market level.
- With elasticity -1.0: at 30% lower price (about $315k) units are x1.43, about 2,550; at 50% lower (about $225k) x2.0, about 3,560. With -0.5: about 2,130 and 2,520. At a true $100k and -1.0, about 8,000 a year ($0.8B). The real MOSAIC price points ($250-500k) therefore imply today's market plus 0-40%.
- Share the factory can touch in an aircraft: engine, propeller and avionics are bought in and I estimate them at $80-110k for a four-seat [U]; that alone nearly exhausts a $100-150k price. Airframe labor is where automation helps, and the RV-12iS at $246k with two seats shows how little room remains.

**4. Bear, base, bull to 2032.**
- Slate-style vehicle (new entrant, 2027 start). Bear: under $200M cumulative revenue, $1-2B consumed, bankrupt by 2030; reason: demand at the real transaction price is 20-30k a year and capital dries up. Base: 20-40k units a year, $0.6-1.1B revenue, $2.5-4B consumed, still loss-making; reason: niche demand, no service network. Bull: 100-150k a year, $3-4.5B revenue, $3B consumed, low-teens gross margin; reason: tariff wall holds, product broadens to four seats.

- MOSAIC aircraft. Bear: prototype and under 20 deliveries, $30-60M consumed; reason: bill of materials and insurance. Base: 50-150 aircraft a year at $250-300k, $15-45M revenue, $60-120M consumed. Bull: 800-1,500 a year at about $180k, $150-270M revenue, $200-300M consumed; requires a non-certified engine at a third of today's cost and a training and financing program.

**5. Kill criteria and cheapest 90-day test.**
- Vehicle: do not start before Slate's first twelve months are public. Kill if Slate's first-year US registrations are under 30,000, if fewer than 15% of $300 preorders become orders, or if its average transaction price exceeds $32,000. The cheapest test is observation: buy state registration data for Slate monthly through 2027 (a few thousand dollars) and interview 50 reservation holders who cancelled. If still interested, run a $500 non-refundable deposit campaign for a four-seat variant at a firm price in three metros with financing pre-approval; under 1,000 deposits in 90 days is a no.
- Aircraft (about $75-150k): (i) get supplier quotes for engine, propeller and avionics at 500 a year and build a bottom-up cost; (ii) get written product-liability quotes from two aviation underwriters for a four-seat special LSA at 100 and 1,000 units a year; (iii) take $5,000 non-refundable deposits at a firm $149k at one major fly-in and online. Kill if cost plus insurance exceeds $110k at 500 a year, or deposits are under 200.

The cheap-car gap is caused by trade policy, safety rules, product mix and thin margins, and the one company testing it has spent $1.4B+ without a confirmed delivery. Wait for its 2027 data. The microcar has no legal product. ## C13. Short-lead-time made-to-order kitchens and cabinetry, and installed home products (stairlifts, home elevators, saunas)

### Takeaway
The first round's evidence for this candidate was thin, and the fresh evidence is mostly negative. The US cabinet market is about $19-22B and shrinking in volume; the largest maker earns a 30% gross margin and about 1% net, so there is no fat manufacturer margin to strip; cabinets are 25-40% of a kitchen project and the factory cost is roughly 13% of it; and the two closest precedents failed in the last 19 months: Form Kitchens (direct-to-consumer) halted in April 2025 and Wren Kitchens US, a vertically integrated maker with its own 252,000 sq ft Pennsylvania plant, showrooms and a Home Depot partnership, liquidated in April 2026 on $19M of revenue. A small quick-ship plant selling to professional remodelers is buildable; a consumer kitchen brand is not supported.

**1. **2. Actor simulation, 2027-2032.**
- (a) Consumers. A kitchen is bought roughly once in 15-20 years, triggered by a home purchase or accumulated equity, and deferred when rates are high (as in 2025-2026). Buyers choose a designer or contractor they trust, then accept the cabinet line that person sells. Low-budget buyers take stock or IKEA today. So the consumer rarely chooses the cabinet maker and rarely pays for speed. After Wren and Form, consumers who read the news have a fresh reason not to prepay $25,000 to a new kitchen company. Stairlift buyers act within days of a fall or discharge and buy from whoever answers first; price sensitivity is low and service matters.
- (b) Business buyers. Remodelers, kitchen dealers, multifamily renovators and small builders are the real choosers. They value reliable dates, complete and correct orders, and fast replacement of damaged parts more than a headline lead time. They will try a quick-ship supplier for rush jobs and replacements first, then move share only after a year of clean deliveries. They resist any supplier that also sells direct to their customers.
- (c) Incumbents. MasterBrand-American Woodmark (about $4.1-4.2B annualized, roughly a fifth of the market by my calc) is cutting $100M+ of cost and carries $1.1B of net debt; it will defend dealer and home-center shelf space with quick-ship programs on its top SKUs and with price, and it can buy a regional entrant cheaply. Home Depot and Lowe's control the low and middle of the consumer funnel and have just been burned by the Wren partnership. IKEA holds the low end with stock on the shelf. Savaria, Stannah and Acorn hold dealer networks in accessibility.
- (d) Regulators. Little drag: formaldehyde and finish emissions rules, state contractor licensing if the entrant installs. Tariffs are the main policy variable and have already slipped once.
- (e) Labor. Installers are scarce and are the pacing item. Cabinet installers and finish carpenters are independent trades with no union obstacle.
- (f) Financiers. Home-equity rates near 7.5% (Nov 2026) hold remodel demand down. Venture investors have the furniture cohort and Form in mind; equipment lenders will finance CNC lines against the machines. A plant can be built for $10-20M [E].
- (g) Liability. Customer deposits are the hazard: the business takes 50%+ up front, and one cash squeeze strands paid orders (Interior Define, Form, Wren).
- (h) Installers again. Any time saved in the factory is lost if the installer, the countertop templater (who can only measure after cabinets are set) or the plumber is not available. Speed has to be sold as a package with installation to be worth anything.

| Year | Most likely course for a quick-ship B2B entrant | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | Remodel volume still soft; entrant wins rush and replacement orders from 30-60 pros in one metro | $3-8M revenue; no price premium; lead time wins the first order only |
| 2028 | Tariff either steps to 50% or is traded away; incumbents launch quick-ship lines | Margin 28-33% if plant is over 60% loaded |
| 2029-2030 | Rates ease, volumes recover [E]; entrant adds a second region or is acquired | $20-40M revenue |
| 2031-2032 | Three to five regional plants possible; software ordering becomes standard across the industry | Advantage narrows to service quality |

**3. Market arithmetic.**
- Size today: cabinets about $19-22B (2025, vendor estimates) within a $228B kitchen and bath category (2026). Stairlifts and home elevators: no US market size found; Savaria's global revenue of about $0.9B (2025, across all its lines) bounds it at low single-digit billions.
- Share the factory can touch (my calc, [E]): cabinets are about 29% of a remodel. Maker cost of goods is about 70% of maker revenue (30% gross margin), and maker revenue is roughly 60-70% of what the homeowner pays for cabinets after dealer margin [U for the dealer share]. So factory cost is about 45% of the cabinet price and about 13% of the kitchen (0.29 x 0.455). Halving factory cost cuts the kitchen price about 6.5%. Removing the dealer layer could cut another 6-10% of the project if its design and service work were free, which it is not.
- Elasticity: none measured for kitchens. I assume -0.3 to -1.0; the purchase is deferrable and driven by housing turnover and equity.
- At 30% lower delivered kitchen price: project volume +11% to +43% (0.7^-0.3 = 1.11; 0.7^-1 = 1.43); category dollars fall 0-22%. At 50% lower: volume +23% to +100%; dollars fall 0-38%. A 30% cut is not reachable from the cabinet factory alone: the most a zero-cost factory plus zero dealer margin could deliver is about 20-23% of the project.
- Lead-time effect: no data. My judgement is that cutting cabinet lead time from 8 weeks to 1 shortens the median whole project by less than the difference, because design decisions, permits, installer scheduling and countertop fabrication are also on the path.

**4. Bear, base, bull to 2032.**
- Consumer-direct kitchen brand. Bear (most likely): $5-20M peak revenue, $30-60M consumed, halted with customer deposits outstanding; reason: acquisition cost and installation, as with Form and Wren US. Base: $30-50M revenue, break-even at best. Bull: $150M+, requires a showroom network, i.e. Wren's plan with better execution.
- Quick-ship plant selling to professionals. Bear: $5-10M revenue, $15-25M consumed, sold for equipment value. Base: $30-60M revenue by 2032, $25-50M capital, 30-35% gross margin, 5-10% operating margin; reason: a well-run regional cabinet maker with better software. Bull: $300-500M revenue from 3-5 plants (about 2% of US cabinets), $150-250M capital; exit to a consolidator.
- Stairlifts, elevators, saunas. Bear: importer-installer with $5-10M revenue. Base: regional accessibility dealer-installer network, $30-80M revenue, good margins, little production content. Bull: shaftless home elevator maker with a national dealer network, $150-250M revenue; the constraint is dealers and code, not the factory.

**5. Kill criteria and cheapest 90-day test.**
- Kill the consumer-direct version now on precedent unless you own a showroom or contractor network already. Kill the professional version if: fewer than half of trial accounts reorder within six months; professionals will not move at least 20% of their cabinet spend without a price cut of more than 10%; gross margin is under 28% at 60% plant loading; customer deposits ever fund operating losses.
- 90-day test (under $100k, no plant): contract spare capacity at an existing CNC cabinet shop and white-label it. Offer 20 remodelers and multifamily renovators in one metro a 10-working-day delivery guarantee at market price with a late-delivery credit. Measure: orders placed, share of each account's volume, reorder rate, error rate, and whether any account will pay 5% more for the guarantee. Pass marks: 12 of 20 order, 8 reorder, at least 3 accept a premium.
- Stairlifts: mystery-shop 30 dealer quotes in three metros to measure the installed-price spread and response time; test a fixed-price, 48-hour-install offer through two hospital discharge planners.

Judgement: this candidate is the easiest of the four to start and the least like the founder's thesis. Nothing found supports the claim that production cost or production lead time is what holds the kitchen market back, and the two most relevant recent attempts ended in a halt and a liquidation. What survives is an ordinary, useful regional manufacturing business that sells reliability to professionals, with a ceiling in the tens of millions of revenue per plant and no latent-demand unlock. ## Cross-candidate comparison and what would change the ranking

### Takeaway
Ranked for a founder on evidence, not on market size: (1) asset-light ADU installer-seller, conditional on the 90-day price test; (2) quick-ship cabinets for professionals as a small, low-risk business; (3) HUD-code infill as developer-retailer using incumbents' plants, as an option on 2028-2029 rules; (4) MOSAIC aircraft as a niche; (5) a Slate-style vehicle, which should wait for Slate's 2027 data; (6) microcar, consumer-direct kitchens and a startup HUD-code factory, which should be dropped. Pods and an accessibility dealer-installer network are sound small businesses outside the thesis. In none of them is the factory the constraint, so none fits the "abundant production unlocks latent demand" thesis without a parallel change in site work, financing, zoning, trade policy or pilot supply.
