## 1. Is the casting/forging shortage a durable structural gap or a cycle that incumbents close by 2029-2030? Capex plans and reasons suppliers hold back

### Takeaway
For the hardest parts (single-crystal turbine airfoils, premium vacuum-melted nickel alloy, isothermal forgings) the shortage looks contracted through 2031-2032, not a cycle that closes by 2029. For simpler castings and machined parts it is more likely a queue that eases by 2029-2030. Incumbents are expanding only against signed customer contracts and are returning far more cash than they invest, while the engine makers themselves are buying or building foundries, which shrinks the open market a startup can sell into.

### Inferences
- [I] Reinvestment arithmetic for Howmet, 2026: capex about $0.5B against $2.0B of free cash flow after capex, so roughly 20% of operating cash ($0.5B of about $2.5B) goes into capacity and capex is about 4.9% of revenue ($0.5B / $10.3B). With a new $5B buyback authorization [S], the leading supplier is choosing price and returns over volume. This matches the "value over volume" behaviour of turbine OEMs in the energy note.
- [I] Timing of supply additions already announced: ATI's fifth VIM by mid-2028; Carpenter's Athens melt shop from mid-2027 (fiscal 2028); Howmet's Wichita Falls expansion starting construction in 2027; GE-CPP closing in H2 2027; SpaceX foundry roughly 2029-2030 on Jefferies' view. Most of this lands in 2028-2030. Much of it is pre-sold (ATI 80% contracted; Howmet customer-backed).
- [I] Verdict on durability. Structural through at least 2030 for hot-section airfoils and premium melt, because the sold-out claims run to 2031-2032, alloy supply is contracted, and the binding inputs (furnaces, experienced engineers) take two to ten years. Cyclical for simpler structural castings, sand castings and machined parts, where the "shortage" is mostly queue and labour and where Hadrian-type capacity and incumbent hiring arrive by 2028-2029.
- [I] The uncomfortable implication for a startup: the part of the shortage that lasts is the part it cannot enter quickly, and the part it can enter quickly is the part that closes first.
- [I] OEM vertical integration cuts both ways. It removes GE (and SpaceX) volume from the merchant market. It also leaves Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce, MHI, Siemens Energy, Kratos, Williams and every rocket company more exposed to a Howmet-PCC duopoly plus a GE-owned CPP, and so more willing to sponsor a new independent source.

## 2. How long does qualification of a new casting or forging source take, by segment, and which segment gives revenue in 18-24 months?

### Takeaway
I found no public, sourced figure for the time to qualify a new casting source on a flight engine. The dated evidence that does exist (a Navy solicitation's 180-day and 270-day approval steps, Jefferies' four-year view on SpaceX, "two years to install" at Howmet, entrants' contract dates) supports this ordering: rockets and expendable small engines in months, Navy and depot replacement parts in about a year, land-turbine non-rotating parts in one to three years, flight-engine hot-section parts in three to five years or more. Only the first two fit an 18-24 month revenue window.

### Cited Findings
- [S] A Navy forging-related solicitation lists 180 days after receipt for process-control-plan review and approval and 270 days for the first-article test report (contract-specific, not a general rule) — [SAM.gov notice (one of several returned; exact notice not confirmed)](https://sam.gov/opp/5b9aa38ed76b45f2b5062b10a3586d5c/view)
- [S] HII Newport News requires a formal request and evaluation before any forging supplier outside its approved list can be used; no timeline given — [HII approved forging suppliers, rev. 2025](https://hii.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/NNS_Approved_Forging_Suppliers.pdf)
- [S] In Aug 2026 the Navy said it was removing barriers that made first-article testing slow for additively manufactured substitutes for submarine parts (page blocked; details not read) — [Navy press release](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4580193/navy-removes-bureaucratic-barriers-to-accelerate-3d-printing-and-accelerate-sub/)
- [E] Shortage of experienced engineers at OEMs, Tier 1s and casters "slows down both the new-part qualification and ongoing production"; training production staff takes three to six months — [BCG, 2024](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/fixing-aerospaces-supply-chain-for-casting-and-forging)
- [E] Jefferies: SpaceX's turbine blade and vane products "could still be about four years away" even with SpaceX's capital and urgency — [Stocktwits](https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/markets/equity/space-x-is-coming-for-a-10-b-market-says-jefferies-but-how-soon-can-it-deliver/cZYzGyARJsq)

### Inferences
Estimated time from first purchase order to repeat production revenue, by segment. All ranges are [I], built from the dated cases above; none is a published standard.

| Segment | Who qualifies | Estimated time | Basis | Fits 18-24 months? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rocket engine castings (turbopump housings, manifolds) | The launch company itself, by hot-fire test | 3-12 months | Buyer owns design and test; Hadrian's first customers in 2022 were SpaceX, Rocket Lab and Astra ([Contrary](https://research.contrary.com/company/hadrian)) | Yes |
| Expendable small turbine engines for cruise missiles and drones | Engine maker plus program office | 6-18 months | Kratos funded development with Rangeview in Jul 2026; short engine life lowers the evidence burden | Yes, small volumes |
| Navy and depot replacement castings and forgings | NAVSEA/DLA source approval, first article | 9-18 months | 180-day plus 270-day steps in a Navy solicitation [S]; Navy contracted Rangeview for MRO parts | Yes, lumpy |
| Land gas-turbine structural parts, vane segments, shrouds | OEM supplier quality; no FAA | 12-36 months | OEMs are short and building their own foundries; no FAA gate | Partly |
| Land gas-turbine single-crystal blades | OEM, with long rig and field validation | 3-5 years | Jefferies' four years for SpaceX; "two years to install" capacity at Howmet [S] | No |
| Flight-engine structural castings | OEM under its FAA production approval | 2-4 years | BCG on engineering shortage; five- to seven-year contracts | No |
| Flight-engine hot section and rotating parts, or PMA | OEM or FAA PMA with test and analysis | 3-5+ years | GTF powder-metal precedent; duopoly "locked-in" | No |

- [I] Paper certifications are not the long pole. AS9100 and Nadcap special-process accreditation are typically achievable in roughly 6-12 months each [U]; ITAR registration and CMMC Level 2 add months and cost, not years [U]. The long pole is the customer's own part-specific qualification and the willingness of a supplier-quality organisation to spend scarce engineers on a new source.
- [I] A startup can earn revenue inside 18-24 months only in space, expendable defense engines and Navy/depot replacement parts. Those are real but small and lumpy. The large markets (flight engines, land-turbine airfoils) are a second act that begins around year three to five.

- No sourced timeline or cost for a PMA turbine blade programme.
- AS9100, Nadcap, CMMC and ITAR durations above are recollection, not sourced.

## 5. C5: is the constraint propellant chemistry and qualification rather than production, and how concentrated is the buyer?

### Takeaway
Yes. Every source that looks under the motor names ammonium perchlorate (one US producer), binder, cases, nozzles and requalification as the limits, and incumbents say outright that a third motor maker is not needed. The buyer is one government acting through two or three primes; the government's own plan cuts the ten-programme munitions line 46% after FY2027, and Congress is already funding FY2027 at a fraction of the request outside the multiyear legacy lines.

### Cited Findings

**What actually constrains motors**
- [E] Ammonium perchlorate (AP) is 68-73% of composite propellant; the only US producer as of May 2026 is AMPAC at Cedar City, Utah; a new AP plant needs over a year for approval and around a decade to build; Northrop's second-source AP effort ran from a 2017 Army request to lab qualification in 2021 and was still scaling in April 2026; grain curing takes days; "every motor for every program requires years" of static fires, environmental and lot-acceptance testing. ## 6. Candidate C1: fast long-lead metal parts

### Takeaway
C1 has the best-evidenced, longest shortage and a clear price umbrella (39% segment margins at the leader, an $11.75B purchase of a foundry group by an engine maker), but its first 24 months of revenue can only come from small, self-qualifying buyers. It is a five-year, $300-500M build with an attractive strategic exit, not a quick wedge.

### Cited Findings

**Thesis.** Engine makers, turbine makers, space companies and defense primes wait 6-18+ months for castings, forgings and precision parts because a handful of incumbents ration capacity. A producer using digital tooling and automation that delivers in weeks can charge for speed, starting with buyers who qualify suppliers themselves and moving up to land turbines and flight engines.

## 7. Candidate C4: China-free motion and power components

### Takeaway
C4 is the cheapest and fastest to start, and the legal protection on the defense side is real and bipartisan. But the protected market is in the low hundreds of millions of dollars a year, capacity for the simplest part (small motors) is already being installed at programme scale, the law lets allied suppliers in, and the truly scarce inputs (magnets, cells) sit upstream. First-round ranking of C4 as the top defense wedge looks too generous for commodity motors and packs; it holds better for harder components.

### Cited Findings

**Thesis.** US law now bars Chinese motors, magnets and batteries from defense drones and blocks new foreign drones and robots from FCC approval, while compliant parts are scarce enough to have delayed 40% of the first Drone Dominance order. A producer of compliant motors, actuators, reducers, magnets, packs and flight electronics sells into a market closed to the low-cost leader, then rides drone and robot volumes.

| Capital to first revenue (10 = low) | 8 | Single-digit to low tens of millions |
| Founder buildability | 8 | Known technology, hireable skills |

Judgement: C4 is buildable and will produce revenue quickly, which is why many are already building it. The adversarial reading is that the first round mistook a 2026 delivery crunch for a durable gap. The legal wall keeps China out but not Japan, Korea, Taiwan or Europe; the listed US leader earns a 35% gross margin and still loses money at a $64M run-rate; one new motor line covers the whole programme; and the buyer is trying to cut drone prices by more than half. The version worth building is narrower: components that are hard to make (precision actuators, reducers, high-power speed controllers, packs with compliant cells) for buyers with statutory compliance needs and longer programmes (missiles, interceptors, larger aircraft), with magnets secured by contract.

### Gaps
- Component price premiums (no published numbers).
- Allied-supplier capacity and pricing for drone-class motors and reducers.
- Magnet capacity by supplier and year.
- Evidence that any Western humanoid maker buys merchant actuators at volume.

## 8. Candidate C5: merchant solid rocket motors and energetics, plus cheap interceptors

### Takeaway
C5 is the weakest of the three for a new founder. Demand is large on paper, but motor casting is not the scarce step, qualification takes years, the one buyer is cutting the surge, incumbents are tripling under seven-year frameworks, and the new missile primes make their own motors. The only parts of C5 that survive are upstream (binder, cases, nozzles, igniters) and the interceptor product race, which is a different business.

### Cited Findings

**Thesis.** Missile output is capped by solid rocket motors from two incumbents, the Pentagon is ordering several times current output and wants more sources, and cheap interceptors are replacing $4M missiles for drone defence. A merchant maker of tactical motors and energetics, and of low-cost effectors, sells into funded shortage.

**Actor simulation, 2027-2032** (all [I])

- China and trade policy matter little to demand here; the input exposure is domestic single points (AP, binder), plus rare earths for seekers and actuators, which the motor maker does not control.
- For interceptors the sequence is different: fly-off entry within months, small lots, rapid redesign, and a market where NATO and Gulf buyers matter as much as the US. It rewards a product team, not a propellant plant.

**Market arithmetic**
- Market today [I]: L3Harris's missile-propulsion unit had about $3.6B of 2025 revenue [C via Contrary] and includes more than tactical motors; with Northrop's undisclosed propulsion business the US solid-propulsion market is probably $6-8B a year. I found no published total.
- Supply gap in units (FY2027 request vs current output [N1]): PAC-3 MSE 3,203 vs about 550 (5.8x); THAAD 857 ordered vs 96 a year (8.9x; target 400); SM-6 96 a year vs a stated maximum of 239-360.
- Dollar gap already allocated: Northrop's second-source PAC-3 motor framework is $2B over seven years, about $286M a year; THAAD components $1B over seven years, about $143M a year.
- Expansion if motors were 5-10x faster and 30-50% cheaper: I assume the motor is about 10% of missile cost (unsourced; Contrary reports no data), so a 50% motor price cut lowers a $4.3M PAC-3 by about 5%, which does not move demand. The expansion is unblocked missiles, which are other firms' products: PAC-3 going from 550 to 2,000 a year adds 1,450 x $4.3M = $6.2B a year of missiles and, at 10%, about $0.6B a year of motors, which the frameworks have already assigned to L3Harris and Northrop.
- The genuinely price-elastic tier (cheap cruise missiles and hypersonics, roughly 9,000-11,000 a year planned [N1]) uses jet engines or in-house motors; merchant booster content at an assumed $10,000-30,000 each would be $90-330M a year at most [I].
- Interceptors: 13,000 x $15,000 = $195M bought in 2026 [N1]; NATO's $40B over five years is $8B a year for all counter-drone spending; if effectors are 30% (assumption) that is about $2.4B a year across the alliance [I].

**Kill criteria**
- No written AP allocation from AMPAC (tons per year, start date) within six months.
- No prime or program office willing to name a specific motor, qualification plan and cost-share within 12 months.
- FY2028 request for the ten-programme munitions portfolio at or below about $27B.
- Northrop released from its FTC supply obligation and two more entrants qualified, leaving no open second-source slot.
- Site licensing and safety approval projected beyond 24 months.

**Cheapest 90-day test (about $100-200k).** Do not cast propellant. Test whether there is an open slot at all: (a) ask AMPAC for an AP allocation letter; (b) interview the propulsion supply-chain leads at ten buyers (two incumbent motor makers, three legacy primes, five new missile and interceptor makers) with one question, "which purchased item is setting your delivery date today, and would you issue a purchase order to a new source for it?"; (c) try to obtain one paid order for a non-energetic item (a nozzle, insulated case or igniter housing), which needs no explosives licence and can be made in a machine shop. If the answers point to cases, nozzles and binder rather than motors, the business to build is an inert-component or ingredient supplier, which merges into C1. For the interceptor half, the test is to get a prototype into the next JIATF-401 or Gauntlet-style evaluation, which costs more and takes longer than 90 days.

Judgement: the first-round note ranked merchant motors second among defense wedges on the strength of a sole-source PAC-3 motor and a 3,203-versus-550 gap. ## 9. Cross-candidate comparison and re-ranking

### Takeaway
Ranked by evidence after stress-testing: C1 first (real, lasting production bottleneck; slow and capital-heavy; strategic exit), C4 second in a narrowed form (fast and cheap to start; small, crowding, weakly defensible), C5 last (production is not the bottleneck; single buyer cutting back). In all three the first-round notes overstated how much of the opportunity a new, faster producer can capture inside 24 months.

### Cited Findings
- [M] The three hardest facts found in this pass: Howmet Engine Products at a 39.1% EBITDA margin with customers sold out to 2031 ([release](https://finviz.com/news/411256/howmet-aerospace-reports-third-quarter-2026-results); [IIR](https://www.industrialinfo.com/news/article/howmet-adds-capacity-as-gas-turbine-demand-skyrockets--364394)); Unusual Machines at $13.9M a quarter, 35.4% gross margin, adding 100,000 motors a month ([letter](https://finviz.com/news/417591/unusual-machines-third-quarter-2026-shareholder-letter)); the Senate's $47.2B munitions and $3B autonomy lines against requests of roughly $70B+ and $55B ([Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/senate-appropriators-unveil-1-1t-pentagon-spending-bill-folding-in-reconciliation-priorities/)).
- [M] Buyers and incumbents are integrating in all three: GE-CPP and SpaceX's foundry (C1) ([Leeham](https://leehamnews.com/2026/09/09/ge-aerospace-moves-to-secure-critical-engine-casting-capacity/)); Figure, 1X, PDW and drone makers (C4) ([Humanoid.guide](https://humanoid.guide/us-humanoid-supply-chain-push-tests-china-dependence/) [S]); Anduril and Castelion (C5) ([Contrary](https://research.contrary.com/report/the-american-missile-crisis)).
- [N1] The red-team rule that winners had a customer paying before the factory, and that specialised plants have almost no resale value — [skeptic note sources, e.g. Construction Physics on Katerra](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/another-day-in-katerradise)

- C4 has the highest unweighted total because it is easy, not because it is large or durable. Weighting the two criteria that the founder's question is about (latent demand that a faster, cheaper producer unlocks; production as the true bottleneck), C1 leads.
- None of the three is a Model T. In each, a faster and cheaper part mostly unblocks someone else's product (turbines, aircraft, drones, missiles), and the part's own market grows with that product rather than with its own price. The parts supplier's reward is a qualified position and margin, not a volume explosion.
- Common failure mode to avoid: scaling a plant before a buyer has qualified the output (Mesquite, Katerra, Northvolt in the first-round notes). The 90-day tests above all substitute rented capacity and paid first articles for a factory.
- A combined path worth testing: the inert-component finding in C5 (cases, nozzles, igniter housings) and the expendable-engine finding in C1 point to the same customer set (missile and interceptor makers) and the same capability (fast, qualified metal parts). A C1 company whose first customers are missile-propulsion buyers is better supported by the evidence than either C1 aimed at flight engines or C5 as a motor maker.

- The weightings behind the re-ranking are a judgement; the report writer should test them against the other sectors' candidates.
- Three numbers would change the ranking if obtained: the real qualification calendar at a turbine OEM (C1), the real compliant-versus-Chinese price gap by component (C4), and whether any open second-source motor slot exists (C5).
