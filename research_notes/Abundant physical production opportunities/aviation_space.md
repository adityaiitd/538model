## 1. Market size today, backlogs, delivery rates vs targets, engine shortages, fleet age and lease rates

### Takeaway
Commercial airliners are the single clearest "sold-out" physical product in the economy: about 16,400 jets on backlog (roughly 11 years of output, on the order of $1.3T by my estimate), record fleet age, and record lease rates, with engines and their cast/forged parts as the binding constraint. Space is smaller in hardware dollars (satellite manufacturing about $20B/yr, 2025) but is the only segment where unit volumes have already grown 10x+ on cost reduction.

### Inferences
- [EST, mine] Backlog value: 16,402 aircraft at roughly $80M average realized value (Avolon's ~$120B for roughly 1,450-1,500 deliveries in 2026) is about $1.3T. Treat as an order-of-magnitude figure; neither OEM's dollar backlog was retrieved this session.
- The airliner shortage is a supply-chain and certification-rate problem more than a final-assembly problem. The binding items named by sources are engines (GTF durability, LEAP ramp), castings/forgings, wings, titanium and composites. A 10x cheaper final-assembly line would not by itself clear the backlog.
- The engine aftermarket is where the pain is monetised: overhaul costs up 68% on 10% more flying, spare-engine rents up 30%. This is evidence of pricing power, not just scarcity, and it is where airlines most want a new supplier.
- Space hardware is small in dollars relative to aviation ($20B satellite manufacturing vs about $120B airliner deliveries plus $136B MRO), but unit growth is far faster.

## 2. Cost structure: what abundant production could cut vs what is certification, liability and low volume

### Takeaway
In aviation, most of the price is not touch labor: it is non-recurring engineering and certification amortised over small volumes, supplier pricing power on engines and castings, and (for light aircraft) liability. Abundant production attacks recurring cost directly, but the 3-10x gains require volume and a lighter certification path at the same time. General aviation is the proof: real prices rose about 4-5x as volumes fell about 10x.

### Cited Findings

**Airliners**
- [EST] Clean-sheet development: modern programs cost $20-30B+ once overruns are included (777 est. $12-14B; A380 budgeted $10.7B; 787 planned $5.5B/5 years, took 9 years and about 4x). A derivative costs roughly 10-20% of a clean sheet and gets most of the benefit — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/a-cycle-of-misery-the-business-of); [Transport Geography](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter5/air-transport/aircraft-development-costs/)
- [EST] Program economics: breakeven around 500 units; Boeing averaged just over 5% profit 1970-2010; of 22 jet airliners developed by 1982 only two were believed to have made money; learning curves mean low-volume programs gain most from each doubling — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/a-cycle-of-misery-the-business-of)
- [MEASURED] A unit-cost data point: Boeing's 2017 trade petition alleged Bombardier sold CSeries jets to Delta at $19.6M each against a $33.2M production cost; Bombardier's development cost went from a $2.1B estimate (2005) to $5.4B at certification (2015) and $7B by its 2020 exit — [Wikipedia, Airbus A220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A220)

**Engines**
- [EST] Development: P&W geared turbofan about $10B; payback takes 15-20 years; testing runs thousands to tens of thousands of hours; commercial engines usually derive from government-funded military cores. Only GE/CFM, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce make large commercial engines; China has not yet succeeded with an engine for the C919 — [Construction Physics, "Why It's So Hard to Build a Jet Engine"](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/why-its-so-hard-to-build-a-jet-engine)
- [EST] A single process escape is ruinous: P&W lost billions on GTF defects traced to a microscopic contaminant in turbine-disk powder metal — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/why-its-so-hard-to-build-a-jet-engine)
- [EST] In an overhaul, parts and materials are about 60% of direct cost (i.e., the OEM-controlled part, not shop labor) — [Airways citing Oliver Wyman](https://airwaysmag.com/new-post/airline-engine-bills-rise-groundings-ease)

**General aviation decomposition**
- [MEASURED] Volume: US manufacturers shipped 17,811 GA airplanes in 1978 (GAO rounds to about 18,000), 2,691 in 1983, and 928 in 1994; output fell 94% between 1978 and 1988; rebounded to 2,816 in 2000 after GARA — [GAO-01-916](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916.htm); [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2011/08/response_to_tho.html); [Wikipedia, GARA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Aviation_Revitalization_Act)
- [MEASURED] Today: 1,782 piston airplanes shipped worldwide in 2025 — [GAMA](https://gama.aero/news-and-events/press-releases/gama-releases-2025-aircraft-shipment-and-billing-report/)
- [MEASURED] Cessna 172 volume: 1,725 (1977), 1,725 (1978), 1,850 (1979), 1,125 (1980); production halted 1986-1996 because of product liability costs; more than 44,000 built in total — [Wikipedia, Cessna 172](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cessna_172)
- [MEASURED/EST] Price: a 172 listed at $12,450 in 1966; about $12,500 in 1970 (about $85,000 in 2021 dollars per Plane & Pilot). A new 172S is quoted at $400,000-$520,000 in 2026 (base sticker around $419,620; dealer/marketing sources, not Textron's price list) — [Wikipedia, Cessna 172](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cessna_172); [inkl/Plane & Pilot](https://www.inkl.com/news/prices-for-new-general-aviation-aircraft-may-be-pricing-pilots-out-of-the-market); [BlackJet](https://blackjet.com/post/cessna-172-cost)
- [MEASURED] GAO price series: average new single-engine piston rose from just over $25,000 (1975) to nearly $112,000 (1990), more than doubling in constant dollars; new pistons averaged $162,000 (1994) and $220,000 (1999), so prices kept rising after liability reform — [GAO-01-916](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916.htm)
- [MEASURED] Liability: industry paid claims and defense costs rose from $24M/yr (1976) to $210M/yr (1986); in 1987 the three big single-engine makers put liability at $70,000-$100,000 per aircraft built. After GARA's 18-year statute of repose (1994), one maker's open suits fell from about 900 to about 80 — [GAO-01-916](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916.htm)
- [CLAIM] GAMA's Hennig: 20-30% of a new aircraft's price is the manufacturer self-insuring against product liability — [inkl/Plane & Pilot](https://www.inkl.com/news/prices-for-new-general-aviation-aircraft-may-be-pricing-pilots-out-of-the-market)
- Counter-evidence on the liability story: GAO also lists a glut of used aircraft, the 1980-82 and 1990-91 recessions, repeal of the investment tax credit, and declining interest in flying; AVweb argues the tax change, not liability, was the primary driver — [GAO-01-916](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916.htm); [AVweb](https://avweb.com/features/will-general-aviation-survive/)

### Inferences
- [EST, mine] GA real-price decomposition. A 172 cost roughly $85-125k in today's dollars around 1966-70 and about $420-520k now: a real increase of about 4-5x while annual 172 output fell from about 1,800 to (unsourced, probably) a few hundred. The 1987 liability figure ($70-100k per aircraft) was comparable to the entire average selling price of the day, which explains the 1986 shutdown; Hennig's 20-30% suggests liability is now roughly $100-150k of a $450k+ aircraft. The remainder is low-volume hand-building, certified-component pricing (engine, avionics) and amortised certification. Because prices kept rising after GARA, liability reform alone does not restore 1970s prices: volume and a lighter certification path are also needed.
- For airliners, a new entrant cannot count on recurring-cost savings to offset a $20-30B clean-sheet and 500-unit breakeven; the CSeries numbers show even a good aircraft was sold at 60% of unit cost early in its learning curve.
- For satellites, cost has already fallen about 100x from GEO-class ($150-400M) to Starlink-class (under $1M) inside one vertically integrated firm; merchant buses ($3.5-6M) sit in between. Government proliferated satellites at $40-60M each are the obvious remaining price umbrella.
- Engines are the least "abundance-ready" product: the cost is in materials science, testing and 15-20 year payback, not factory labor. Cast/forged parts within engines are the more addressable layer.

## 3. Latent demand: what happens at 3-10x lower price or shorter lead time

### Takeaway
The strongest measured elasticity is in space (satellites launched up 54% in one year; Starlink from 1M to 13.6M subscribers in under four years) and small drones (Walmart's millionth delivery, 40% of it in one quarter). Airliners show proven unmet demand at today's prices (an 11-year queue), which is a lead-time problem more than a price problem. Personal aviation shows a historical 10x volume level at roughly one-quarter of today's real price, but the evidence that this demand would return is weaker.

### Cited Findings
- [MEASURED] Launch and satellites: 4,544 spacecraft in 2025 (+54% YoY) on 325 launches (+25%) — [BryceTech via Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/launch/2026/04/10/brycetech-report-shows-spacex-accounted-for-50-of-launches-in-2025)

### Inferences
- [EST, mine] Airliner latent demand at shorter lead time. If the Oliver Wyman figure of 6,000+ aircraft lost to supply limits through 2030 is right, about 1,200 extra aircraft/yr would be absorbed at today's prices, worth roughly $90-100B/yr at about $80M each. That is demand for delivery slots, not for a cheaper product. A 3x cheaper airliner would shift airline economics little, because the aircraft is a minority of seat cost (fuel, crew, airports, maintenance); I found no sourced elasticity for aircraft price.
- [EST, mine] Personal aircraft at car-like prices. The US bought about 17,800 GA aircraft in 1978 with 222M people when a 172 cost roughly $85-125k in today's money. Scaling for population alone gives about 27,000/yr; a plausible bracket for the US at $100k and simplified operation is 10,000-30,000 units/yr ($1-3B), against about 1,800 worldwide today. At a true car price ($30-50k) the limit becomes pilots, airports, weather and insurance, not price. Evidence against: prices kept rising after GARA without demand recovering, the pilot base fell, and GAO cites lifestyle change. This is a real but modest market, not a Model T.
- Drone delivery is in the steep part of an S-curve: Walmart's run-rate implies about 1.6M deliveries/yr from 66 stores, with 570+ sites announced, a roughly 9x site expansion before any Part 108 relief. Unit demand for delivery aircraft could reach tens of thousands per year in the US. No cost-per-delivery figure was found, so profitability is unproven.
- Space demand responds strongly when the buyer also controls the cost curve (Starlink) or when government buys proliferated architectures. Third-party commercial demand is thinner than headline counts suggest.

## 4. Regulatory and timeline gates

### Takeaway
Certification is the dominant time gate in crewed aviation: 8-15 years and $5-20B+ for a new airliner, about 10 years and $10B for an engine, and eVTOL leaders are now two or more years late with passenger service slipping to 2027. Three deregulatory changes matter for a new entrant: MOSAIC (in force since July 2026), Part 108 BVLOS (final rule at OIRA, not yet published), and the supersonic overland NPRM (comments closed Aug 2026, not final). Space licensing is a months-scale gate, not a decade-scale one.

### Cited Findings

**Transport aircraft and engines (Part 25 / Part 33 / EASA / production approval)**
- [MEASURED] COMAC C919: program started 2008, first flight May 2017, CAAC certificate Sept 2022 (14 years); announced cost CN¥58B (US$9.5B), estimated actual well over CN¥125B (US$20B). EASA validation not yet achieved; EASA's director estimated 2028-2031 — [Wikipedia, C919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comac_C919)
- [MEASURED] Mitsubishi SpaceJet: launched March 2008, first flight Nov 2015, never certified, terminated Feb 2023; cost rose from ¥150B to ¥350B by 2017 plus a further ¥220B injection in 2018; four of five delays were at least partly failures to document certification work — [Wikipedia, SpaceJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_SpaceJet)
- [MEASURED] Bombardier CSeries: launched July 2008, certified Dec 2015, in service July 2016 (8 years) — [Wikipedia, A220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A220)
- [MEASURED] Production approval is a separate gate: the FAA capped 737 output and only approved 42/month in late 2025 — [Oliver Wyman](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2026-2036.html)

**Light aircraft (Part 23 and MOSAIC)**
- [CLAIM] Part 23 data point: Otto Aviation said in 2020 it needed about $200M for Part 23 certification of the Celera in three years — [Wikipedia, Otto Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Aviation)
- [MEASURED] MOSAIC final rule issued July 2025. Sport pilot, repairman and maintenance provisions effective 22 Oct 2025; light-sport aircraft certification provisions effective 24 July 2026. It removes the 1,320 lb weight cap, allows airplanes with up to four seats (sport pilots limited to one passenger), and permits retractable gear, constant-speed propellers and simplified flight controls; new rotorcraft and powered-lift LSA are limited to two seats — [FAA MOSAIC](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/MOSAIC); [FAA fact sheet](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/fact_sheets/MOSAIC_Fact_Sheet.pdf)
- [MEASURED] MOSAIC uses a manufacturer Statement of Compliance to consensus standards instead of a Type Certificate and Production Certificate; the FAA formally accepted the ASTM standards in July 2026 — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ae/insights/publications/2025/07/faa-issues-final-mosaic-rule); [General Aviation News](https://generalaviationnews.com/2026/07/19/faa-accepts-astm-standards-mosaic/)

**Drones (Part 108)**
- [MEASURED] NPRM published August 2025; final rule sent to OIRA on 10 July 2026; still under review in mid-November 2026; FAA "hoping" to publish before end of 2026 ("on the 10-yard line"); one source computes a statutory deadline of 7 Dec 2026. Publication will carry a later effective date. Until then operators need case-by-case Part 107 waivers or exemptions — [UAV Coach](https://uavcoach.com/part-108-stuck/); [Vertical Aviation International](https://verticalavi.org/vai-daily/part-108-final-rule-sent-to-oira-for-review/)

- [MEASURED] Existing path works but is bespoke: Zipline got Part 135 in June 2022 and BVLOS without visual observers in Sept 2023; Wing got the first drone air-carrier certificate in April 2019 — [Wikipedia, Zipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipline_(drone_delivery_company)); [Wikipedia, Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_(company))

**eVTOL (powered-lift)**
- [MEASURED] Joby and Archer both pushed first US passenger operations to 2027 in Q3 2026 earnings (both had targeted 2025). **Supersonic**
- [MEASURED] Executive Order 14304 (6 June 2025) directed the FAA to repeal the overland supersonic ban (14 CFR 91.817) within 180 days and set an interim noise-based standard. The NPRM "Enabling Supersonic Overland Flight" was published 2 July 2026 (about a year later than the EO's deadline); comments closed 17 Aug 2026; no final rule found as of Nov 2026. ### Inferences
- Rough gate costs for planning (my synthesis of the above, flagged EST): new airliner 8-15 years and $7-25B; large engine about 10 years and $10B; Part 23 aircraft about 3+ years and $200M+; eVTOL 6+ years after certification basis and multiple billions; MOSAIC LSA and Part 108 drones months to low years via declaration of compliance; satellites and launch months to about two years for licences.

- Timelines announced by executive order have slipped by about a year in both the BVLOS and supersonic cases; discount stated regulatory dates accordingly.

## 5. Objections and actor responses

### Takeaway
Incumbents respond to entrants and to shortage with pricing power, trade action and aftermarket control rather than with capacity. Every new airliner entrant since 2008 either failed, was absorbed, or remains hostage to Western engines and regulators. The realistic objections for a new producer are qualification time, incumbents' contractual lock on repair data and parts, and capital providers' memory of these failures.

### Cited Findings
- **Duopoly trade response.** Boeing's 2017 petition led the US Commerce Department to set a 292% duty on the CSeries; although the ITC later reversed, the pressure pushed Bombardier to hand Airbus 50.01% of the program for a symbolic CAD$1, and Bombardier exited entirely in 2020 for $591M after about $7B of spend — [Wikipedia, A220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A220)
- **Why entrants struggled.** SpaceJet: certification-documentation failures, redesigns, and US pilot-union scope clauses (the MRJ90 was over the 39-tonne limit, costing a 100-aircraft order). C919: 44 or so delivered in four years against a 150/yr target, dependent on US-licensed LEAP-1C engines, no EASA approval before 2028-2031. A220: only 559 delivered by Oct 2026, breakeven slipped to mid-2020s — [Wikipedia, SpaceJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_SpaceJet); [Wikipedia, C919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comac_C919); [Wikipedia, A220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A220)
- **Structural economics.** Of 22 jet airliners developed by 1982, only two were believed profitable; competitors (Lockheed, Douglas, Convair, de Havilland) were driven out or merged — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/a-cycle-of-misery-the-business-of)
- **Engine OEMs and the aftermarket.** IATA says "longstanding commercial restrictions on repair instructions, tooling, approved repair networks, and spares distribution" limit airlines' use of certified alternatives; it wants OEMs to adopt the IATA-CFM agreement principles on third-party MRO, alternative (PMA) parts and repairs. United's Scott Kirby, asked whether engine makers have too much pricing power, said "yes and yes" — [IATA](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/06-24-iata-outlines-four-priorities-to-strengthen-aviation-supply-chain); [Airways](https://airwaysmag.com/new-post/airline-engine-bills-rise-groundings-ease)
- **Airlines.** IATA's Walsh frames the issue as value-chain imbalance (airline margins vs engine and avionics makers) and asks to "re-balance value chain profitability" — airlines are a willing customer for alternative supply — [IATA, Dec 2025](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-12-09-01/)
- **Casting/forging suppliers.** They prefer larger, more profitable volume to new capacity; an RBC analyst called added capacity "incredibly capital intensive"; contracts run 5-7 years; SpaceX's entry into blade casting was judged "far less threatening than it looks" by Deutsche Bank — [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/fixing-aerospaces-supply-chain-for-casting-and-forging); [Bloomberg via Tiger](https://www.itiger.com/news/2650735186); [Stocktwits](https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/Markets/Equity/elon-musk-s-space-x-blade-plan-knocks-howmet-as-hwm-suffers-steepest-slide-in-16-months/cZYz4OwRJsz)
- **FAA.** Holds production-rate approval over Boeing (42/month) and has not certified MAX 7/10 years after launch — [Oliver Wyman](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2026-2036.html); [Forecast International](https://flightplan.forecastinternational.com/2026/11/10/airbus-and-boeing-report-october-2026-commercial-aircraft-orders-and-deliveries/)
- **Insurers/liability.** Liability drove Cessna out of pistons for a decade and is still claimed to be 20-30% of new-aircraft price — [GAO-01-916](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-916.htm); [inkl/Plane & Pilot](https://www.inkl.com/news/prices-for-new-general-aviation-aircraft-may-be-pricing-pilots-out-of-the-market)
- **Cities/NIMBY.** Volocopter did not fly at the 2024 Paris Olympics (engine not certified) and Paris later scrapped its flying-taxi plans; Volocopter's Singapore launch stalled for lack of local funding partners — [Wikipedia, Volocopter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volocopter)
- **Customers hedge.** United's Kirby said in Nov 2026 that Boom's Overture will miss 2029 (aggregator-reported); PaxEx notes airline contracts are conditional on "industry-standard operating, performance and safety requirements"; United's Archer order is conditional with a $10M deposit for 100 aircraft — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/boom-supersonic); [PaxEx.Aero](https://paxex.aero/boom-pivots-to-power); [Wikipedia, Archer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archer_Aviation)
- **Financiers.** eVTOL insolvencies after large raises: Lilium accumulated losses of €1.359B by March 2024, filed for insolvency Oct 2024 and again Feb 2025; Volocopter filed 30 Dec 2024 — [Wikipedia, Lilium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilium_GmbH); [Wikipedia, Volocopter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volocopter)
- **Launch/satellite incumbency.** SpaceX and CALT were about 88% of 2025 launches; Starlink and Kuiper may prioritise internal payloads; many merchant bus makers were bought by primes (Terran Orbital by Lockheed for about $450M, Blue Canyon by RTX) — [Contrary Research, Apex](https://research.contrary.com/company/apex)
- **Workforce.** 41% of US mechanics are over 60; foundry work is seen as "dark, dirty, and dangerous" — [Oliver Wyman](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2026-2036.html); [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/fixing-aerospaces-supply-chain-for-casting-and-forging)

### Inferences
- Airlines and lessors are natural allies of a new parts or engine-component supplier; engine OEMs are the natural opponents, and they control the approved repair and parts data. A PMA or licensed-repair entrant should expect contractual, not regulatory, resistance.
- The duopoly does not need to out-compete an entrant on product. It can wait out the 8-15 year certification clock, use trade law, or buy the entrant at distress (CSeries). This argues against a clean-sheet airliner as a first product.
- Pilot unions matter mainly through scope clauses (regional jets) and single-pilot/autonomy resistance; I found no 2026 source quantifying this beyond the SpaceJet case.

## 7. Buildability: capital, time to revenue, and best wedges

### Takeaway
The evidence favours entering where a declaration of compliance or a customer qualification replaces a type certificate, and where a named buyer is already short: engine and structural parts (castings, forgings, machined parts) sold first to defense, space and land-turbine customers; satellite buses; delivery drones and their production; and MOSAIC-category light aircraft. Finished certified passenger aircraft remain a $1B-$20B, 8-15 year bet with a poor base rate.

### Cited Findings
- [MEASURED] Parts wedge is fundable and fast: Hadrian reached a $7.87B valuation selling machined parts and dedicated factory capacity to primes and the Navy — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/hadrian-1-37bn-series-d-7-87bn-defence-factories)
- [EST] The bottleneck parts have 6-18+ month lead times, 2-year furnace lead times and 5-10 years to rebuild talent, and incumbents decline to add capacity — [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/fixing-aerospaces-supply-chain-for-casting-and-forging); [Bloomberg via Tiger](https://www.itiger.com/news/2650735186)
- [MEASURED] IATA is explicitly asking for expanded "licensed production of critical components", alternative parts and repairs, i.e., the customer side is organised and vocal — [IATA/Emerton](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/06-24-urgent-action-needed-to-ease-engine-mro-bottlenecks/)
- [MEASURED] Satellite bus wedge: Apex went from seed (Oct 2022) to $60M revenue (2025); York to $386M revenue and IPO — [Contrary Research](https://research.contrary.com/company/apex)
- [MEASURED] Drone wedge: 570+ additional Walmart sites announced across Zipline and Wing; Part 108 as proposed needs no type certificate — [Supermarket News](https://www.supermarketnews.com/grocery-technology/walmart-zipline-reach-agreement-to-add-300-drone-delivery-sites); [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/11/walmart-drone-delivery-wing-los-angeles-miami); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/faa-proposes-comprehensive-bvlos-uas-regulatory-framework)
- [MEASURED] MOSAIC wedge: since 24 July 2026 a four-seat airplane can be sold on a Statement of Compliance with no Type or Production Certificate — [FAA](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/MOSAIC); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-ae/insights/publications/2025/07/faa-issues-final-mosaic-rule)
- [MEASURED] Certified-aircraft base rate: SpaceJet (15 years, never certified), CSeries ($7B, sold for CAD$1 of control), C919 ($20B+, 44 delivered), Lilium/Volocopter insolvent, Joby/Archer two-plus years late — sources in sections 4-6.

### Inferences (ranked, my judgement; all EST)
1. **Engine and turbine hot-section and structural parts (castings, forgings, precision machining) as a merchant supplier.** Demand: airliner backlog, engine ramp to about 3,700/yr, shop visits rising about 7x by 2040, plus land-turbine backlogs of up to eight years. Wedge: start with space, defense and industrial gas-turbine customers (no FAA gate), then pursue OEM qualification, licensed production or PMA. Capital: hundreds of millions to low billions; first revenue in 2-3 years; aviation-grade qualification likely 3-5+ years. Main risk: OEM qualification and incumbents' long-term contracts; a single quality escape is existential.
2. **Satellite buses and space components at rate.** Proven 2-3 year path to revenue; defense demand (SDA, Golden Dome) pays $40-60M per satellite today against $3.5-6M merchant bus prices. Risk: crowded (Apex, K2, York, Rocket Lab, Muon), commercial non-Starlink demand only about $8B, launch access controlled by a competitor.
3. **Delivery-drone airframes and production (or contract manufacturing for operators).** Steepest measured demand curve; regulation moving to declarations; sub-55 lb aircraft suit high-rate automated production. Risk: Zipline and Wing are vertically integrated; Part 108 timing; unit economics undisclosed.
4. **MOSAIC four-seat personal aircraft at roughly $100-150k.** A newly legal category, a 10x historical volume precedent, and no type certificate. Risk: modest absolute market ($1-3B/yr US by my estimate), liability, engine and avionics still bought from certified-price suppliers unless also re-sourced, pilot supply.
5. **MRO capacity and alternative parts/repairs.** $136B market growing to $193B, organised customer demand, labor shortage favouring automation. Risk: OEM control of repair data; slower, service-heavy business.
6. **eVTOL, supersonic, blended-wing, new airliners.** Largest prizes but 8-15 years and $1B-$20B+ with a poor base rate; better approached later from a parts or engine-component position.

- Common pattern for wedges 1-3: sell first to buyers who can qualify a supplier themselves (DoD, SpaceX-like primes, turbine packagers, Walmart), and treat FAA-certified civil aviation as the second market.
