## 1. Model T: what actually happened, and what enabled it besides the assembly line

### Takeaway
The Model T is a real 100x+ case: runabout price fell 68% nominal (about 75% real by 1921) while Ford output rose about 180x and US car registrations rose about 57x between 1909 and 1925. But the paper trail shows five enablers besides the moving line: a design-for-cost product aimed at non-owners (farmers), a utilitarian use case with no status penalty, a field-serviceable product with an aftermarket ecosystem, public road spending, and consumer credit. Where one of those was missing (abroad, where tariffs made the car 20-130% dearer), adoption did not follow.

### Cited Findings
- Runabout price and Ford production by year: 1909 $825 / 10,666 units; 1910 $900 / 19,050; 1911 $680 / 34,858; 1912 $590 / 68,733; 1913 $525 / 170,211; 1914 $440 / 202,667; 1915 $390 / 308,162; 1916 $345 / 501,462; 1917 $500 / 735,020; 1920 $395 / 941,042 (fiscal); 1921 $325 / 971,610; 1922 $319 / 1,301,067; 1923 $364 / 2,011,125 (peak); 1924 $265 / 1,922,048; 1925 $260 / 1,911,705; 1926 $360 / 1,554,465; 1927 $360 / 399,725. Touring car: $850 (1909), $290 (1925). Table total 14.69 million; Ford counted 15 million. — [Wikipedia, Ford Model T (table sourced to McCalley)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_T)
- Sources disagree on the launch price: $825 (Ford corporate), $850 (The Henry Ford, touring), $950 (transport geography text). — [Transport Geography](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter1/the-setting-of-global-transportation-systems/ford-cost-production-1908-1924/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_T)
- "The Model T's nominal and real price fell by roughly half between 1909-15; the real price then halved again between 1915-21." — [Eli, Hausman & Rhode, NBER WP 31454 (2023)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- US automobile registrations (measured, FHWA): 1900 8,000; 1905 77,400; 1908 194,400; 1909 305,950; 1910 458,377; 1913 1,190,393; 1915 2,332,426; 1917 4,727,468; 1920 8,131,522; 1923 13,253,019; 1925 17,481,001; 1929 23,120,897; 1930 23,034,753. — [FHWA Highway Statistics, Table MV-200](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ohim/summary95/mv200.pdf)
- Before the Model T the car was a yacht-like luxury: mean 1908 car price about $3,000 (about $87,000 in 2021 dollars) against per-capita income of $317 and manufacturing wage earnings of $518 a year; one car per 300 Americans in 1909. — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Household car ownership (OWID series): 10% in 1915, 28% in 1920, 49% in 1925, 58.5% in 1929, then flat or down for two decades (52% in 1935, 58% in 1950), 75% in 1960. — [Our World in Data, technology adoption by US households](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/technology-adoption-by-households-in-the-united-states)
- Ford share: 15% of US car production in 1908, 48% in 1914, 57% in 1923 (search summary, page not opened). Stock share: Fords were 29% of cars on the road in 1914, 42% in 1917, 47% (Model Ts) in 1922, and half by 1926. Around 1913 Model Ts were 96% of all cars sold under $600. — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Labour productivity: chassis assembly fell from 12.5 labour-hours (summer 1913) to 2 hours 38 minutes (December 1913), almost 80%. In 1914 Ford made 260,722 cars with 13,000 employees; all other US makers made 286,770 with 66,350 employees. — [NBER WP 31454, citing Nevins 1954](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Full-car build time fell from 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by 1914. Five-dollar day announced 5 Jan 1914 as a profit-sharing bonus ($2.30 base plus $2.70) against a "crippling labor turnover rate of 370 percent". — [The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/explore/blog/fords-five-dollar-day/)
- Model T learning rate: 85% progress ratio (15% cost fall per doubling), per Abernathy 1974. — [Construction Physics, "Where Are My Damn Learning Curves?"](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/where-are-my-damn-learning-curves)
- Design for cost and for the unserved buyer: about 1,200 lb using vanadium steel, high ground clearance for unpaved roads, no electric starter until 1919, black only 1914-25, farmer-serviceable, and a large aftermarket for modifications (the authors compare it to an app store). — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- New buyers, not substitution: after mass production began in 1913, cars per capita 1909-1915 grew 840% in the least rural quartile of states and 2,050% in the most rural. About one third of cars were farmer-owned in 1924. A 1914 trade study: "People will more readily buy Fords where the kind of car they drive has little or no effect on their social standing." — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Income elasticity of car ownership across states was "well above one" in 1909 and "well under 1" by 1929, i.e. luxury became necessity. — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Natural experiment on price: the Model T cost 20-50% more in Canada and 20-130% more elsewhere (tariffs of 12-35% plus sub-scale local production). UK vehicles per capita fell from 36% of the US level in 1913 to 14% in 1929; Germany had 30% of US telephones per capita in 1929 but 4% of US motor vehicles. — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Complements: Federal Aid Road Act 1916 ($75 million); Federal Aid Highway Act 1921; 31,000 miles of paved road by 1924; numbered highways approved 1926; GMAC founded 1919 to finance GM buyers; GM overtook Ford in the late 1920s using annual model changes. — [Wikipedia, Automotive industry in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_the_United_States)
- Capital intensity: Model T production used about 32,000 machine tools. — [Wikipedia, Productivity-improving technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity-improving_technologies)

### Inferences
- Naive arc elasticity, Ford level (own calculation from cited figures): real price down about 75% 1909-1921 (ln 4 = 1.39), Ford output up 91x (ln = 4.51), ratio about 3.3. Market level: registrations up 30x over the same years. These are upper bounds, not clean elasticities: income, roads, credit, quality and Ford's share gain all moved at once. The NBER authors explicitly decline to estimate an elasticity.
- The price cut that mattered was about 6x against the market average in nominal dollars (about $3,000 for the average 1908 car vs $525 for a 1913 Model T runabout), not the later $850 to $260 path. The step change was a new product for a new buyer; the learning curve then compounded it.
- The window was about 15 years. Output peaked in 1923 and slipped in 1924-25 while the price was at its low ($260-265), then fell to 1.55 million in 1926; household penetration stalled near 58% in 1929. Once the first-car market saturated, demand moved to features, credit and style, and GM took the lead. A cost leader's profit window closes when penetration saturates.
- Speed vs cost: the line cut labour-hours about 80% in months, but price fell about 50% over six years. Labour was only part of cost.

### Gaps
- Share of cars bought on instalment credit in the 1920s (commonly cited at around three quarters, Olney) was not verified in a fetched source.
- Dealer network size by year and Ford profit by year were not retrieved.
- Ford's production-share figures (15/48/57%) come from a search summary only.

## 2. Other cost-collapse cases with numbers

### Takeaway
Across about twenty cases, 10x+ market expansion followed cost collapse when the product was a general-purpose input or had large unserved need (solar, batteries, sequencing, containers, aluminium, launch-plus-Starlink). The inventing producers rarely kept the profit: gains went to buyers, to vertically integrated users of the cheap input, or to one scale leader. Wartime cases show speed can improve 5x in under two years, with cost improving much less.

### Cited Findings
- Solar PV modules: $132.38/W (1975), $21.23 (1983), $4.75 (2008), $0.857 (2013), $0.467 (2019), $0.273 (2023), $0.133 (2024), OWID inflation-adjusted series; about 1,000x in 49 years. Learning rate about 20% per doubling (20.2% average of studies); cumulative capacity 0.3 MW (1976) to 578,553 MW (2019). — [OWID solar-pv-prices](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/solar-pv-prices); [OWID, learning curves](https://ourworldindata.org/learning-curve)
- Solar electricity LCOE fell 89% 2009-2019 (learning rate 36%); onshore wind learning rate 23%; offshore wind 10%; coal $111 to $109/MWh (no learning; fuel is about 40% of cost); nuclear rose. — [OWID, "Why did renewables become so cheap so fast?"](https://ourworldindata.org/cheap-renewables-growth)
- Lithium-ion cells: about $9,200/kWh (1991) to $78/kWh (2024), more than 99%; about 19% fall per doubling since 1998; cumulative production up 27 million-fold 1991-2023. — [OWID, battery price decline](https://ourworldindata.org/battery-price-decline)
- Battery packs 2025: $108/kWh global average (down 8% on 2024); stationary storage $70/kWh (down 45%); BEV packs $99; LFP $81 vs NMC $128; China $84. — [BloombergNEF 2025 battery price survey](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/new-record-lows-for-battery-prices/); [Energy-Storage.news](https://www.energy-storage.news/li-ion-battery-pack-prices-fell-8-since-last-year-despite-metals-prices-rising-bloombergnef-says/)
- LEDs (Haitz's law): cost per lumen falls 10x per decade, light per package rises 20x per decade. — [Wikipedia, Haitz's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitz%27s_law)
- TVs and PCs: US CPI for televisions down 94% and for PCs down 96%, Dec 1997 to Aug 2015. — [BLS, The Economics Daily (2015)](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2015/long-term-price-trends-for-computers-tvs-and-related-items.htm)
- Smartphones: US adult ownership 35% (2011), 51-58% (2013), 81% (2019), 90% (2023), 91% (2024, 2025 and 2026). — [Pew Research mobile fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/)
- DNA sequencing: cost per human genome $95.3 million (2001), $7.1 million (2007), $342,502 (2008), $5,096 (2013), $525 (2022); about 180,000x in 21 years. — [OWID, NHGRI data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/cost-of-sequencing-a-full-human-genome)
- Launch to LEO ($/kg, FY2021 dollars): Space Shuttle $65,400 (1981); Saturn V $5,400 (1967); Delta IV Heavy $11,600; Atlas V $8,100; Falcon 9 $2,600 (2010); Falcon Heavy $1,500 (2018). — [OWID / CSIS Aerospace Security Project](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/cost-space-launches-low-earth-orbit)
- Starlink: 13.6 million subscribers (30 Sep 2026); about 11,150 satellites in orbit (Oct 2026), about 75% of active manoeuvrable satellites; 2025 revenue $11.4 billion with $4.4 billion operating income. — [Wikipedia, Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink)
- Drones: DJI Phantom launched at $629 (2013); DJI held 77% of the US consumer market (2020) and over 90% of the world consumer market (June 2024); 2025 revenue CN¥80 billion ($11.5 billion). FCC banned new DJI models in Dec 2025. — [Wikipedia, DJI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DJI)
- Lab-grown diamonds: prices down 74% from 2020 to early 2025 (growth time fell from weeks to hours); synthetics went from 2% of the gem market (2013) to 17% of diamond jewellery (2023). — [Wikipedia, Synthetic diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_diamond)
- Chinese EVs: plug-ins 6.3% of sales (2020), 15% (2021), 47.9% (2024), 51% (2025, 16.49 million passenger EVs); about 500 EV makers in 2019, about 100 by 2023; 2.62 million vehicles exported in 2025. — [Wikipedia, Electric vehicle industry in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_vehicle_industry_in_China)
- Container shipping: loading cost $5.86 per ton by hand vs $0.16 with containers (36x) in 1956; Sea-Land became profitable in 1961 and was sold for $530 million in 1969; about 90% of non-bulk cargo containerised by 2009. Port infrastructure adoption went from 10% (1968) to 80% (1981). — [Wikipedia, Malcom McLean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcom_McLean); [Wikipedia, Containerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containerization); [OWID adoption data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/technology-adoption-by-households-in-the-united-states)
- US air travel after 1978 deregulation: real yield fell from 12.3 to 7.9 cents per mile (1978-1997, 1982 dollars); real price of flying down 44.9% 1978-2011; passenger miles tripled 1978-2005; Braniff, TWA and Pan Am failed and every legacy carrier but Alaska has filed for bankruptcy since 2000. — [Wikipedia, Airline deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_deregulation)
- Aluminium: $34/oz in 1852 (dearer than gold); about $8/lb (1886); $2/lb (1889); $0.50/lb (1894). World output 3.6 t (1884), 6,800 t (1900), over 100,000 t (1916), over 1 million t (1941). Alcoa was the US monopolist into WWII. — [Wikipedia, History of aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_aluminium)
- Steel: Gilchrist-Thomas process cut cost 90% vs puddling; steel rails lasted over 10x longer than iron; labour per tonne fell 1,000x from 1920 to 2000 (3+ hours to 0.003). Bottle machine (1905): $1.80 to $0.10-0.12 per gross. Rail vs wagon freight in 1890: 0.875 vs 24.5 cents per ton-mile. — [Wikipedia, Productivity-improving technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity-improving_technologies)
- Nitrogen fertiliser: Haber-Bosch nitrogen feeds roughly half the world (44% in 2000, 48% in 2008; about 4 billion people). — [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-people-does-synthetic-fertilizer-feed)
- Household durables, US years from 10% to 50% to 80% of households: refrigerator 1931, 1942, 1950; radio 1925, 1931, 1939; colour TV 1966, 1974, 1981; microwave 1982, 1986, 1992; home air conditioning 1957, 1974, 2002; washing machine 1930, 1965, 2002; dishwasher 1964, 1998, not reached (69% in 2011); dryer 1960, 1992, 1998; cellphone 1994, 2002, 2009. — [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/technology-adoption-by-households-in-the-united-states)
- Liberty ships: 2,710 built 1941-45 in 18 yards at about $2 million each (about $46 million in 2025 dollars); first ships about 230 days, median 39 days by 1943; record 4 days 15.5 hours (a stunt). — [Wikipedia, Liberty ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_ship)
- Liberty labour: nearly 250 days and about 1.1 million labour-hours per ship in early 1942; under 50 days and about 0.5 million hours by end-1943 (best Kaiser yards 0.3 million). British yards were already at about 336,000 hours in 1942. Enablers: welding, 50-ton prefabricated blocks, one standard design, new government-funded yards, workforce from 168,000 to over 1.5 million. Yards were sold "for pennies on the dollar" after the war. — [Construction Physics, "How the US Built 5,000 Ships in WWII"](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-the-us-built-5000-ships-in-wwii)
- WWII aircraft: about 325,000 built; output value up about 70x from 1939 to 1944; B-17 labour from over 140,000 hours (1941) to under 20,000 (1944); average aircraft cost fell only 32%; nearly every type took more than a year to go from unit 5 to unit 500; the government owned about 90% of capacity by war's end; contractor profits were capped in single digits; US manufacturing productivity was lower at war's end than in 1941. — [Construction Physics, "How to Build 300,000 Airplanes in Five Years"](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-to-build-300000-airplanes-in)
- Willow Run: 6,972 B-24s of 18,482 total; peak 428 in April 1944, one every 63 minutes; 42,500 employees; a 1943 congressional committee criticised the plant as too much like a car line. Ford's plant "produced virtually nothing for its first two years". — [Wikipedia, Willow Run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow_Run); [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-to-build-300000-airplanes-in)
- Jevons: backfire (rebound over 100%) needs highly price-elastic demand; direct rebound is "usually small" in mature markets and micro-level rebound is "usually less than 100%"; evidence for widespread backfire is limited. — [Wikipedia, Jevons paradox (citing Sorrell 2009; Gillingham et al. 2013)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox)

### Inferences
- Who captured profit (pattern from the cases above): (a) the buyer or a downstream integrator when the product is a commodity input: solar, LEDs, TVs, airlines, lab diamonds; (b) one scale leader with a moving cost frontier and a brand or ecosystem: Ford for about 15 years, DJI, Alcoa (patents), Sea-Land early; (c) a vertically integrated user of its own cheap input: SpaceX took launch from $8,100-11,600/kg to $1,500-2,600/kg and then built the demand itself with Starlink.
- The launch case warns about benchmarks: Falcon is 44x cheaper than Shuttle but only about 3-4x cheaper than Saturn V or Atlas V. External launch demand did not grow 10x on a 3-4x cut; SpaceX's own constellation did.
- Diffusion has a floor set by complements, not price: air conditioning took 45 years to go from 10% to 80% of homes and washing machines 72 years. Products that needed no new household infrastructure (radio, colour TV, microwave) took 10-15 years.
- War production shows the ordering a writer should expect from "abundant production": time-to-build fell 80% in under two years; labour-hours fell 55% (ships) to 86% (B-17); unit cost fell 32% (aircraft). Speed gives first, cost gives last.

### Gaps
- 2025-26 solar module spot prices and manufacturer margins (BNEF, InfoLink) were not retrieved; the OWID series ends in 2024.
- Textile price and output series for the industrial revolution (Harley, Allen) were not retrieved; only productivity multiples (spinning jenny 8 threads, flying shuttle 2x) are in the fetched source.
- Jet-era (1958-1975) fare and traffic data were not retrieved.
- The airline traffic figure on Wikipedia ("250 million to 750 million passenger miles") has unclear units; treat only as a 3x ratio.
- Bernhofen et al. estimates of containerisation's trade effect were not retrieved.
- Per-satellite and per-launch internal SpaceX costs were not retrieved. The Starlink revenue figures come from Wikipedia and should be checked against filings.

## 3. Counter-cases: cheaper did not expand demand, or the innovator kept nothing

### Takeaway
Cheapness fails in four repeatable ways: the product carries a status penalty (Nano), the binding constraint is elsewhere (Concorde's overland ban, Segway's use case, nuclear's indirect and regulatory cost, housing's site and jurisdiction variety), the market is already saturated (TVs, smartphones), or the cost collapse is shared by every producer and competed away (solar, EVs, diamonds, airlines).

### Cited Findings
- Tata Nano: launched at ₹100,000 (about $2,000-2,500) in 2008 with 250,000 units a year of capacity; peak sales 74,527 (FY2011-12); 7,591 in FY2016-17; discontinued 2018. Cited causes: seen as a "poor man's" car, still dear next to a motorcycle, fires, zero-star crash result, plant relocation delays. — [Wikipedia, Tata Nano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Nano)
- Segway PT: $5,000 at launch; 140,000 units sold in its lifetime; ended 2020; about 1.5% of company profit at the end. — [Wikipedia, Segway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segway)
- Concorde: 20 built, 14 in airline service; more than 100 options (74 from 16 airlines) cancelled, mostly 1972-73; development cost over six times the estimate (£70 million planned, £1.5-2.1 billion by 1976); overland sonic boom bans limited routes; 15.8 passenger-miles per gallon vs 46.4 for a 747; retired 2003. — [Wikipedia, Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde)
- US nuclear: overnight cost about $1,000/kWe for late-1960s starts and about $9,000/kWe ten years later (2010 dollars); build time from just over 5 years to 12 by 1980; NRC regulatory guides from 21 (1971) to 143 (1978); indirect costs were 72% of the 1976-88 cost increase; about 75% of craft hours were lost or unproductive in a 1980 study; Vogtle about $8,000/kWe overnight. — [Construction Physics, "Why are nuclear power construction costs so high?"](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/why-are-nuclear-power-construction)
- NuScale: UAMPS project went from $58/MWh (2021) to $89/MWh after subsidy (Jan 2023), and from $3.6 billion for 720 MWe to $9.3 billion for 462 MWe; cancelled Nov 2023. — [Wikipedia, NuScale Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NuScale_Power)
- Construction: value added per hour improved 1.1x from 1947 to 2010, vs 8.6x in manufacturing and 16.1x in agriculture (McKinsey); a house took 85 labour-hours per 100 sq ft in 1962 and about 86.5 today; about 20,000 US permitting jurisdictions; about 1 million single-family homes a year vs 15 million cars; factory-built modules serve only about a day's drive; "substantial efficiency improvements have in general not materialized with factory-based construction". — [Potter, "Labor Productivity in Construction", Aspen Institute (2025)](https://www.aspeninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Potter_Construction_Productivity.pdf)
- Construction productivity has fallen or stagnated for decades and measurement error is not the sole cause; more productive states do not gain share. — [Goolsbee & Syverson, NBER WP 30845 (2023)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30845)
- Commodity trap, solar: module prices fell 51% in one year (2023 to 2024); LONGi announced a 30% workforce cut in 2024 citing overcapacity. — [OWID solar-pv-prices](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/solar-pv-prices); [Wikipedia, LONGi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LONGi)
- Commodity trap, EVs: about 500 Chinese EV makers in 2019, about 100 by 2023. — [Wikipedia, Electric vehicle industry in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_vehicle_industry_in_China)
- Saturation: US households with a TV were 96.6% in 1992 and 94% in 2018 while TV prices fell 94% (1997-2015); smartphone ownership has been flat at 91% for 2024-2026. — [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/technology-adoption-by-households-in-the-united-states); [BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2015/long-term-price-trends-for-computers-tvs-and-related-items.htm); [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/)
- Inelastic goods: steel -0.2 to -0.3; eggs -0.1; car fuel -0.09 short run and -0.31 long run; university education near 0. — [Wikipedia, Price elasticity of demand (sources listed there)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand)
- Baumol: across US industries 1948-2001, each extra point of productivity growth meant about one point of relative price decline and about 0.75 points more real output growth (Nordhaus 2008); services such as health, education and live arts got relatively dearer. Agriculture went from 21.5% of the workforce (1930) to 1.9% (2000). — [Wikipedia, Baumol effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baumol_effect)
- Model T abroad is itself a counter-case: same product, 20-130% higher price, and no mass market. — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)

### Inferences
- The Nordhaus relationship (0.75 points of output per point of productivity) is the base rate: on average, cost collapse grows unit volume less than proportionally. 10x expansions are the tail, and need elasticity well above 1 plus low starting penetration.
- "Cheaper version of what rich people have" works only when the cheap version is not socially marked (Model T for farm work) and fails when it is (Nano as "cheapest car").
- In nuclear and construction, cost sits in indirect labour, engineering, site coordination and compliance, so factory automation of hardware addresses the smaller share.

### Gaps
- OTC hearing aids (FDA rule 2022): no fetched source on uptake; the Wikipedia pages tried had no data.
- Memory (DRAM/NAND) margin cycles, steel overcapacity figures and LED maker margins were not retrieved.
- Salt and other fully inelastic staples: no elasticity source retrieved.

## 4. Screening framework and elasticity estimates

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a seven-test screen. The decisive tests are (1) own-price elasticity at the market level above about 1.5, (2) penetration under about 20% of plausible need, and (3) factory conversion cost, not land, permits, installation or status, is the largest cost share. Published elasticities for most big categories (housing, health care, energy, cars at market level) are below 1, so a 10x market needs a new buyer segment or new use, not a slide down the existing demand curve.

### Cited Findings
- Air travel fare elasticity (InterVISTAS for IATA, 2007): route level -1.4 (literature -1.2 to -1.5), national -0.8, pan-national -0.6; route range -0.84 to -1.96 by region; firm-specific -1.24 to -2.34; income elasticity above 1 (luxury good). Gasoline -0.2 short run, -0.7 long run. — [IATA / InterVISTAS, "Estimating Air Travel Demand Elasticities"](https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/publications/economic-reports/estimating-air-travel-demand-elasticities---by-intervistas/)
- Airline travel by segment: first class -0.3, discount -0.9, pleasure travellers -1.5. Ford compact car (single model) -2.8. Coca-Cola -3.8 vs soft drinks overall -0.8 to -1.0. Mobile telecom -0.405; broadband -0.434; cinema -0.87; bus travel -0.20; medical insurance -0.31; paediatric visits -0.03 to -0.06; spirits -1.5. — [Wikipedia, Price elasticity of demand (Pindyck & Rubinfeld; Samuelson & Nordhaus; Perloff; others)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand)
- Housing demand is "income and price inelastic" (both below 1). — [Albouy, Ehrlich & Liu, NBER WP 22816](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22816)
- Health care (RAND Health Insurance Experiment): 25% coinsurance cut spending 20% vs free care; 95% coinsurance cut it about 30%; hospitalisations 20% lower with cost sharing. — [RAND Research Brief RB9174](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9174.html)
- Car ownership income elasticity moved from above 1 (1909) to below 1 (1929). — [NBER WP 31454](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31454)
- Backfire-scale demand response requires elasticity above 1. — [Wikipedia, Jevons paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox)
- Productivity to output pass-through averages about 0.75. — [Wikipedia, Baumol effect (Nordhaus 2008)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baumol_effect)

### Inferences
- Aggregation rule (from the IATA and soft-drink data): elasticity roughly halves at each step up from firm (-1.2 to -3.8) to route or segment (-1.4) to whole market (-0.6 to -0.8). A founder's own-product elasticity overstates market expansion; most early volume is share taken from rivals.
- Constant-elasticity arithmetic (own calculation): quantity multiple = (price ratio)^elasticity. A 3x price cut gives 2.1x volume at -0.7, 3x at -1.0, 5.2x at -1.5, 9x at -2.0. A 10x price cut gives 5x at -0.7, 10x at -1.0, 32x at -1.5. So at typical market elasticities (-0.6 to -1.0) even a 10x cost cut grows units 4-10x and shrinks or holds revenue. Revenue grows only when elasticity exceeds 1.
- Proposed screen, with the case evidence behind each test:
  1. Market-level elasticity above 1.5, or a clear new-buyer segment. Evidence: Model T rural quartile growth 2,050%; leisure air travel -1.5.
  2. Penetration under 20% of households or addressable users. Evidence: cars 1% (1908); solar, sequencing, satellites near zero. Fails: TVs, phones at 91-97%.
  3. Price at least 5-10x above the materials floor, with conversion (labour, tooling, overhead, margin) the largest share. See section 5.
  4. Production, not site, land, permit or installation labour, is the binding constraint. Fails: housing (20,000 jurisdictions, half of cost on-site labour), nuclear (72% of cost growth indirect).
  5. Complements exist or can be bundled: finance, infrastructure, distribution, service. Evidence: GMAC 1919, road acts 1916 and 1921; air conditioning's 45-year diffusion.
  6. Demand is not positional. Evidence: Nano; the 1914 Ford quote on social standing; natural vs lab diamonds.
  7. Buyer can adopt without multi-year qualification. See section 7.
- Profit-capture screen, separate from market-size screen: favour cases where the entrant also owns the demand (Starlink), holds a fast-moving cost frontier in an integrated product (DJI, Ford 1913-23), or sells the complement rather than the commodity.

### Gaps
- No fetched meta-analysis for electricity elasticity (the NREL host was blocked in this session) or for market-level new-car demand; the usual textbook values (electricity about -0.1 to -0.4 short run; new cars near -1 at market level) are unverified here.
- Numerical housing elasticities from Albouy et al. were not on the abstract page.
- No direct elasticity estimates for durable goods as a class were retrieved.

## 6. Wright's law and learning rates by technology

### Takeaway
Learning rates cluster at 15-25% per doubling for small, modular, standardised, factory-made units (solar modules, batteries, the Model T, airframes within one design) and near zero or negative for large, site-built, customised, regulated systems (nuclear, coal plants, buildings). The rate is a property of the product architecture and of continuity of production, and it resets when the design, team or site changes.

### Cited Findings
- Wright (1936): each doubling of cumulative airframes cut labour-hours 20% (80% curve). Typical progress ratios run 60-90%. Model T 85%. — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/where-are-my-damn-learning-curves)
- Why construction shows no learning: every project resets team, owner, layout and site; process or style changes reset the curve; variable inputs (food processing, site concrete) show no learning; little fixed cost to spread. — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/where-are-my-damn-learning-curves)
- Solar modules 20.2%; solar electricity 36%; onshore wind 23%; offshore wind 10%; batteries about 19%; coal and gas none; nuclear negative in the US and UK, flat in France and South Korea where designs were standardised. — [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/cheap-renewables-growth); [OWID batteries](https://ourworldindata.org/battery-price-decline)
- Across 62 technologies, Wright's law gives the best forecasts with Moore's law close behind; forecast error grows at about 2.5% a year of horizon. — [Nagy, Farmer, Bui & Trancik (2013), PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0052669)
- Way, Ives, Mealy & Farmer: probabilistic forecasts tested on over 50 technologies; a fast transition based on solar, wind, batteries and electrolysers saves "many trillions of dollars"; a nuclear-driven path is "far more expensive". — [INET Oxford working paper page](https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/no-2021-01-empirically-grounded-technology-forecasts-and-the-energy-transition)
- Malhotra & Schmidt (Joule, 2020) sort technologies by design complexity and need for customisation: Type 1 learns fast and Type 3 slow; a simple device with a complex manufacturing process (a solar panel) can still learn fast. — [Secondary summary, "learning about learning"](https://uptownfrunk.substack.com/p/learning-about-learning); paper at [Joule](https://www.cell.com/joule/fulltext/S2542-4351(20)30440-2) (403, not opened)
- WWII: most aircraft types took over a year to get from unit 5 to unit 500; Liberty ships fell from 1.1 million to 0.5 million labour-hours in under two years. — [Construction Physics, airplanes](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-to-build-300000-airplanes-in); [ships](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-the-us-built-5000-ships-in-wwii)
- Haitz's law for LEDs: 10x per decade in cost per lumen. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitz%27s_law)

### Inferences
- For a screen: expect 15-25% per doubling only if the product is (a) made in thousands to millions of identical units, (b) small enough to ship, (c) designed once and frozen, and (d) free of per-site or per-customer engineering. Each violation pushes the rate toward zero.
- AI could plausibly change (d), by making per-site engineering and compliance work cheap, more than it changes the physical learning rate. That is the main lever for the low-learning classes.
- Doublings are what matter. A new product at tiny cumulative volume gets many doublings quickly (the Model T had about 7.5 doublings of annual output in 14 years); a mature product like cars gets one doubling in decades.

### Gaps
- The Malhotra & Schmidt cell assignments and learning rates by technology could not be verified from the paper. My recollection (unverified): Type 1 = standardised, simple (PV modules, LEDs); Type 2 = platform or mass-customised (wind turbines, rooftop PV, building retrofits); Type 3 = complex and customised (nuclear, CCS, biomass plants).
- Way et al.'s headline numbers (about 10% a year cost decline for solar, wind, batteries; about $12 trillion saving) were not on the page fetched; verify in the Joule paper (2022).
- The Nagy et al. per-technology table (Santa Fe Performance Curve Database) was not retrieved.

## 8. What "abundant production" can plausibly mean by 2030-2035

### Takeaway
The evidence supports large gains in speed and flexibility for products that are already factory-made, and much weaker evidence for 10-100x unit-cost cuts. Lights-out plants exist for high-volume fixed designs; humanoids are in the hundreds of units with repeated missed targets; and the best historical analogue (WWII) shows a 7x cut in labour-hours delivering only a 32% cut in unit cost.

### Cited Findings
- Lights-out today: FANUC has run one since 2001 (about 50 robots per 24 hours, up to 30 days unsupervised); Xiaomi's Changping plant has 11 automated lines and capacity of about 10 million phones a year; ASE runs 56 lights-out packaging and test factories; Philips' razor plant has 128 robots and 9 quality staff. "Few facilities run exclusively lights-out production." — [Wikipedia, Lights out (manufacturing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lights_out_(manufacturing))
- Limits: robots in such plants do highly repetitive motions and need people to reprogram for any change; most of an industrial robot's cost is installation and set-up; at least 26 safety standards govern robots near people; humanoids are likely "transition labor" comparable to swapping steam engines for electric motors before factories were redesigned. — [Foundation for American Innovation, "The Manufacturing of the Future Won't Be Human-Shaped"](https://policygradients.thefai.org/p/the-manufacturing-of-the-future-wont)
- Humanoids, Figure: over 350 Figure 03 units delivered by April 2026; about 740 operating by end-June 2026; plant output of one robot per hour by April 2026 against a 12,000 a year plan; 98.5% of human performance in a 10-hour sorting contest; valuation $39 billion (Sep 2025); Fortune questioned its BMW claims. — [Wikipedia, Figure AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure_AI)
- Humanoids, Tesla Optimus: "production-ready by 2023" and "1,000+ units in 2025" were not confirmed; production start slipped to about July-August 2026; target cost $20,000-30,000; lines designed for 1 million a year (Fremont) and 10 million (Texas, from 2027) are company statements; demos were criticised for teleoperation. — [Wikipedia, Optimus (robot)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimus_(robot))
- Bloomberg Intelligence: humanoids could cut final-assembly labour cost by about 4% at first, with about 90% of jobs still done by people and conventional robots in its scenario (search summary, page not opened). — [Bloomberg Professional](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/?p=130919)
- Automakers testing humanoids (BMW, Hyundai, Mercedes-Benz, Tesla) find them slower than people, and experts say simpler automation is often cheaper. — [Business Report, citing the New York Times, 11 Aug 2026](https://www.businessreport.com/?p=275976)
- Tesla "unboxed" process: claimed to halve line length and, per Musk, be "more than 5 times faster"; Cybercab formal production began April 2026; 319 registered in Texas by 10 Oct 2026. — [Wikipedia, Tesla Cybercab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Cybercab)
- Historical bound on labour automation: B-17 labour-hours fell 7x while average aircraft cost fell 32%; nearly every type needed over a year to get from unit 5 to unit 500; Willow Run made almost nothing for two years; a B-29 needed about 900 design changes before volume production. — [Construction Physics](https://www.construction-physics.com/p/how-to-build-300000-airplanes-in)
- Where labour is a large share: about half of home construction cost; building materials are already near their floor. — [Potter, Aspen Institute (2025)](https://www.aspeninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Potter_Construction_Productivity.pdf)
- US manufacturing: record $2.913 trillion output in 2024, 12.76 million workers, about 11% of GDP; output up over 30% since 2009 on 5% more workers. — [Wikipedia, Manufacturing in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_in_the_United_States)
- Productivity to price pass-through is about one-for-one and to output about 0.75 (Nordhaus). — [Wikipedia, Baumol effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baumol_effect)

### Inferences
- Bounding arithmetic (own calculation): if direct labour is a share s of unit cost and automation removes all of it, cost falls by s. At s = 15%, the ceiling is a 1.18x cost improvement; at 50% (site construction), 2x. Getting to 3-10x needs labour removed along the whole supply chain (where upstream labour is embedded in "materials"), plus cheaper capital and energy, plus design changes. 100x unit-cost cuts have only come from physics-level learning over decades (solar, sequencing, LEDs).
- What can move 10x or more by 2030-35 is time: design-to-first-unit, tooling and changeover, and lot-size-one customisation, because those are engineering and set-up labour, the area the FAI piece identifies as the main robot cost. The WWII record (80% cut in build time in two years) is the supporting precedent.
- So "Model T-style" screens should weight (a) products whose price is dominated by low-volume engineering, tooling and qualification (aircraft-like $/kg at car-like complexity), and (b) products where speed itself is the product (lead times of months to years), over products whose price is already near materials and energy.
- Current humanoid counts (hundreds) against stated plans (millions) imply at least 10 doublings to reach scale; at historical rates that is the early 2030s at best. A plan that depends on general-purpose humanoid labour before 2030 is exposed to the same slippage seen in 2022-2026.

### Gaps
- No sourced figure for labour's share of manufactured-goods cost was retrieved; the brief's "10-20%" is unverified here. Census Annual Survey of Manufactures (payroll over value of shipments) or BEA KLEMS would settle it.
- BCG Henderson Institute's "Can AI deliver fully automated factories?" returned 403; McKinsey Global Institute and IFR robot-density data were not retrieved.
- No audited data on cost or throughput gains from AI CAD/CAM tools, from Tesla's unboxed process, or from Chinese "dark factory" programmes beyond the named plants.
- No credible quantified forecast of 2030-35 manufacturing cost under AI plus robotics was retrieved; the bounds above are my arithmetic, not a published estimate.
