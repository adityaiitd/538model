# Defense hardware, shipbuilding and heavy transport equipment as a target for 10-100x faster/cheaper production (as of 24 Nov 2026)

Research-process note for the report writer: the shared web-search budget ran out after three search rounds, so later evidence came from direct page fetches. Several primary sites (CSIS, CBO web pages, Cato, Seatrade, USNI, Inside Unmanned Systems) returned HTTP 403 and are cited from search-result summaries only; those items are marked "(search summary, page not opened)". ## 1. Market size today ($ and units/year)

### Takeaway
The US munitions budget roughly tripled on paper in one year (about $24B in FY2026 to a $70.5B FY2027 request), but 55-82% of the FY2027 increase sits in a reconciliation bill that had not passed as of September 2026. Commercial shipbuilding is a roughly 200m CGT orderbook that is 64-65% Chinese and under 1% American, while Ukraine alone makes more than 4 million drones a year against a US program buying tens of thousands.

### Cited Findings

**US defense topline and procurement**
- FY2026 enacted DoD funding: $1.01 trillion. FY2027 request: $1.45 trillion ($1.1T discretionary + $350.0B mandatory via reconciliation), a 44% increase. Procurement $413.1B and RDT&E $343.7B requested (both including mandatory). — [CRS R49023, updated 3 Sep 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49023.html)
- FY2027 request by category (total, discretionary + mandatory): missiles and munitions $95.0B; missile defense $82.2B; shipbuilding $87.2B; aircraft $102.2B; hypersonics $37.9B; Defense Autonomous Warfare Group (DAWG) $53.6B mandatory + $1.0B discretionary; Collaborative Combat Aircraft $2.58B. — [CRS R49023](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49023.html)
- Munitions procurement on a narrower definition: just under $20B in FY2025, $24.4B in FY2026, about $70.5B requested for FY2027 (Army $36.6B, Navy $22.6B, Air Force $11.3B), a 188% increase; about $40B (roughly 55%) depends on reconciliation. — [Breaking Defense, Apr 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/04/the-pentagon-wants-a-188-percent-bump-for-missile-procurement-can-industry-deliver/)
- CNAS 10-program munitions portfolio (constant FY27 dollars): FY2016-26 enacted average $5.8B/year; FY2027 request $49.5B, of which 82% ($40.5B) is in reconciliation; FY2028-31 plan averages $26.7B/year, a 46% drop from FY2027. — [CNAS, 7 Oct 2026](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Sources conflict on scope: $70.5B (Breaking Defense), $95.0B "missiles and munitions" (CRS), $49.5B for ten programs (CNAS), $47.0B munitions in the mandatory request and $31.8B "to expand production" (DoD documents, search summary). These measure different slices and should not be added. — [CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49023.html); [DoW mandatory funding overview (search summary, page not opened)](https://comptroller.war.gov/Portals/45/Documents/defbudget/FY2027/DoW_FY2027_Mandatory_Funding_Overview.pdf)

### Inferences
- The best US producer of small attack drones (Neros, about 1,200/week, roughly 62,000/year, see section 7) makes about 1.5% of Ukraine's 2025 output. One Euromaidan headline puts Ukraine's 2026 target at "70 times more than the US", implying US output near 100,000/year; that is a headline claim, not a measured figure.
- The defense "market" a founder can address is much smaller than the request: the stable base is the discretionary portion (about $9B of the CNAS munitions portfolio, versus $49.5B requested), and the plan itself drops 46% after FY2027.
- Shipbuilding dwarfs munitions in tonnage and capital but the US-addressable commercial piece is close to zero today; the US market is the Navy ($47B-87B/year) plus whatever policy creates.

## 2. Lead times, backlogs and unit costs

### Takeaway
Exquisite munitions take 3-5 years from budget request to first delivery and the Iran war burned through half or more of key interceptor stocks, so replenishment runs to 2029-2030 even with tripled budgets. Navy combatants now take 9-11 years to build (5-8 in the 2000s), and commercial slots at Korean and Chinese yards are sold out for about 3-3.5 years.

### Cited Findings

**Munitions stock gaps and replenishment**
- CSIS estimates Patriot interceptors fell from about 2,330 pre-war to 1,030 at the April 2026 ceasefire and 759-827 after later fighting (at least 65% down); THAAD from 452 to 234-278 (at least 38% down); more than 1,000 Tomahawks fired. Rebuilding takes at least three years; Tomahawk could take until late 2030. The Pentagon has not confirmed these estimates. — [Military Times, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/07/30/iran-war-depleted-us-patriot-missile-stockpiles-creating-readiness-challenges-experts-say/); [PBS/AP](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/world/u-s-will-need-years-to-replenish-stockpiles-of-advanced-weapons-used-in-iran-war-new-analysis-finds); [CSIS (page not opened)](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project)
- CNAS: as of April 2026, 53-81% of THAAD and 45-61% of PAC-3 expended, at least 32% of Tomahawks, 25% of available stealthy JASSMs; the Air Force used at least 1,000 JASSMs by July 2026. Supplemental request for the Iran war: $67B, including $21B for munitions. — [CNAS, Oct 2026](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Request-to-first-delivery pipelines run 36-64 months; most munitions show three- to four-year lead times, unchanged for 6 of 10 programs; FY2027 delivery timelines actually lengthened versus pre-ramp baselines (PAC-3 MSE +2 months, SM-6 and Tomahawk +4 months, AMRAAM nearly a year). — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Production rates versus orders: PAC-3 MSE about 550/year now against a 3,203 request (CNAS questions reaching 2,000); THAAD production books cap at 96/year against an 857 order and a 400/year target; SM-6 at 96/year with a stated maximum of 239-360; Tomahawk target 800/year. — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)

**Solid rocket motors**
- L3Harris was the sole PAC-3 motor producer; on 3 Aug 2026 the Pentagon signed framework deals with Northrop Grumman worth over $3B ($2B Patriot motors as a second source, $1B THAAD components) over seven years; L3Harris says it will "nearly triple" PAC-3 propulsion output but its seven-year deal was not finalized. About a dozen such framework agreements were signed in 2026. — [Breaking Defense, Aug 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/08/northrop-gets-3b-framework-deal-to-increase-motor-production-for-pac-3-thaad/); [Breaking Defense, Jul 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/pentagon-inks-deal-with-l3harris-to-ramp-rocket-motor-production/); [Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/news/pentagon-congress/2026/08/03/pentagon-moves-to-triple-patriot-quadruple-thaad-interceptor-production/)
- Analysts call solid rocket motors and seekers "significant bottlenecks" and doubt any dollar figure overcomes them in one year. — [Breaking Defense, Apr 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/04/the-pentagon-wants-a-188-percent-bump-for-missile-procurement-can-industry-deliver/)

**155mm shells**
- Output rose from 14,000 to 36,000 rounds/month by March 2026 against a 100,000/month goal due October 2025; with current metal-parts plants the ceiling is 71,000/month. The Army spent $4.5B on 155mm production from Feb 2022 to Sep 2025. — [Breaking Defense, Jul 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/); [DoW Inspector General DOWIG-2026-095](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/2026-07/DOWIG-2026-095%20155-mm_Redacted_Secure.pdf); [Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/news/your-military/2026/07/14/manufacturing-woes-hamper-us-155-mm-ammo-production/)
- The $469M General Dynamics plant at Mesquite, Texas, meant to make 30,000 metal parts/month with an "unproven production process", had produced zero as of March 2026; Scranton was at about 15,000 against a 35,000 goal. — [Breaking Defense, Jul 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/); [TWZ](https://www.twz.com/land/new-500m-artillery-shell-plant-failed-to-produce-any-155mm-parts)

**Naval ships**
- Destroyers and submarines took 5-6 years to build in the 2000s and now take 9-10; carriers 8 versus 11 years; amphibious assault ships 5 versus 9. — [CBO testimony (Eric Labs), 22 Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Delays versus contract: CVN-80 18-26 months; first Columbia 12-16 months; Virginia class 24-36 months (average four years late versus original dates); first Constellation frigate 36 months; DDG-51 delays grew 25 months in three years. Delays increased slightly from 2025 to 2026 "despite substantial investments". — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Attack submarine output averaged 1.1/year over the past two years against purchases of 2.0/year; 33% of the 48-boat SSN fleet is in or awaiting maintenance (goal 20%). If yards still built at historical speed the fleet would exceed 300 ships; the 2026-2030 shortfall is about 20 ships. — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Real price growth per ship: DDG-51 +16% (FY2024-25), Constellation frigate +29% (FY2023-26), John Lewis oiler +21% (FY2020-24), Polar Security Cutter +55% (FY2021-25); more than $10B budgeted for cost-to-complete. — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- GAO: more than $10B invested in the submarine industrial base without knowing how much more is needed; Constellation frigate options worth over $3B were exercised before the Navy walked away in 2025; 92 GAO recommendations to the Navy since 2016, many open. — [GAO-26-109068, 22 Apr 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109068)

**Commercial yards**
- Korean big-three backlog covers about 3-3.5 years; HD Hyundai Heavy's backlog was $60.68B at end-July 2026; Hanwha Ocean's merchant backlog covers about three years. Some Chinese yard orderbooks extend beyond 2029. — [Hellenic Shipping News (search summary)](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/?p=1118298); [Money Today, 29 Jul 2026 (search summary)](https://www.mt.co.kr/en/industry/2026/07/29/2026072914183658621); [Riviera](https://www.rivieramm.com/news-content-hub/not-just-scale-explaining-chinas-staggering-shipbuilding-dominance-89822)
- A recent Jones Act ship (Janet Marie) took about 50 months from first steel to delivery. — [Cato (search summary, page not opened)](https://www.cato.org/blog/new-jones-act-ship-offers-cautionary-tale-shipbuilding-protectionism)

### Inferences
- The price ladder now spans three orders of magnitude for roughly the same job (hit a fixed target a few hundred km away): $5M Tomahawk, $350-450k FAMM, $30-50k Shahed-class. The 10x step (Tomahawk to FAMM) is already contracted; the next 10x (FAMM to Shahed-class) is the open one.
- Interceptor economics are inverted: a $4.3M PAC-3 against a $30-50k drone is roughly 100:1 against the defender; a $15k Merops is the first US purchase at better than 1:1.
- Lead time, not just price, is the binding constraint. Three- to four-year lead times held steady through a tripling of the budget, which suggests the limit sits in sub-tier supply (motors, seekers, metal parts), where money takes years to become capacity.

## 3. Cost breakdown and the US vs China vs Korea gap

### Takeaway
US commercial ships cost roughly 4-5x Asian prices on recent quotes (2x on older academic estimates) and US Navy combatants take about twice as long as they did 20 years ago. No source decomposes the gap quantitatively, but the evidence points to low volume, workforce inexperience, sole-source suppliers and design churn more than to wage rates alone.

### Cited Findings
- A Philadelphia-built tanker can exceed $220M versus about $47M in China or Korea; building in the US costs "four or five times" as much (shipyard executives and shipowners). — [WSJ via To Vima, 13 Oct 2025](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/south-korea-promises-it-will-rekindle-u-s-shipbuilding-thats-still-far-off/)
- CRS-cited figure: allied (Japanese, Korean) production costs roughly a quarter of domestic; building about 20 large LNG carriers in the US would cost roughly $15B more than buying Korean. — [War on the Rocks, Nov 2025](https://warontherocks.com/2025/11/america-should-build-its-own-warships-while-buying-tankers/)
- Counter-estimate: an academic comparison puts the US premium at roughly 2x; the 1936 subsidy regime also assumed 2x. — [Brian Potter on Noahpinion, Sep 2024](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/why-cant-the-us-build-ships)
- Shipyard labor is about 40% of a military ship's procurement cost. — [CRS RL32665](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32665.html)
- Potter's causal account: persistently higher US labor and steel input costs since the 19th century, plus a protected industry with no pressure to compete (cabotage since 1817, subsidies with strings, union resistance to new methods, abandonment of large-block construction after WWII). US productivity rose about 30% from the early 1990s to early 2000s but foreign yards improved as fast or faster. — [Noahpinion](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/why-cant-the-us-build-ships)
- WWII counter-example: Liberty ship labor fell from 1.1 million to 486,000 man-hours and average build time reached 50 days when one design was built in volume. — [Noahpinion](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/why-cant-the-us-build-ships)
- China's edge as described by BRS: dense supplier ecosystem, parallel block/module construction, cumulative learning from long series of similar ships, state-backed finance, and growing robotics/machine-vision use. No labor or steel figures given. — [Riviera, 2026](https://www.rivieramm.com/news-content-hub/not-just-scale-explaining-chinas-staggering-shipbuilding-dominance-89822)
- Korean yards earn high margins at those low prices: HD Hyundai Heavy 18.4% operating margin in Q2 2026, Hanwha Ocean 13.5%; Hanwha says it will not cut prices to fill backlog. — [Money Today / Herald Corp (search summaries)](https://www.mt.co.kr/en/industry/2026/07/29/2026072914183658621); [Quartr HD Hyundai Q2 2026](https://quartr.com/events/hd-hyundai-heavy-industries-co-ltd-329180-q2-2026_F4qDTkF4)
- Navy-side causes per CBO: workforce challenges "probably loom the largest" (hiring, retention, a retiring generation), incomplete designs, Navy-driven changes after construction starts, and about 70% of critical submarine component suppliers having no competitor. Naval tonnage under construction rose 80% from 2014 to 2024 (68,000 to 123,000 tons). — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Wages are moving: Ingalls signed a contract raising pay 18% immediately and roughly 40-45% over five years; voluntary attrition is under 10%/year at Bath and Electric Boat and about 15% at Ingalls. — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Jones Act: requires US-built, -owned, -flagged and -crewed ships for domestic trade; only about 150 US commercial vehicle carriers, tankers and containerships exist and much of the fleet is old. — [WSJ via To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/south-korea-promises-it-will-rekindle-u-s-shipbuilding-thats-still-far-off/)
- Munitions: contracts in the new low-cost programs are firm-fixed-price with minimum quantity floors, explicitly "not on a cost-plus basis". — [DefenseScoop, 15 May 2026](https://defensescoop.com/2026/05/15/defense-department-low-cost-containerized-missile-contracts/); [TWZ](https://www.twz.com/air/usaf-plans-to-buy-28000-low-cost-cruise-missiles-in-five-years-advance-with-new-deals)

### Inferences
- Wages cannot explain a 4-5x gap when labor is around 40% of cost and Korean wages are not one-tenth of US wages; Korea's 13-18% margins at a quarter of the US price imply far fewer labor hours per ship and cheaper materials through scale purchasing. The gap is mostly series production, supplier density and learning, which is exactly what a "factory, not craft yard" entrant would attack.
- The Jones Act guarantees a tiny captive market (about 150 large ships) at 4-5x prices. That is a wedge for a new low-cost US yard (customers already pay $220M for a $47M ship) but too small to build scale on.
- For Navy ships, design instability and sole-source components are procurement-made problems that a faster factory cannot fix alone.

### Gaps
- No source splits ship cost into materials, labor, overhead and compliance for US versus Asian yards. No comparable breakdown for missiles either.
- Cato's price series (blocked) and Chinese labor-hour-per-CGT data were not retrieved.
- A widely repeated estimate that a Jones Act LNG carrier would cost about $1B (more than $700M above foreign price) appeared only in a search summary of Cato/Drewry coverage; not verified.

## 4. Latent demand: what would buyers take at 5-10x lower cost?

### Takeaway
The clearest proof of elastic demand is in expendables: when drones cost hundreds to thousands of dollars Ukraine and Russia each buy millions a year, and the Pentagon has now committed to tens of thousands of sub-$500k cruise missiles where it used to buy 20-55 Tomahawks. For ships the latent demand is real for the Navy (it buys more than yards can deliver) but for commercial owners the evidence is that they will keep buying Asian unless forced.

### Cited Findings
- Ukraine's drone output rose roughly 5x in two years (800k in 2023 to 4M+ in 2025) and contracting hit about $8B in H1 2026. — [Aviation Week (search summary)](https://aviationweek.com/defense/supply-chain/ukraine-eyes-drone-production-topping-7-million-units); [Euromaidan Press, Oct 2026](https://euromaidanpress.com/2026/10/11/ukraine-ordered-54-more-fpv-attack-drones-this-year-than-in-all-of-2025-and-deliveries-jumped-almost-fourfold/)
- Demand there is funding-limited, not appetite-limited: a presidential adviser said FPV capacity exceeded 5 million/year while actual output was about 2 million because of money; a deputy minister said 20 million is reachable if allies fund it. — [Kyiv Independent via Yahoo (search summary)](https://www.yahoo.com/news/ukraine-produce-5-million-fpv-154406354.html); [United24](https://united24media.com/war-in-ukraine/ukraine-targets-annual-production-of-20-million-drones-under-new-eu-partnership-20805)
- Low-cost cruise missiles: the Army-led LCCM program aims for at least 10,000 within three years (Anduril, CoAspire, Leidos, Zone 5; Anduril 1,000/year capacity, Leidos an initial 3,000); the Air Force's FAMM plans nearly 28,000 over five years under seven-year frameworks with Anduril, CoAspire and Zone 5. — [DefenseScoop, May 2026](https://defensescoop.com/2026/05/15/defense-department-low-cost-containerized-missile-contracts/); [TWZ, Jul 2026](https://www.twz.com/air/usaf-plans-to-buy-28000-low-cost-cruise-missiles-in-five-years-advance-with-new-deals)
- Castelion's Blackbeard low-cost hypersonic: at least 500/year for two years after validation; the Pentagon is seeking authority to buy over 12,000 in five years. — [DefenseScoop, May 2026](https://defensescoop.com/2026/05/15/defense-department-low-cost-containerized-missile-contracts/)
- Counter-drone: Army bought 13,000 Merops interceptors at about $15,000; JIATF-401 gave Perennial Autonomy a $500M three-year ceiling contract (May 2026) and announced $7B of C-UAS IDIQ awards (ceilings, not spending). — [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/05/19/pentagon-awards-500-million-contract-to-perennial-autonomy-for-counter-drone-systems/); [AFCEA Signal (search summary)](https://www.afcea.org/signal-media/defense-operations/1-year-old-jiatf-401-awards-7-billion-counter-drone-contract)
- Unmanned vessels: the Navy ordered 30 medium USVs (10 each from HII, Galliano Marine and Saronic) at about $40M per hull in Sep 2026; the fleet goal includes 134 large unmanned vehicles. — [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/u-s-navy-takes-first-big-unmanned-step-buying-30-production-musv-hulls); [CRS RL32665](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32665.html)
- Navy demand exceeds supply: it has bought Virginia-class boats at 2.0/year for a decade and received 1.1/year. — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Policy-created ship demand: the Maritime Action Plan (13 Feb 2026) proposes a universal fee on foreign-built vessels calling at US ports (charged on arriving tonnage; the plan estimates a 1-cent rate raises about $66B over 10 years and a 25-cent rate close to $1.5T) feeding a Maritime Security Trust Fund, plus a Strategic Commercial Fleet. — [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/white-house-releases-america-s-maritime-action-plan); [Waterways Journal](https://www.waterwaysjournal.net/2026/02/20/white-house-releases-long-awaited-maritime-action-plan/)
- SHIPS for America Act: not enacted as of the latest coverage found; most of it passed the House on 10 Jul 2026 as an FY2027 NDAA amendment, with revenue provisions split off. — [Mondaq (search summary)](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/unitedstates/marine-shipping/1732526/progress-of-the-ships-for-america-act)
- Korea: Hanwha bought Philly Shipyard for $100M in 2024, pledged $5B and 12 ships (Aug 2025); the two LNG carriers will be built almost entirely in Korea and the 10 tankers have no outside customers yet. — [WSJ via To Vima, Oct 2025](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/south-korea-promises-it-will-rekindle-u-s-shipbuilding-thats-still-far-off/)
- Decarbonization pull exists but is captured by China: Chinese yards hold 1,082 dual-fuel or battery ships on order (738 LNG, 178 methanol, 19 ammonia dual-fuel). — [Riviera](https://www.rivieramm.com/news-content-hub/not-just-scale-explaining-chinas-staggering-shipbuilding-dominance-89822)

**Counter-evidence**
- Shipowners are not waiting for a US option: MSC has 128 ships on order, all at Chinese yards. — [Maritime Executive, Jul 2026](https://maritime-executive.com/article/chinese-shipyards-double-their-new-order-volume-in-first-half-of-2026)
- War on the Rocks argues the US should buy tankers from allies and build only warships; it calls the proposed rule requiring 15% of US LNG exports on US-built ships by 2047 costly with minimal security value. — [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/11/america-should-build-its-own-warships-while-buying-tankers/)
- Even at $5,000 the US struggled to absorb supply: 40% of Gauntlet I drones were late, held up by NDAA-compliant motors, batteries, sensors and chips. — [DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/09/30/neros-gauntlet-ii-100-million-order-800-bonus-drones/); [Inside Unmanned Systems (search summary)](https://insideunmannedsystems.com/component-shortages-slow-fpv-deliveries-from-drone-dominance-gauntlet-1-winners/)

### Inferences
- Tomahawk went from 20-55/year to a single-year request of 785, and the low-cost tier adds roughly 7,000-9,000 missiles/year across LCCM and FAMM. That is the Model T pattern in this sector: a 10x price cut produced a more than 100x unit increase in planned buys. Whether it holds depends on appropriations (section 5).
- Demand elasticity ranks: expendable drones and interceptors (proven, millions of units) > cheap cruise missiles and rocket motors (contracted frameworks, tens of thousands) > unmanned vessels (tens to low hundreds) > crewed warships (supply-capped) > US-built commercial ships (policy-dependent, owners resist).
- The components under the drones (motors, batteries, magnets, flight controllers) show the least-served demand: orders exist and vendors cannot fill them with compliant parts.

### Gaps
- No verified text or status for MASGA's $150B pledge; the WSJ piece predates most of it and my MASGA search did not run.
- No UNCTAD/Clarksons fleet-age or replacement-demand figures retrieved.
- No evidence gathered on what price would move commercial owners to a US yard; none of the sources quote owners on a threshold.

## 5. Regulatory and timeline gates

### Takeaway
For exquisite munitions the delay is mainly physical production (about 72% of the pipeline is award-to-delivery), but for everything new the gate is money arriving on time: on-time defense appropriations have happened twice since 2008, FY2027 again started under a continuing resolution, and four-fifths of the munitions surge depends on a reconciliation bill the House drafted at 83% below the request. For ships, statute blocks the obvious shortcut of building hulls abroad.

### Cited Findings
- Award-to-first-delivery is a median 72.5% of the 36-64 month request-to-delivery pipeline; worst contracting lag was 11 months (FY24 LRASM). CNAS concludes production, not contracting, is the main bottleneck for the ten legacy programs. — [CNAS, Oct 2026](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Since 2008 a defense appropriations bill passed before the fiscal year began only twice. — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- FY2027: no NDAA or defense appropriations enacted as of 3 Sep 2026; a continuing resolution runs through 11 Dec 2026 with no new-start money; General Atomics is self-funding CCA production while it waits. — [CRS R49023](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49023.html); [Air & Space Forces Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/cca-builders-rev-production-await-2027-budget/)
- The $350B reconciliation request faced a House draft of $60B (83% less). Framework agreements are nonbinding and some cannot be formalized until Congress passes the FY27 budget. — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- FY2026 NDAA (signed 18 Dec 2025) reforms: Portfolio Acquisition Executives made statutory (Sec. 1802); commercial buying becomes the procedural default (Sec. 1822); wider exemptions from cost accounting standards for nontraditional contractors (Sec. 1826); multiyear procurement eligibility widened to munitions (Sec. 804); second-source qualification prioritized (Sec. 832); defense-unique rules no longer flow down automatically to subcontractors (Sec. 1824). — [War on the Rocks industry guide, Dec 2025](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/)
- What was dropped or weakened: automatic "commercial" status for nontraditionals, the broader nontraditional definition, right-to-repair, most statutory repeals; the JROC was narrowed, not abolished. — [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/)
- Fast lanes that worked in 2026: Drone Dominance placed orders within days of a two-week fly-off and reallocates orders from vendors that miss deadlines; the FY2026 NDAA authorized five-year FAMM production contracts and DoD seeks seven. — [Breaking Defense, Mar 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/03/drone-dominance-pentagon-to-order-30000-one-way-drones-in-next-few-days/); [TWZ](https://www.twz.com/air/usaf-plans-to-buy-28000-low-cost-cruise-missiles-in-five-years-advance-with-new-deals)
- Supply-chain compliance gates: from 1 Jan 2027 defense rules bar Chinese-origin rare earth material anywhere in the magnet chain (mining through magnet making); the hard step is metal and alloy conversion, which CSIS calls an experience-driven bottleneck. Phased bans also cover optical glass, displays, batteries and power inverters. — [AP via AJC, Aug 2026 (search summary)](https://www.ajc.com/news/2026/08/us-faces-new-pressure-to-build-weapons-without-chinas-rare-earth-magnets/); [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/)
- Ships: 10 U.S.C. 8679 bars building naval vessels or major hull components abroad absent a presidential waiver with 30 days' notice, and a recurring appropriations rider bars it with no waiver. The Jones Act requires US build for domestic trade. — [CRS RL32665](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32665.html)
- Program instability is itself a gate: since January 2025 the administration cancelled most of the FFG-62 frigate program and two offshore patrol cutters; Eastern Shipbuilding and Fincantieri Marinette laid off workers afterward. — [CBO testimony, Apr 2026](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Qualification is the stated top risk for new missile makers: Castelion's production thesis "depends on clearing test, certification, and carrier qualification gates". — [Sacra: Castelion](https://sacra.com/c/castelion/)

### Inferences
- Two different gates apply. Legacy programs: production-limited, so a faster sub-tier supplier (motors, castings, seekers, metal parts) is selling into funded, unmet demand. New products: funding- and qualification-limited, so speed of factory matters less than surviving a year of continuing resolutions on venture capital.
- The reforms lowered the paperwork gate (commercial-first, fixed-price frameworks, fly-off buying) but did not touch the appropriations gate, which is now the larger risk.
- The compliance rules (China-free magnets, batteries, optics) are simultaneously a barrier and a protected market for whoever can make those inputs domestically.

### Gaps
- No evidence gathered on how Portfolio Acquisition Executives have performed in practice during 2026.
- No sourced timelines for energetics qualification, ITAR licensing, facility clearances or CMMC certification (searches did not run).
- ABS/classification society timelines for novel or autonomous commercial vessels not researched.

## 6. Objections and actor responses

### Takeaway
The strongest objections are budget whiplash (a planned 46% drop after FY2027 and repeated cancellations), a single buyer that spreads work thinly and signs ceilings rather than orders, and incumbents who are investing billions of their own to hold the high end. The Mesquite 155mm failure is a direct warning that "new, faster process" claims can fail in production.

### Cited Findings
- Budget cliff built into the plan: munitions portfolio falls from $49.5B (FY27) to an average $26.7B (FY28-31). — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Ceilings versus cash: Anduril's Army enterprise agreement is "up to $20B over 10 years" with about $174-176M (roughly 0.9%) obligated; JIATF-401's $7B and Perennial's $500M are ceilings. — [New Market Pitch, 2026](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/defense-tech-anduril-update); [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/05/19/pentagon-awards-500-million-contract-to-perennial-autonomy-for-counter-drone-systems/)
- Incumbent response: Lockheed plans a multibillion-dollar, three-year program across more than 20 facilities (up to $9B through 2030) and received a $4.7B undefinitized action; RTX plans $3.1B of 2026 capex; Northrop has invested over $2B since 2019 and expects to triple tactical motor capacity by 2027; a $35B seven-year THAAD award was made in June 2026. — [Breaking Defense, Apr 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/04/the-pentagon-wants-a-188-percent-bump-for-missile-procurement-can-industry-deliver/); [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- Primes also buy or partner their way into the cheap tier: Kongsberg acquired Zone 5 (June 2026); Rheinmetall won a Bundeswehr loitering-munition framework worth up to €2.4B and partners with Anduril on European Barracuda and Fury; HII won 10 of the 30 MUSV hulls. — [TWZ](https://www.twz.com/air/usaf-plans-to-buy-28000-low-cost-cruise-missiles-in-five-years-advance-with-new-deals); [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/european-drone-startups-are-raising-billions-while-the-old-defense-giants-scramble-to-keep-up/); [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/u-s-navy-takes-first-big-unmanned-step-buying-30-production-musv-hulls)
- Process risk: the Army accepted an "unproven production process" at Mesquite that the IG called "high-risk, high reward"; $469M later it had produced nothing and the Army cut the line from three to two. — [Breaking Defense, Jul 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/07/army-well-short-of-155mm-ammo-production-goal-texas-facility-in-the-spotlight/)
- Startup execution risk: a WIRED investigation (March 2026) reportedly described safety issues, production delays, management churn and rocket-motor problems at Anduril; Reuters/WSJ reported 2025 test problems including an Anvil test that caused a 22-acre fire. — [ValueAdd VC blog (secondary, search summary)](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/anduril-industries-how-a-61b-defense-startup-is-rewriting-the-pentagons-playbook); [New Market Pitch](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/defense-tech-anduril-update)
- Valuation skepticism: Anduril at $61B is about 41x 2025 sales with a projected $1.2B 2026 operating loss and no profit expected until 2030; Helsing is valued at $18B with roughly one-thirtieth of Quantum Systems' revenue. — [New Market Pitch](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/defense-tech-anduril-update); [Sacra: Anduril](https://sacra.com/c/anduril/); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/helsing-1-8-billion-series-e-18-billion-valuation)
- Buyer behaviour toward losers: Blue Water Autonomy and Saildrone sued the Navy after the MUSV phase-one awards. — [USNI News (search summary, page not opened)](https://news.usni.org/2026/09/29/3-louisiana-shipyards-to-build-30-drone-ships-in-400m-navy-musv-deal)
- Congress: Sen. Shaheen criticized a six-month drone buying cadence as too slow; CSIS's Karako called relying on a second reconciliation bill a "gamble". — [Breaking Defense, Mar 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/03/drone-dominance-pentagon-to-order-30000-one-way-drones-in-next-few-days/); [Breaking Defense, Apr 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/04/the-pentagon-wants-a-188-percent-bump-for-missile-procurement-can-industry-deliver/)
- Allies: European budgets are already strained by the NATO push; Spain secured an exception capping its spending near 2.1% of GDP. — [Reuters analysis via The Star, Jul 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/07/06/analysis-nato-defence-push-already-strains-europe039s-budgets); [CEPA (search summary; attribution of the Spain figure not confirmed)](https://cepa.org/article/european-natos-warriors-and-peaceniks/)
- Workforce and unions: Bath Iron Works ship designers recently struck; shipyards face a retiring generation; Saronic alone projects up to 10,000 direct jobs at Port Alpha. — [CBO testimony](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf); [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/u-s-navy-takes-first-big-unmanned-step-buying-30-production-musv-hulls)
- Korean yards: profitable, selective, and unwilling to discount; their US investments keep high-value work in Korea. — [Herald Corp Q3 2026 call (search summary)](https://biz.heraldcorp.com/article/10916522); [WSJ via To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/south-korea-promises-it-will-rekindle-u-s-shipbuilding-thats-still-far-off/)
- Adversary response: Russia's 7.3 million FPV plan shows cheap mass is matched quickly, which sustains demand but erodes any one design's advantage. — [Kyiv Post](https://www.kyivpost.com/amp/post/75714)

### Inferences
- Boom-bust risk is unusually explicit: the surge is tied to Iran-war replenishment and a single reconciliation bill, and the government's own plan halves the munitions line afterward. A new entrant should size the factory to the FY28-31 base, not the FY27 request.
- Monopsony cuts both ways. The buyer now deliberately splits awards three or four ways (FAMM, LCCM, MUSV, CCA, Gauntlets), which lowers the bar to a first order but caps any one vendor's share.
- Automation is partly an answer to the workforce objection, but 10,000-job announcements show the leading entrants are still labor-heavy.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence gathered on investor/LP ethical exclusions or bank financing constraints in 2026.
- No sourced union positions on yard automation or foreign-worker programs.
- No data on what happens to Ukrainian drone makers' volumes under a ceasefire scenario.

### Inferences
- The pattern for the winners is product-first, factory-second: a working unit, a fly-off or OTA win, then a nine- or ten-figure raise to build the plant. Nobody listed won a first contract on the strength of a factory.
- Capital is no longer scarce at the platform layer (drones, missiles, USVs each have three to five funded entrants plus primes). It looks scarcer one tier down, where awards are still $10M-140M development contracts and the customer is openly asking for second sources (motors, metal parts, magnets, seekers).

## 8. Buildability: capital, time to first contract, wedges, non-US angles

### Takeaway
A small-drone or interceptor company can now reach first orders in months through open fly-offs, but platform-level entry costs hundreds of millions before revenue and faces five funded rivals. The better wedge for a new entrant with a production-technique edge is the constrained sub-tier (solid rocket motors, shell and missile metal parts, China-free motors/batteries/magnets, ship modules), with Europe and Ukraine as faster first customers than the US.

### Cited Findings
- Fastest path observed: 25 firms entered Gauntlet I, flew for two weeks, and winners received orders "over the next few days"; vendors that deliver get bonus orders taken from those that miss (Neros gained 2,000 + 800 bonus drones). — [Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/03/drone-dominance-pentagon-to-order-30000-one-way-drones-in-next-few-days/); [DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2026/09/30/neros-gauntlet-ii-100-million-order-800-bonus-drones/)
- Ukrainian firms took part and committed to US manufacturing, so foreign entrants can use the same lane. — [Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/03/drone-dominance-pentagon-to-order-30000-one-way-drones-in-next-few-days/)
- Capital before meaningful revenue, by tier: Saronic raised $55M (Series A, Oct 2023) and $175M (Jul 2024) before 2024 revenue of $12.5M; Castelion has raised $464M since 2022 and is pre-production; Mach has raised about $485M with no disclosed revenue; Shield AI says X-BAT needs about $1B to reach operational capability. — [Sacra: Saronic](https://sacra.com/c/saronic/); [Sacra: Castelion](https://sacra.com/c/castelion/); [Sacra: Mach](https://sacra.com/c/mach-industries/); [Sacra: Shield AI](https://sacra.com/c/shield-ai/)
- Sub-tier entry tickets are smaller: X-Bow's roughly $70M agreement funds rate tooling for 500 motors/year; Ursa Major's programs are $10M-137M; the government put $1B directly into L3Harris motor capacity and requests $30.4B of Defense Production Act funds, of which $5.6B is for munitions production and $6.8B for critical chemicals. — [ASDNews](https://asdnews.com/news/defense/2026/10/07/xbow-awarded-nearly-70m-develop-advanced-booster-future-navy-missiles); [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget)
- The customer is explicitly asking for sub-tier help: CNAS recommends investing "across the full production pipeline, including subcomponents, long-lead parts, and critical materials"; the FY2026 NDAA prioritizes second-source qualification; the Navy uses about 25 outsourcing arrangements and "focus factories" for submarine modules, with more than 20 companies building sections. — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/reports/munitions-procurement-and-the-fiscal-year-2027-defense-budget); [War on the Rocks](https://warontherocks.com/2025/12/an-industry-guide-to-the-fy2026-ndaa/); [CRS RL32665](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32665.html); [CBO testimony](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Simpler ships open the yard base: the FF(X) frigate is a smaller, simpler design so "shipyards that do not currently produce Navy ships could participate"; the Navy ordered at least 12 medium landing ships off existing commercial designs; Saronic, Conrad and Master Boat Builders won LCU landing-craft prototypes (about $280.5M combined). — [CBO testimony](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf); [Naval News (search summary)](https://www.navalnews.com/naval-news/2026/09/saronic-awarded-production-contract-for-marauder-under-u-s-navys-musv-marketplace/)
- Counter-caution: any yard new to a Navy program "would face a similarly steep learning curve, so delays could be widespread". — [CBO testimony](https://armedservices.house.gov/UploadedFiles/2026-04-22_Labs_Testimony.pdf)
- Europe: NATO spending above $1.8T in 2026; Rheinmetall's first loitering-munition call-off is about €300M for a five-figure number of drones; Quantum Systems reached about €300M revenue in 2025 selling from seven countries. — [Anadolu](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/nato-defense-spending-projected-to-top-18t-in-2026/3989627); [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/european-drone-startups-are-raising-billions-while-the-old-defense-giants-scramble-to-keep-up/); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/quantum-systems-at-330m-year/)

Shield AI/JSW's $90M Hyderabad plant is a live example of a US design built there. — [Wikipedia: Defence industry of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_industry_of_India); [Sacra: Shield AI](https://sacra.com/c/shield-ai/)

### Inferences
Ranked wedges for a new entrant in this sector, by evidence of funded unmet demand, entry cost and crowding (my judgment, built on the findings above):

1. **China-free drone and munition components** (motors, batteries, magnets, flight electronics). Orders are funded and going unfilled: 40% of Gauntlet I was late on exactly these parts and a hard magnet ban starts 1 Jan 2027. Customers are 20+ drone makers, not one program office. Lowest capital, shortest time to revenue; risk is commodity pricing once China-free supply catches up.
2. **Solid rocket motors and energetics as a merchant supplier.** One incumbent was sole source for PAC-3; the tier above is ordering 3,000 PAC-3s against 550/year output. Entry tickets of $70-140M are visible. Risks: hazardous operations, long qualification, Northrop/L3Harris/Anduril all expanding.
3. **Cheap interceptors and counter-drone effectors.** A $15k interceptor replaced a $4M one and 13,000 were bought fast; NATO pledged $40B+ over five years. Crowded and design cycles are short.
4. **Automated metal parts (shell bodies, missile airframes, castings/forgings substitutes).** The 155mm shortfall is specifically metal parts (36k versus 100k/month). Mesquite shows the failure mode; a proven process would have an immediate buyer.
5. **Ship modules and unmanned hulls for the Navy.** Demand exceeds supply and the Navy already outsources modules; but capital is in the billions (Port Alpha $3B) and Saronic, HII and Korean owners are ahead.
6. **Low-cost cruise missiles / FPV airframes.** Demand proven but each has four-plus funded vendors splitting fixed-price floors.
7. **US commercial ships.** Largest theoretical gap (4-5x) but the weakest demand signal: owners buy Asian, legislation is unpassed, and the captive Jones Act fleet is about 150 ships.

- Time to first contract appears to be months for expendables via fly-offs and one to three years for motors and vessels (Saronic: Series A Oct 2023, first large Navy award Dec 2025; this timing is read from funding and award dates, not a stated figure).
- Europe and Ukraine are faster learning markets than the US: Ukraine fills in-stock orders in 9 days and contracts $8B per half-year; European buyers are placing five-figure drone orders without multi-year fly-off ladders.

### Gaps
- No sourced figures for capital-to-first-revenue for a components or motor startup; the ranking above is inference.
- Rail rolling stock and heavy trucks: no evidence gathered on backlog, cost structure or latent demand, so they are unranked. They should not be assumed attractive or unattractive on this note's evidence.
- Castings and forgings: only indirect evidence (70% of critical submarine suppliers are sole source; more than $10B of submarine industrial base spending). No lead-time or price data retrieved.
- India drone/munition program specifics and iDEX outcomes not retrieved.
