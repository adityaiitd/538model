## C6. Satellite buses and space components at rate: who buys outside SpaceX and Amazon, how much is government, what do Apex, K2 and York earn, and is the segment over-capitalised?

### Takeaway
The merchant bus segment is limited by orders and award timing, not by production capacity, and it is crowded: York (the only pure-play with public filings) guided 2026 revenue to $380-400M at an 18% gross margin, saw backlog fall through 2026, and wrote off $750M of goodwill because it cannot predict contract awards beyond 2026. Demand outside SpaceX and Amazon is mostly US national security, it goes mainly to primes and to Rocket Lab, and the bus is a small part of a $40-60M government satellite, so a $1-5M bus does not unlock much new volume by itself.

### Inferences

**1. Thesis and assumptions.** Thesis: a producer of standard buses (or bus components) at automotive-like rate can sell hundreds to thousands a year at $1-5M each, because government proliferated constellations and new commercial constellations are short of platforms. Cheap buses would then expand the satellite market the way cheap launch did.

**2. Actor simulation, 2027-2032 [MINE].**
- Government buyers (SDA, Space Force, MDA). Precedent: they pause and re-plan (T3 Transport, S9), divert funds under budget stress (S9), split awards four ways (S8), and favour primes and one or two proven mid-tier firms. 2027: FY27/FY28 budgets decide Transport vs MILNET; SBI prototypes fly. 2028: SBI demonstration deadline; a down-select follows. 2029: a new administration takes office in January; Golden Dome is a presidential priority and is the single largest swing factor. If it continues, 2029-2032 brings production orders in the low thousands of satellites; if it is cut back, the merchant bus makers lose their growth case. Effect: volumes are lumpy, pricing is fixed-price and tight, timing risk sits with the supplier (York's impairment is the proof).
- Commercial constellation operators. SpaceX and Amazon build in-house. Other large programmes are few (SES via K2, S7). Most likely response: two or three new commercial constellations order 50-300 buses each over the period; price converges at $3-6M (LEO) and about $15M (large). Not enough to absorb 400+ buses a year of stated capacity.
- Incumbents. York needs volume and will cut price to fill its plant; Apex and K2 hold over $1.7B of private capital between them and can sell below cost for years; Rocket Lab sells buses plus components plus launch as a bundle; Lockheed and RTX keep their captive bus units (S13). Expect one or two acquisitions of sub-scale bus makers by primes at modest prices (Terran went for about $450M).
- SpaceX. It wins government work directly (MILNET, SBI awards, S9-S10) and its Starshield bus is the lowest-cost platform. It is unlikely to sell buses to rivals. Its presence caps price.
- China and trade policy. Chinese buses cannot serve US government work and ITAR limits US exports; this is a protected market, so there is no import threat, but also no export upside to the fastest-growing foreign constellations (Guowang, Qianfan build domestically). [RECOLLECTION, UNVERIFIED on the Chinese programmes.]
- Regulators. FCC/ITU spectrum filings and deployment milestones gate commercial constellations more than bus supply does; launch licensing is months-scale (first-round notes). No change expected that helps a bus maker.
- Financiers. York's write-down and share-price reaction make public investors cautious; late-stage private money continues to back the leaders (Apex talks at about $4B). A new 2027 entrant competes for capital against firms valued at 20x+ revenue.
- Launch suppliers. SpaceX remains the default; Neutron and New Glenn slip (S4, S14). No relief for a bus maker's schedule risk before 2028.
- Net effect: bus prices drift down 20-40% by 2030, gross margins stay in the high teens to twenties for bus-only makers, volume growth depends on one political programme.

**3. Market arithmetic [MINE unless tagged].**
- Size today. Merchant bus-maker revenue, 2026 run-rate: York about $390M (S1) + Apex roughly $60-130M (S5, S6) + K2 small + Rocket Lab Space Systems about $790M annualised from Q3 product revenue, which includes components (S4) + captive Terran/Blue Canyon. Call it $1.3-1.6B a year. Against that, private capital raised by Apex (about $720M), K2 ($1B+), Muon ($225M) and Loft ($325M) totals about $2.3B, plus York's IPO. The pure-play leader earns an 18% gross margin. That is an over-capitalised segment.
- Government share. York is mostly SDA; Apex is two-thirds defense (S5); Rocket Lab's largest satellite contracts are SDA and Space Force. I estimate 70-85% of merchant bus revenue is US national security.
- Bus share of a government satellite. $5.25B / 108 = about $49M per Tracking satellite (S8). At a $5-12M bus, the bus is 10-25%. Cutting the bus to $3M lowers the satellite price by 4-18%. With any plausible elasticity that is not a market-expanding move.
- Expansion case. Apex's own figure: $30B platform layer / $3.5M = about 8,600 buses for Golden Dome (S11). Spread over 2029-2035 that is about 1,200 a year and about $4B a year, three times today's merchant revenue. This is the whole bull case and it is a single-customer, single-programme bet.
- Commercial expansion. Non-Starlink commercial demand is about $8B for whole satellites (S11); buses at 15-25% of value are $1.2-2B, mostly already served in-house or by GEO primes. I find no evidence of a price-elastic buyer pool; the Starlink "elasticity" was internal demand.

**4. Bear / base / bull to 2032 for a bus maker founded in 2027 [MINE].**
- Bear: under $20M revenue, $150-300M consumed. No programme win; incumbents with heritage and spare capacity undercut; sold for parts or acqui-hired.
- Base: $80-200M revenue, $400-700M consumed, gross margin 15-25%. Wins subcontract roles on one or two defense programmes; never earns its cost of capital.
- Bull: $0.5-1B revenue, $1B+ consumed. Golden Dome goes to production in the thousands and the Pentagon deliberately qualifies a fifth supplier.
- Component variant (optical terminals, propulsion, solar arrays, reaction wheels, radiation-tolerant avionics sold to all bus makers): base $30-100M revenue on $50-150M capital at 35-45% gross margin. Rocket Lab's acquisitions (Mynaric for $155M in 2026, Geost, first-round notes) show both the demand for components and the exit route.

**5. Kill criteria and 90-day test.**
- Kill if: the FY28 budget request (early 2027) again leaves the Transport Layer unfunded in favour of MILNET; SBI stays at prototype funding through 2028; York's backlog keeps falling; no buyer will sign a deposit-backed order with a delivery date.
- Cheapest test: do not design a bus. Interview 15-20 programme and supply-chain leads at York, Apex, K2, Rocket Lab, Muon, Loft, the primes and two commercial operators and ask for their three longest-lead bus components, current price and lead time. Then ask for one paid study or letter of intent with price and date. Pass = two named buyers each naming the same component with 9+ month lead time and fewer than three funded suppliers. Cost: travel and time, under $50k.

Judgement: the first round ranked this second in its sector on the strength of the $40-60M price umbrella and Apex's fast start. The public numbers from York say the umbrella belongs to payload makers and primes, and that the bus layer is now a low-margin, order-starved business with more factory than demand. A founder should not start a bus company in 2027. The defensible version is a component or subsystem that every bus maker is short of, tested with the 90-day survey above.

## C7. Delivery-drone airframes and contract production: do operators build in-house, what do airframes and deliveries cost, is Part 108 final, and would operators outsource?

### Takeaway
Drone-delivery demand is real and steep, but there is no merchant airframe market to sell into: Zipline, Wing, Flytrex and DoorDash build their own aircraft, Amazon flies its own MK30 design (who assembles it was not confirmed), and the two leaders have just announced a $1.5B raise earmarked for manufacturing (Zipline) and up to $125M for a plant (Wing), and small aircraft are cheap enough to build that Wing says a 15,000 sq ft plant makes "tens of thousands a year". Part 108 was still unpublished as of 17 Nov 2026. Airframes look like a low-single-digit percentage of delivery revenue, so this is neither the bottleneck nor the profit pool.

## C10. Automated or decentralised cell-therapy manufacturing: what do Cellares and point-of-care programmes show, is the access gap about slots, and what does in vivo CAR-T do by 2030?

### Takeaway
Manufacturing is a real cost in CAR-T, but the evidence that manufacturing slots are what keeps 80% of eligible patients untreated is weak in 2026: the documented barriers are referral relationships, distance, payer delay and treatment-centre capacity, incumbents now report 99% manufacturing success and are doubling capacity, and Kite's CAR-T sales are falling. Cellares has the customers and the regulatory designation but still no published cost per dose; the one company built on decentralised point-of-care manufacturing (Galapagos) shut that business without finding a buyer; and big pharma has spent over $5B buying in vivo CAR-T programmes that need no ex vivo manufacturing at all, though all are still in Phase 1.

Cash demand is proven (Neko: 100,000+ scans and a 350,000 waitlist; Prenuvo: about $100M revenue in 2024; Function sells a $499 MRI), but nobody has published clinic-level economics, and the arithmetic says the scanner is a small part of cost per scan: clinician time, premises and customer acquisition dominate. **2. What would have to be true by 2028-2030 for a $10-20k humanoid to do useful work [MINE].**
- Reliability: task success of 99.9%+ on a defined family of tasks, with a human intervention less than about once per eight-hour shift. Today's public figures are about 90% over two weeks (R7) and vendor-run endurance streams.
- Speed: at least 70-80% of a human on the same task (today 30-50%, R8).
- Duty: 16-20 hours a day through battery swap or opportunity charging.
- Hands: durable for thousands of hours at about $1-2k each (today about $16k as an add-on, R1).
- Supply chain: actuators, reducers and magnets from non-Chinese sources at near-Chinese cost, or a tolerated Chinese supply (today a 3-6x gap, R3 vs R1).
- Safety: ISO 25785-1 published and accepted by insurers (R8).
- Cost arithmetic: $20k robot over three years ($6.7k/yr) + $10k service + $5k software = about $21.7k a year. At 4,000 hours and 50% of human speed that is 2,000 human-equivalent hours, about $11 per hour. At a $130k Western build cost it is about $58k a year, about $29 per hour, no better than a person. So price matters only for Western builds; at Chinese cost the economics already work on paper and still nobody deploys at scale, which shows the binding constraint is unsupervised reliability and task breadth.

**3. Actor simulation, 2027-2032 [MINE].**
- Factory and warehouse operators. Precedent: pilots that end (BMW), milestone-conditional orders (Agility), preference for task-specific automation (first-round Gartner view). 2027-2028: pilots multiply; paid fleets stay in the hundreds per site at most. 2029-2030: if the reliability threshold is met for tote handling and machine tending, orders reach thousands per customer. They will buy on a per-hour or per-pick basis.
- Tesla, Figure, 1X. Tesla ramps to thousands a week for internal use first (R5) and prices externally near cost; Figure and 1X lease at $500-600 a month (R6). Effect: Western list price converges on $20-30k by 2028 regardless of true cost, financed by investors. A new entrant cannot match this.
- Unitree and Chinese makers. They hold cost leadership (R1) and 86-97% of volume (R2), are blocked from new US approvals (R8), and will sell through approved legacy models, third countries and components.
- US trade and security policy. FCC list holds; Section 232 on robots may add tariffs after April 2027 (first-round notes); China retaliates through magnet licensing. Effect: a US price umbrella for robots built with non-Chinese parts, and a shortage of those parts. This is where a production business could exist (actuators, reducers, magnets, hands), consistent with the first round.
- Standards bodies, insurers, unions. ISO 25785-1 in 2027; insurers require fall-zone controls; I found no union position on factory humanoids (first-round gap persists). Teamsters and city councils have opposed robotaxis (first-round notes).
- Ride-hail fleets and robotaxi operators. Precedent: Waymo buys base vehicles from carmakers and fits its own system (R9); it tolerated a doubled vehicle cost rather than wait. 2027: Ojai fleet in service, Ioniq 5 ramps; 2028: Hyundai's 50,000-unit programme starts (R11); China-technology rules may end further Zeekr imports (R10). Tesla and Zoox stay in-house. Uber and Lyft partner with whoever has vehicles.
- NHTSA. Exemption path widens (R12); the cap stops binding by about 2028 in my base case.
- Carmakers. Hyundai, Toyota and others see a small, prestigious fleet order book and price it near retail. A startup cannot beat a volume carmaker on a crash-certified base vehicle.
- Net effect: humanoid volume in the West stays in the tens of thousands a year until reliability is proven; robotaxi base-vehicle demand in the US is tens of thousands a year by 2030, supplied by carmakers.

**4. Market arithmetic [MINE unless tagged].**
- Humanoids today. 50,000-60,000 units (2026, R2) at roughly $25k is about $1.3-1.5B, mostly Chinese, mostly research and entertainment. Western productive deployments are in the hundreds to low thousands (R4, R6, R7).
- Humanoid expansion. Mechanism is a capability threshold, not a demand curve. Below it, demand is the finite pool of labs, universities and showrooms. Above it, at about $11 per human-equivalent hour, demand is very large. I put a 25-35% chance on the threshold being met for a commercially meaningful task family by 2030.
- Robotaxi vehicles. Waymo does about 129 paid rides per vehicle per week (500,000 / 3,871, R11). If US robotaxi rides grow 20x to 10 million a week by 2030, the fleet is about 75,000 vehicles; at a five-year life plus growth, 15,000-25,000 vehicles a year, worth $0.75-1.25B at $50k each. Hyundai's 50,000 order alone covers several years of that.
- Tariff evidence. $78k after tariff / 2.025 = about $38.5k before tariff, so Waymo paid about $39.5k extra per van, about $126M across 3,200 (R9). That measures the scarcity value of a purpose-built US-legal vehicle in 2026. A Georgia-built Ioniq 5 removes it.

**5. Bear / base / bull to 2032 [MINE].**
- New humanoid maker. Bear: under $5M revenue, $0.2-1B consumed (Agility's 2026 looks like this). Base: $20-100M revenue from research units and pilots, $0.5-2B consumed. Bull: $1-3B revenue if the threshold is met by 2029 and trade barriers hold, $3B+ consumed.
- Robotaxi vehicle supplier. Bear and base: no vehicle revenue; at most $0-50M from upfitting, depot or sensor-integration services. Bull: a contract for about 10,000 vehicles a year at $50k ($500M) at single-digit margins, needing $1-2B and a crash-certified platform. I do not see a credible path for a startup.

**6. Kill criteria and 90-day test.**
- Kill (humanoids) if: after 60 days of tuning on one customer task, interventions exceed one per hour or throughput stays under 70% of a person; no customer will pay per unit of work; non-Chinese actuator quotes stay above 3x Chinese prices.
- Cheapest test: do not build a robot. Put a commercially available robot (an already-approved model, or a US-made mobile manipulator) on one paying customer's real task for 30-60 days with remote assistance, and log interventions per hour, human-equivalent throughput and uptime. Publish the numbers to the customer. Pass = a signed paid extension. Cost: $100-300k.
- Robotaxi vehicles: ask procurement at Waymo, Zoox, May Mobility, Nuro and Uber whether they would buy a base vehicle or sub-system from a non-carmaker. Kill if none will issue a request for quotation.

## Cross-candidate comparison: does "abundant production" unlock any of the five?

### Takeaway
None of the five passes the test the founder set. In each case volume is limited by something other than the cost or speed of making the physical product: contract awards and payloads (C6), customers who are also the makers (C7), referral, payment and modality change (C10), clinicians and premises (C11), and unsupervised capability (C14). The buildable residue in each is a narrower component, tool or service business.
