# F6 v1 seller and client panel, round 1 (simulated, 24 Nov 2026)

Brief tested: `F6_v1.md` (buy one or two medical billing companies totalling $35-45M of revenue at about one times revenue within 9-12 months, first closing by month 5-6, move the work to AI agents, cost to serve from 85% to 60-65% in 18 months).

Everything below is a simulated hypothesis from archetype personas. It is not seller, client or staff validation. No real people, quotes, offers or orders.

Evidence labels: [source: URL] = checked in this session; "summary" means the figure came from a search-result summary and the page itself was not opened; [notes] = the founder's research notes and earlier panels; [DK] = domain knowledge, unverified.

Web calls used: 6 of 6 (4 searches, 2 page fetches). Not found: a closed-deal comparison set for billing companies of $15-40M revenue, any count of such firms sold per year, any 2026 purchase of a physician billing firm by a lab-backed roll-up, and any post-2015 public data on client loss after a billing-company sale. ## 1. Founder-owner, aged 62, billing company with $18M revenue

**Who.** 180 US staff in one town, 140 physician-practice clients, 26 years in business. No successor. [DK archetype]

**His numbers.** Revenue $18.0M. Payroll $11.4M. Clearinghouse, statements and software $1.8M. Rent, insurance and other $1.6M. Owner pay and family on payroll $1.0M. Reported EBITDA $2.2M (12%). Adjusted for owner costs: $2.9M (16%). Average client $129K a year; top client 7%; top ten 34%. Clients sit on 11 different practice systems. About 60% of contracts are past their first term and renew yearly with 60-90 days' notice either way. [DK]

**Incentives and constraints.** Cash at close and certainty. He lives among the 180 staff. Two clients were bought by sponsor-backed platforms last year and left, and wages rose 5%. He receives two or three buyer letters a month.

**Alternatives.** (a) A sponsor-backed billing platform: 7-8x adjusted, all cash, 60-75 days, 10-20% rollover. (b) An offshore-enabled billing firm such as persona 2: 7-8x cash, funded from its own balance sheet. (c) A venture-backed AI roll-up: 8x with part in stock. (d) Wait a year. He believes waiting costs him, because buyers already discount labor-heavy books [source: PitchBook title].

**The founder's offer as briefed.** $18M: 1.0x revenue, 6.2x his adjusted EBITDA, 8.2x reported. 15% seller note ($2.7M), bank debt, cash at close $15.3M, from a buyer who has never closed a deal.

**Decision.** **Refuses** that offer. **Would sell** to the founder on these terms:
- $21M headline (7.2x adjusted, 1.17x revenue).
- At least $18M cash at close. Seller note at most $2M, secured second lien, 9%, 24 months. $1M rollover at his option.
- No earnout tied to client retention, because the buyer intends to change the workflow and cut staff. He will not warrant retention.
- Equity commitment letter and a lender term sheet before exclusivity. 60 days' exclusivity. $500K deposit that goes hard after 45 days.
- Tax structure that gives the buyer a step-up only if the buyer pays his extra tax. Buying the entity (after a reorganisation) keeps the client contracts in the same legal party, so most need no assignment consent. [DK]
- He stays 12 months at $250K and introduces the buyer to the top 25 clients himself.
- All-cash alternative: $20M, no note, 60-day close. That is his floor (1.1x revenue, 6.9x adjusted).

**Reason.** At equal price he takes the platform's cash. A first-time buyer has to pay half a turn more or put money at risk. His price is not far from the brief. The structure is.

**What would change it.** Nothing moves him to $18M with a 15% note except the other buyers walking away. A competing all-cash $22M offer ends the founder's deal.

## 2. Owner, $35M-revenue billing company with offshore operations

**Who.** 90 US staff, 520 in a captive centre in India. 60 clients: sponsor-backed specialty groups, hospital-employed physician groups, surgery centers. Average fee 3.6%. Adjusted EBITDA $8.4M (24%). Top client 11%; top ten 48%. [DK archetype]

**Approaches in hand.** (a) Sponsor-backed platform: 10x, $84M, 25% rollover. (b) India-based strategic: 11x cash, $92M; the same type of buyer paid 15.7x for a larger asset in April [source: summary]. (c) Venture-backed AI roll-up: 9.5x with 30% in stock.

**The founder's offer.** $35M is 4.2x EBITDA.

**Decision.** **Refuses.** Will not sign a confidentiality agreement with an unfunded first-time buyer. Hires a banker in Q1 2027. Floor: $80M (9.5x, 2.3x revenue), at least 75% cash, no financing condition.

**Reasons.**
1. One times revenue values his margin as if it were 15%. It is 24%.
2. His cost to serve is already 62-65% through offshore seats at $14-18K a year [notes: C3 capital panel, DK]. The saving the founder plans has been taken. Agents replacing $16K seats save far less than agents replacing $60K seats.
3. He licenses denial and claim-status agents himself and passes 10-15% of the saving to clients at renewal [notes: outsourcer pattern].
4. He is a **rival bidder** for persona 1's firm: 7-8x cash, close in 60 days, and a proven plan to move 100 of the 180 jobs offshore in 12 months.

**What would change it.** $85M with committed funds. That is outside the founder's capital.

## 3. Lower-middle-market healthcare-services M&A banker

**Who buys billing companies in late 2026.** [DK unless marked]
1. Sponsor-backed billing platforms doing add-ons (about 200 sponsor-owned firms exist [source: summary]). The most frequent buyer.
2. India- and Philippines-based strategics buying US client books to move offshore.
3. Venture-backed AI roll-ups and AI billing start-ups buying a book to deploy on (Procode is the public example [source]). Lab-backed holding companies have not been seen in physician billing yet [source: search found none].
4. Records and practice-system vendors buying billing firms that use their system.
5. Independent sponsors and search funds, mostly under $10M of revenue.

**Prices.** The market has split in two.

| Target | Adjusted EBITDA multiple | Revenue multiple | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onshore, manual, 10-16% margin, $15-25M revenue | 6-8x | 0.8-1.25x | 70-85% cash; the rest note, earnout or rollover |
| Offshore-enabled or own software, 22-28% margin, $25-40M | 9-12x | 2.0-2.8x | 70-80% cash, rollover |
| Platforms above $100M | 14-18x | 1.6-4.3x | sponsor buyouts [source: summary] |

Basis: 6-11x for $3M+ EBITDA [source: CT Acquisitions]; large comparables [source: summary]; the low end has fallen about one turn since 2024 because sponsors now price in fee compression from agents [source: PitchBook title; DK for the size of the move].

**The brief's "one times revenue".** Half right. It exists, for onshore manual firms. Those firms usually earn 10-13% reported, so one times revenue is 7.5-10x reported EBITDA, and lenders size on reported or lightly adjusted EBITDA. The brief's "6-7x at a 15% margin" holds only on the seller's adjusted figure.

**Supply.** [DK, order of magnitude] Roughly 4,000-5,000 US billing companies, most under $5M. Perhaps 150-250 have $15-40M of revenue, and more than half are already sponsor-owned. Founder-owned independents in the band: 60-100. Six to twelve change hands a year, three to five through a banked process. In any six-month window a buyer sees two to four actionable targets, each with five to ten bidders.

**Time.**
- Banked process: launch to signed letter of intent 3-4 months; letter of intent to closing 75-120 days. A buyer who enters at the teaser and wins closes 5-7 months later.
- Proprietary approach to an owner: 4-9 months when it closes; about half die because the owner changes his mind. Matches the 90-180 day figure for off-market sales to experienced platforms [source: CT Acquisitions].
- A first-time buyer adds 2-4 months: sourcing with no reputation, a quality-of-earnings report, lender approval, co-investor approval, client consent calls.

**How a first-time buyer is treated.**
- Contacted as one of 40-60 parties. Gets the information memorandum.
- Does not reach management meetings without an equity commitment letter for all the equity and a named lender.
- He tells the seller to discount an unfunded bid by 10-15%, or to require a deposit or a reverse break fee of 3-5%.
- A first-time buyer wins about 10-15% of processes entered. Letters of intent with a financing condition fail 30-40% of the time.

**Decision.** Will show the founder deals. Will not recommend exclusivity unless the bid is within 5% of the top bid with committed funds, or half a turn above it without.

**What would change it.** A closed first deal. After one closing the founder is treated as a platform.

## 4. Administrator, 25-physician multi-specialty group paying 5.5%

**Who.** $24M of collections; fee $1.32M a year; the seller's largest client (7% of revenue). Physician-owners take monthly draws from collections. [DK archetype]

**Contract.** Three-year first term long expired; renews yearly; 90 days' notice; assignment needs her written consent, not to be unreasonably withheld. If the buyer purchases the entity, no consent is needed, but the seller's letter of intent makes her comfort call a closing condition anyway. [DK]

**Alternatives.** Her records vendor's billing service at 2.9-4% [notes]; two outsourcers quoting 3.5-4.5% for her size [notes]; an AI billing start-up.

**Decision.** **Stays and renegotiates.** Does not leave in year one: a 10-25% cash dip for 60-120 days [notes] is $0.4-2.0M out of physician draws, more than the fee saving.

**Terms.**
- At the change of owner: 4.5% (a cut of 18%, $240K a year), one-year term, 60-day termination.
- When told agents do the work: 4.0% (a cut of 27% from today), with service levels and fee credits: days in receivables at or under 38, first-pass acceptance at or above 95%, denial rate at or under 7%. Named account manager kept on her account.

**Leaves if.** The account manager leaves, or monthly collections fall more than 5% below the trailing six-month average for two months running. She sees deposits weekly.

**What would change it.** Nothing keeps her at 5.5%. She has known for two years she overpays, and the sale is her occasion to act.

## 5. Chief financial officer, PE-backed specialty group paying 3.2%

**Who.** $85M of collections; fee $2.7M. Sponsor in year four of its hold. The sponsor has an AI programme and preferred access to a lab deployment venture [notes]. [DK archetype]

**Contract.** Negotiated on her paper: right to terminate on 30 days' notice within 90 days of a change of control; most-favoured-customer clause; audit right; consent needed for offshore work and for new subcontractors; service-level credits; 180 days of exit assistance. [DK]

**Decision.** **Renegotiates at once and runs a tender.** She uses the change-of-control right as leverage.

**Terms.** 2.5% from the closing date (a cut of 22%). 2.2% in year two, or per-claim pricing at $2.50-3.50 [notes: C3 capital panel]. Termination for convenience on 90 days. Written list of every model provider touching her data.

**When told agents do the work.** "Then the saving is ours." She asks for cost-plus pricing and asks her sponsor whether to license the same agents and bring billing in-house.

**Likelihood she leaves.** 35% within 12 months; 60% within 24. Reasons unrelated to service: sale of the group, the sponsor's own programme, or an offshore incumbent at 2.5-2.8% [notes].

**What would change it.** A lower price than the tender produces. Loyalty is not a factor.

## 6. Owner, four-physician practice paying 7%

**Who.** $3.2M of collections; fee $224K. Aged 61. One-year contract that renews itself, 60 days' notice, on the billing company's own paper. [DK archetype]

**Decision.** **Stays. Does not renegotiate.** Signs nothing new.

**Reason.** He does not shop. His test is whether deposits arrive and whether the account representative he has dealt with for nine years answers the phone. He is indifferent to agents.

**Leaves if.** That representative leaves: 50% chance he follows her to wherever she goes. Or two bad months of deposits. Or he sells the practice to a hospital or a platform, which ends the contract: 4-6% of such clients a year [notes: 3-5% change owner, DK].

**Fee.** No request in year one. Over three years, 30% chance a records vendor's 4-5% bundled offer [notes] takes him.

**Note for the founder.** These clients are the most loyal and the most profitable per dollar. They also need the most telephone time, and their loyalty is to one employee, not to the company.

## 7. Operations head, surgery-center operator

**Who.** Seven centers, $60M of collections, fee 3.8% ($2.3M). Each center has physician-investors paid monthly distributions from cash. [DK archetype]

**Contract.** One agreement per center, each needing that center's board to consent to assignment; 60 days' notice. [DK]

**Constraints.** Implant billing, out-of-network claims and payer carve-outs are contract-specific and sit in the heads of three people at the billing company [notes: C3 persona 8]. Average claim $2,000-4,000, so one stuck batch moves a month.

**Decision.** **Stays for now. Refuses agents on his account** until a 60-day parallel run on one center shows the same cash. Consents center by center.

**Terms.** Fee to 3.3% when agents go live (a cut of 13%). Service levels with credits: cases billed within three days, days in receivables at or under 35. The three named specialists stay on his account.

**Alternatives.** A specialist surgery-center billing firm; or bringing billing into his own management company, which he rates at 30% likely within 24 months as he adds centers.

**How fast he sees a problem.** He reads daily cash by center and the case-to-bill lag. Ten to twenty days.

## 8. Compliance lead at a client

**Who.** Compliance officer at a 60-physician group that uses the acquired company. Holds a veto, not a vote.

**Findings.** [DK unless marked]
1. **Business associate terms.** Federal rules let a billing company add subcontractors under its own flow-down agreements without the client's consent unless the contract says otherwise. Small practices signed the billing company's template, which is permissive. Larger clients signed their own paper: 30 days' notice of new subcontractors, consent for any offshore access, breach notice in 5-10 days. In the $18M firm that is roughly 25 clients and 40-45% of revenue.
2. **AI disclosure.** No general legal duty to tell a client that software does the work. But most negotiated contracts require notice of material changes in personnel, location or subcontractors, and a model provider processing patient records is a new subcontractor. If the buyer goes live without telling her and she learns later, she treats it as cause to terminate.
3. **Offshore.** Refused. Medicare Advantage plans require offshore attestations and some Medicaid contracts bar it [notes]. Model inference must run in the US with zero retention and no training on her data.
4. **Coding.** A percentage fee plus machine coding is the pattern federal guidance on billing companies warns about [notes]. She requires a certified coder to sign off every claim for 90 days, then a 10% sample at 95% accuracy.
5. **What helps the buyer.** The acquired company has an operating history, an existing security audit and existing payer enrollments. A start-up has none [notes: C3 persona 6]. This removes the 6-12 month wait for audit history.

**Decision.** **Stays, with conditions. Blocks agent go-live on her account** for a 60-90 day review.

**Terms.** Amended business associate agreement; named sub-processors; US-only; sign-off as above; indemnity for coding errors by agents not capped at fees; $10M errors-and-omissions and cyber cover.

**What would change it.** An external coding audit of the agents on another client's live claims.

## 9. Director of operations at the acquired company

**Who.** 22 years at the $18M firm. Runs 150 of the 180 staff. Salary $170K. [DK archetype]

**Interest.** Her job and her people, stated plainly. Her operational facts stand on their own. [DK]

**Where the 180 work.** Follow-up on denials and unpaid claims 60; payment posting 30; charge entry and claim submission 25; coding 20; eligibility and authorizations 15; patient calls 10; account managers 12; management and IT 8.

**Her view of the automation plan.**
- Payment posting is already 80-85% automatic through electronic remittances. The 30 posters handle paper, lockbox, takebacks and patient payments.
- Eligibility already runs in batch through the clearinghouse.
- Follow-up is where the seats are. Agents can sort and draft. Payer portals bar automated logins [notes], and status calls still take 20-30 minutes each.
- 140 clients on 11 practice systems, reached through user logins. Each system is a separate automation that breaks on updates [notes: C3 persona 7].
- Larger clients will require human sign-off for 90 days (persona 8).

**Her estimate.** 25-30 seats in 12 months ($1.6M, 9 points of revenue). Against that: agent and tool cost $0.4-0.6M and a new engineering team $0.8-1.2M. Net year-one saving 0-3 points. By month 18-24: 50-60 seats, 17-19 points gross, 11-14 net. **Cost to serve reaches 72-75% at month 18, not 60-65%.** An offshore buyer gets to 65% in 12 months with a known method.

**Staff retention.** Billing staff already turn over 25-30% a year [DK]. Once the plan is known, the best follow-up staff leave first, and receivables slip before any agent is live. Three of the 12 account managers will be approached by rival firms within 90 days; non-solicitation clauses are weak in several states [DK]. The public record has a case where a selling shareholder competed and that book lost 61% [source: SEC filing].

**Decision.** **Stays 6-9 months if paid. Begins looking at once.** 40% chance she is gone within 12 months without the terms below.

**Terms.** Retention bonus of 30% of salary at 12 months and again at 24; 12 months' severance if dismissed; a 0.5-1% profits interest; control of the order in which tasks are automated; no layoffs for six months; bonuses of $15K a year for each account manager and $20K for each supervisor; staff severance of two weeks per year of service. Cost: $1.0-1.4M of retention over two years plus $0.5-0.8M of severance. Neither is in the brief.

**What would change it.** Being made the person who runs the redesign, with equity. Then she stays and so do most account managers.

## 10. Answers to the test questions

**Real prices and who else bids.** Onshore manual firms: 6-8x adjusted EBITDA, 0.8-1.25x revenue; this panel's seller clears at 1.1x all cash or 1.17x with a small note. Offshore-enabled firms: 9-12x, 2.0-2.8x revenue. Bidders: sponsor-backed platforms, offshore strategics, AI roll-ups, records vendors. All pay cash and have closed before.

**Time for a first purchase, first contact to closing.** 7-11 months is the central range for a first-time buyer. Best case 5-6 months, and only if the co-investor and lender are committed before the first letter of intent.

**Client loss in the 12 months after a sale.** Panel estimate for the $18M firm under the founder's plan:

| Segment | Share of revenue | Clients lost (revenue) | Fee cut on those who stay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small practices at 6-8% | 55% | 10-14% | 0-2% |
| Mid-sized groups at 4.5-6% | 30% | 12-18% | 12-20% |
| Sponsor-backed and surgery centers at 3-4% | 15% | 25-40% | 13-22% |
| Weighted | 100% | 13-19% | 4-7% of total revenue |

Revenue 12 months after closing: 75-83% of the purchased figure before new sales. The seller's normal referral flow replaces 6-8 points if the owner and account managers stay. Net: down 9-19%. Central estimate **down 14%**. Without any workflow change it would be down 3-6%. The brief's "under 10%" is the no-change case.

**Do clients ask for a lower fee?** The larger half of the book does, at the change of owner and again when agents are disclosed: 13-27% cuts among personas 4, 5 and 7. Small practices do not ask. Blended: about 1.0-1.5 points of collections over three years, not half a point.

**Assignment and termination.** Most contracts are at will in practice: 60-90 days' notice after the first term [source: SEC filing; DK]. Buying the entity avoids assignment consents. Negotiated contracts (40-45% of revenue) carry change-of-control exits, subcontractor notice and offshore consent. The contract is not what holds clients. Fear of a cash dip and the account manager are.

**How fast collections show a problem.**

| Failure | Where it shows | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Claims not going out or rejected at the clearinghouse | Submission and rejection reports | 1-3 days |
| Payer denials rising | Remittances | 2-5 weeks |
| Cash falling | Client bank deposits; Medicare pays clean electronic claims after about 14 days, commercial plans in 15-45 | 2-5 weeks |
| Follow-up not worked, appeals weak | Receivables over 90 days; net collection by service-date cohort | 3-4 months |
| Payments misposted or balances written off wrongly | Cohort audit only | 3-6 months, or never |
| Coding errors | Payer and government audits | 1-3 years, with recoupment |

Loud failures are caught inside a month and are recoverable, because filing limits run 90-365 days [DK]. Clients watch deposits weekly, so a three-week dip produces termination notices in month two. Quiet failures compound for a quarter before anyone sees them.

**Do the relationship holders stay?** Not by default. Director: 60% stays 12 months with the terms above, 40% without. Account managers: expect to lose 2-4 of 12 in year one; each carries $1-1.5M of small-practice revenue.

## 12. Panel probabilities

- First acquisition of at least $15M of revenue closes within 6 months: **7%**. Within 9 months: **20%**. Within 12 months: **38%**.
- $40M of gross recognized revenue in a trailing twelve months by month 18: **3%**. A plausible good case (one $18M closing in month 9, a second of $20M in month 14) recognizes about $20M in months 7-18.
- $100M by month 36: **5%**. It needs about $115-130M of purchased revenue by month 24 to offset shrinkage, which is $130-170M of purchases and $60-80M of equity.
- Year five if it works: revenue $100-120M, gross margin 35-42%, EBITDA 20-24% ($22-28M). Enterprise value at 8-10x: $180-280M. Equity after $50-70M of net debt: $120-200M, of which the founder holds perhaps 25-35%. Revenue is recurring and nominally contracted, but terminable on 60-90 days' notice and it floats with client collections.
