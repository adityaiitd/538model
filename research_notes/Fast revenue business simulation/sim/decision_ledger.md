# Decision ledger, second study

The bar: $40M of gross recognized revenue in a trailing twelve months by month 18, and $100M by month 36.

All panel output is simulated. Personas are archetypes played by independent agents told to pursue their own interests. Nothing here is a real interview, order or commitment. Probabilities are the panels' own estimates.

### F1 v1: real-work data for AI labs

| Objection | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Larger suppliers already do this with far more capital | One supplier committed $1bn to buy enterprise data. Another runs a 100-connector program and pays owners in two to four weeks. A lab bid about $10M directly for a bankrupt airline's archive. | Unresolved. v2 stops competing as a principal buyer. |
| Labs buy one corpus at a time, not quarterly licences | Pilots $0.15-0.5M, then $0.5-3M per corpus | v2 leads with a service ordered weekly |
| Data owners want fixed cash, $0.1-5M | Makes the company a principal with inventory risk | v2 buys only against a signed first order |
| Sellers lack title to their clients' information | Outsourcers, accountants and claims firms hold client data. A bank's counsel will not certify de-identified screen video. | v2 limits data to an owner's own internal work and court-ordered insolvency sales |
| Gross margin is 5% with one buyer per corpus, 25% with two | Panel calculation. Expected buyers per corpus 1.5-1.8. | v2 does not rely on corpus margin |
| Labs supply themselves | One records its employees' screens. Others ask contractors to upload past work. | Unresolved |

### F2 v1: trucked gas. Rejected.

| Objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| A contract never repays the equipment | A $57M block earns $4.6-7.4M of EBITDA a year on a 12-30 month contract. The market leader's 135 MW contract returns about $84M on about $140M of equipment. Block return 0-6% unlevered. |
| Lead time equals the bridge | Trailers and a compression hub take 9-18 months. A typical lateral quote is 12 months. |
| No buyer gives sole supply or an advance to a first-time hazardous-materials operator | Buyers want three years of loss history and $25-50M of cover. Insurers offer $10-15M. |
| The brief's numbers were high | Revenue per 35 MW block $27.7M, not $40M. Delivery margin about $8.50-10 per MMBtu, not $11-12. Capital $57M, not $35-45M. |
| Buying an operator instead costs about two times revenue | The leader sold at 8.5 times EBITDA |

### F3 v1: customs duty recovery. Rejected.

| Objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Refund per client is a quarter of the brief | Five simulated importers yielded $6.6M of refunds and $0.8M of fees. $40M needs 200-480 producing clients. |
| Large exporters already claim through specialists at 3-10% | Panel estimate |
| Fee is 12-15% in the mid-market and falling | Funded rivals and a large digital forwarder quote free until paid |
| One of the three pools has closed | Section 232 duty moved to full customs value on 6 April 2026 |
| The licensed drawback broker is the scarce input | She would sell her book at 1.5-2 times fees |

Panel fee path: $0.4M, $5.6M and $15M in years one to three.

### F4 v1: hospital payment recovery by acquisition. Rejected.

| Objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Revenue counts only after closing | First close in month 6-9, second in month 15-20 |
| Clean books cost 8-12 times EBITDA, about 2.5-3.3 times revenue | Five sponsor-backed platforms bid in cash. $40M of revenue needs $80-100M of purchases. |
| Contracts are at will | Lenders give 2.5 times EBITDA. Clients use consent to cut the rate two to three points. |
| AI is not an edge here | Incumbents already sell it. Panel sees a 10-20% lift in recovered dollars, not three times the claims per analyst. |
| No value created | Panel: equity of $40-85M at month 36 against $63M invested |

### F5 v1: physical-work data. Rejected.

| Objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Clearing price is below cost | Program video clears at $8-25 an hour. Workplace capture costs $16-44 per accepted hour. Prices fell about 30% in the first half of 2026. |
| Funded buyers supply themselves or use gig collectors | One gig collector delivered a million hours in a month on $16M of funding |

## What round one showed

1. Organic versions in the best categories reach $12-15M of trailing revenue at month 18 in the panels' base cases. $40M was their high case at about 15% conditional odds, and 3-6% overall.
2. Bought revenue only counts after closing, which takes six to nine months. To own $40M of revenue by month 6-9 on this founder's capital, it must cost about one times revenue or less. Recovery firms and gas haulers cost two to three times revenue.
3. The lessons of the first study held: the scarce input has an owner, buyers want a record before they give sole supply, and AI is not an edge where incumbents already use it.

### F1 v2: expert quality control

| Round-one objection | Status after v2 |
|---|---|
| Sellers lack title | Resolved. Regulated client screens dropped. |
| Fixed cash to owners and inventory risk | Resolved. Corpus bought only against a first order. |
| No quarterly licence | Partly. Weekly hours match how labs buy. Orders end on 30 days' notice. |
| Larger suppliers already hold the category | Unresolved and worse. The largest marketplace sells expert agent evaluation with 200,000+ trained authors. |
| Labs supply themselves | Unresolved. Now as in-house platforms and model judges. |

| New objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| No differentiation a lab pays for | Reviewers work for several vendors. Labs measure agreement in their own tools. |
| Price squeeze | Lab rate card about $135-150 an hour. Senior practitioners want $110-140. Gross margin 22-30%. |
| Human review is a sample | One supplier describes a model-judge pass on about 90% with a 20% human spot-check |
| Reviewer count three times too low | Weekly-active experts average about five hours a week |
| Buyers want price per accepted review | Two personas |

Orders the buyer panel would place: $1.3-2.9M in year one and $4.1-9.7M in year two. $40M needs one lab to make the company a primary vendor in a domain at $10-20M.

### F6 v1: buy medical billing companies. Rejected.

| Objection | Evidence |
|---|---|
| A first purchase takes 7-11 months for a first-time buyer | Panels: 7-10% chance of closing inside six months, 38-45% inside twelve |
| One times revenue buys only onshore manual firms earning 10-13% | Realistic price 1.05-1.35 times revenue. An offshore-enabled $35M firm asks 2.3 times. AI roll-ups pay 8-11 times EBITDA in cash. |
| Equity needed is double the brief | $30-41M for $40M of revenue, not $18M |
| The book shrinks after a sale | One listed buyer lost 22-36% of acquired revenue in year one. Panels expect 12-18%. |
| Larger clients take the saving | Fee cuts of 13-27% at the sale and when agents are disclosed |
| Cost to serve falls slowly | 72-84% at month 18, not 60-65%. Records vendors ban agent logins. |
| No value created | Panel: month-36 equity of $39M against $62M invested |

A panel adviser ranked nine industries for the same play: freight forwarding with customs brokerage first, truck brokerage second, claims and benefits administration third, medical billing fourth. His reason for the first: $0.25-0.50 per dollar of gross revenue, so $40M costs $10-18M. His warning: it "satisfies a revenue target, not a wealth target".
