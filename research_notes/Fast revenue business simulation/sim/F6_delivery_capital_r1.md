## Summary verdict

This is a workable small leveraged services roll-up. It is the cheapest healthcare version of the play: revenue costs about half what it cost in hospital payment recovery (F4). It still misses both targets. The founder's $18M of equity buys one firm of about $18M of revenue, not $40M. The first close is more likely in month 8-11 than month 5-6. The AI saving by month 18 is 2-4 points of revenue, not 20-25, and the technology team costs more than the first firm earns. At month 36 the central case is about $60M of trailing revenue on about $98M of total capital, with equity worth less than the cash put in.

# Delivery personas

## Persona 1: Veteran operator, has run and integrated physician billing companies

- **Incentives:** none in the founder's deal. Would take a paid operating role with equity.
- **Cost structure of an $18-20M-revenue, mostly onshore physician billing firm [DK]:**

| Function | % of revenue | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Operations labor: charge entry and coding review, claims and rejections, posting, eligibility and authorizations, receivables follow-up, denials, patient calls, enrollment | 50% | About 190 staff: 130 onshore at $62-68K loaded, 60 offshore at $16K [loaded costs: notes, C3] |
| Client services and account managers | 8% | Hold the relationships. Do not cut |
| Supervision, quality audit, compliance | 6% | Rises 1-2 points when agents need sign-off |
| Clearinghouse, statements, practice-system licenses | 9% | Partly passed through |
| Rent, IT, insurance, finance, HR | 9% | |
| Sales and owner | 4% | |
| **Cost to serve** | **86%** | Reviewed EBITDA 12-14% after add-backs fail. Firms already 70% offshore run 20-28% and sell for more |

- **Operations hours by task and what agents can take [DK]:**

| Task | Share of operations hours | Automatable now with good access | Realized 18 months after closing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receivables follow-up and claim status | 25% | 45% (status by clearinghouse transaction or portal; phone work 20-30%) | 20% |
| Charge entry and coding review | 15% | 45% (office visits high, surgery low) | 15% |
| Denial triage and appeals | 13% | 50% | 25% |
| Payment posting | 12% | 65% of the manual remainder | 40% |
| Eligibility and prior authorization | 12% | 50% (eligibility 70%, authorization 30%) | 25% |
| Patient statements and calls | 10% | 45% | 20% |
| Claims and rejections | 8% | 50%; submission itself is already automated [notes, C3] | 25% |
| Enrollment, credentialing, refunds | 5% | 15% | 5% |
| **Weighted** | 100% | **about 48%** | **20-25%** |

- **Cost to serve after automation [DK]:** hours removed are not cost removed. Sign-off staff, severance, inference, tooling and access fees take 2-3 points back. Net saving: 2-4 points of revenue ten months after a closing, 5-7 points at 18 months, 10-12 points at 30 months. Cost to serve 81-84%, 78-80%, 73-76%. The brief's 60-65% is possible only for a firm that is fully onshore, runs clients on its own practice-system instance, and keeps its fee, and then in year four or five.
- **The comparison the brief omits:** moving half the onshore seats offshore saves about (65K - 16K) x 65 seats = $3.2M, or 16 points of revenue on an $18-20M firm, in 9-12 months with a known method. Every sponsor-backed billing platform does this and bids the saving into the price. The AI advantage over what rivals already capture is 4-6 points, and less where the firm is already offshore: an agent with inference and review costs $6-10K per seat-equivalent against a $16K offshore seat.
- **What breaks, in order [DK]:**
  1. Access. Staff work as named users inside 10-20 client practice systems. Agents on those logins break the vendors' terms [notes, C3]. Only clients on the billing firm's own instance are safe ground.
  2. The people who know the accounts. Billing staff turnover is 25-30% in a normal year and higher once agents are announced. Attrition does the headcount cut for free, but it takes the wrong people.
  3. Client concentration. The top ten clients are 35-50% of revenue and belong to the seller and two account managers.
  4. Offshore bans in some Medicaid and client contracts [notes, C3].
  5. Structural shrink. 3-5% of practices a year sell to hospitals or sponsor-owned groups and move to a central billing office.
  6. Collections dips. One bad month of cash at a practice ends the account. Clients judge on cash, not on who does the work.
- **Do clients ask for a lower fee?** Small practices: no in year one; they do not ask how the work is done. Groups above $10M of collections: yes at renewal, 0.3-0.7 point. Everyone eventually: rivals have the same agents [notes, ledger lesson 4].
- **Decision:** does not endorse the plan as written. Would be operating partner at $350K a year and 3-4% of equity.
- **Conditions:** at least 60% of target revenue on the firm's own practice-system instance; no more than two such systems; no client above 10%; seller stays 18 months; offshore conversion allowed alongside agents.
- **What would change the cost view:** a signed vendor agreement permitting automated access on the target's main system, and six months of audited touch-time data on a pilot book.

## Persona 2: Integration lead, has merged small healthcare-services firms

- **Timeline from closing [DK]:** days 0-100, change nothing clients can see; meet the top 30 clients; lock in account managers with retention pay (1-1.5% of price). Months 4-9, one function in production (posting or eligibility). Months 9-18, receivables follow-up and denial triage. More than half of volume through agents: 12-15 months after closing for clients on the firm's own instance, 18-24 months for clients on their own systems.
- **Attrition:** staff 30-40% in year one once agents are announced; 2-4 of the 15-20 account managers leave, each carrying $0.5-1.0M of revenue at risk.
- **Systems:** 10-20 practice systems, 2-3 clearinghouse accounts, a home-built work tracker, payer portal logins held by individuals. There is usually no single claims history to measure against.
- **Consents:** a stock purchase leaves payer enrollments and submitter numbers in place, which is the main reason this is faster than a new vendor's 90-150 day go-live [notes, C3]. 20-30% of client contracts need notice or consent on change of control [DK]. Business associate agreements usually require notice, not consent, to add a model supplier as subcontractor. Small practices have no AI committee, so approval is far faster than at hospitals (compare 3-9 months per hospital in [notes, F4]).
- **Diligence the founder has not budgeted:** a coding and billing compliance audit of the target, 4-6 weeks and $75-150K. A stock purchase inherits 6-10 years of claims the firm caused to be filed, and deal insurance excludes billing and False Claims Act matters [notes, F4].
- **Budget:** 5-7% of price per deal, $1.2-1.5M for the first.
- **Decision:** refuses the brief's "one or two firms totalling $35-45M within 9-12 months" if it means two closings inside six months with a first-time team. Accepts one closing, then no second before month nine after it, and only if dollar retention is above 92% at month six.
- **What would change it:** an experienced billing chief operating officer hired before the first closing.

## Persona 3: Partner manager, practice-management and records vendor that sells its own billing service

- **Incentives:** bundled billing revenue, attach rate of the house AI features, no public fight with the billing companies that still bring it practices.
- **Facts:** one major ambulatory vendor prices its platform as a percentage of collections and announced AI-native capabilities in August 2026; another advertises billing near 2.9% [notes, C3]. Partner programs cost $20-60K a year plus 10-25% revenue share or $50-150 per provider per month, with 3-9 months to certify [notes, C3].
- **Does it restrict the acquired company's access?** Not because of the sale: logins are sponsored by the practice. It does two things. It uses the change-of-control clause in the billing firm's partner or reseller agreement to re-paper it with a ban on automated logins outside the certified program. And it enforces bot detection on named-user sessions once volumes look automated: warning, then suspension of the offending logins [DK].
- **Does it poach?** Yes, selectively. It can see which practices use an outside biller from the user accounts. Practices of a registered channel partner are protected. The rest get an offer at renewal: house billing with agents included at 2.9-4%, one contract, one login. Expected loss to the acquired book: 2-4% of revenue a year, concentrated in the 25-40% of clients on vendors that sell billing [DK].
- **Decision:** no restriction on ownership; restriction on agents; standard partner program at list price; no preferred status; no bulk write access to billing tables.
- **What would change it:** the acquired firms standardizing 200+ practices onto this vendor as a reseller, or a revenue share on the automated work. Then it grants interface access and stops marketing to those accounts.
- **Regulatory lever against it:** information-blocking rules cover billing records [notes, C3]. Useful in a dispute, not in an integration plan.

## Persona 4: Senior engineer at a health-AI company, asked to be chief technology officer of the acquired firms

- **Alternatives:** stay, or join a funded specialist: $120M and $40M rounds in 2026 for AI billing companies, plus $16-27M rounds for denial and coding specialists [notes, C3].
- **Decision:** conditional yes. This is a better seat than a start-up with no clients: live claims on day one and staff to label outcomes.
- **Terms:** 5-8% of the holding company, not options in a levered subsidiary; $3-4M a year for 8-10 engineers held outside the lender's covenant group; two model suppliers from the start (one lab disabled its top models for a period in June 2026 [notes]); a target with three years of claim and remittance history in its own data store; client contract language allowing de-identified use across clients.
- **Build or buy:** would license 60-70% of the agents from vendors already selling them (coding, denial, status) and build the work-queue layer and the per-payer rules. That means every competing billing firm can buy the same tools. The saving is real and not exclusive.
- **What works now [DK]:** posting exceptions, eligibility, claim status through clearinghouse transactions at $0.08-0.15 each [notes, C3], denial triage, appeal drafts, office-visit coding suggestions with a certified coder signing.
- **What breaks [DK; list in notes, C3]:** browser agents across 15 practice systems and payer portals; multi-factor prompts; payer phone lines; outcome labels that arrive 30-120 days late; compounding error (97% per step over 12 steps leaves 31% of claims for humans [notes, C3]); two or three acquired stacks by month 24.
- **The number that matters:** the team costs $3M a year and the first firm earns $2.5M. The technology pays for itself only above about $40M of owned revenue at 8 points of saving.
- **Timeline:** first agent in production 4-6 months after closing; 20% of operations hours removed at 15-18 months.
- **What would accumulate:** denial and appeal outcomes by payer, code and argument across 150-300 practices, if contracts allow it. Clearinghouses see two orders of magnitude more [notes, C3 capital].
- **What would change it to an unconditional yes:** written interface access from the vendor behind the target's main system.

# Capital personas

## Persona 5: Senior lender, lower-middle-market services buyouts

- **Who:** non-bank cash-flow lender or licensed small-business investment fund. A $2.5M-EBITDA borrower with no fund sponsor is below most direct lenders' minimum [notes, F4].
- **Market reference:** sponsored unitranche at SOFR plus 575-675 and 4.0-4.5x in mid-2026; government-guaranteed acquisition loans about 10.75% but capped at $5M per borrower, so useful only for a deal under about $8M; subordinated small-business-fund debt of 1.0-1.5x at 10-12% [source: search summaries of advisory and trade pages, including https://www.peony.ink/blog/sbic-for-independent-sponsors and https://ctacquisitions.com/small-business-acquisition-financing-news/; originating page for each figure not confirmed]. Trade headlines in 2026 describe redemption pressure and rising defaults in private credit [source: https://www.abfjournal.com/middle-market-debt-weekly-private-credit-confronts-redemption-pressure-deepening-defaults-regulatory-spotlight, headline only].
- **Decision:** issues a term sheet on the first firm. Refuses the brief's $16M on $40M of purchases.
- **Terms on the $18M-revenue firm [DK]:**
  - $6.5M senior term loan (2.6x reviewed EBITDA) plus a $1.5M revolver against billed fees.
  - SOFR plus 675-775, about 11-12.5% all-in; 2% upfront; five years; 5% amortization; 50% excess-cash sweep.
  - Total leverage including the seller note at most 3.75x, stepping to 3.25x at month 18. Fixed-charge cover at least 1.20x. Minimum liquidity $1.5M.
  - Cash equity at least 45% of capitalization. Seller note subordinated, payments blocked above 3.0x senior.
  - No client above 10% of revenue; top ten below 45%.
  - Seller and operating chief under contract for 18-24 months; key-person default on the operator.
  - Cash-basis quality-of-earnings review and an outside coding audit before closing.
- **Why 2.6x and not 3x:** client contracts terminate on 30-90 days' notice, so contract term gets no credit. The borrower is a first-time owner. And credit committees in 2026 ask of every labor-based services loan whether AI will cut the price before it cuts the cost. A fee set as a percentage of collections, with records vendors advertising 2.9%, fails that question. Projected AI savings get no credit; the technology budget must sit outside the borrower and be funded with equity.
- **On the brief's $40M stack:** $13-14M senior, optionally $4-5M of subordinated debt at 12-13% with warrants. Total 3.4-3.6x, not the brief's 3.7x of senior and seller paper on claimed EBITDA.
- **Stress:** see the debt-service table. A 15% revenue loss takes cover below 1.0x at 2.6x leverage.
- **Later:** at $7-9M of EBITDA with two years of retention data, 3.25-3.75x with a delayed-draw line for acquisitions. Debt capacity at month 36: $20-28M.
- **What would change it:** a fund sponsor backing equity cures; a target with 25%+ margins from an existing offshore operation; three years of client-level retention above 92%.

## Persona 6: Family office, asked to co-invest $10M beside the founder's $8M

- **Incentives:** capital preservation first, 2.5-3x in five to seven years, direct deals without fund fees. Likes non-cyclical healthcare services and a founder with 44% of the equity at risk.
- **Decision:** soft commitment at the letter-of-intent stage on a named target. No blind pool. Wires at closing only.
- **Terms [DK]:**
  - $10M in the same security as the founder's $8M, with an 8% preferred return to all cash investors before any promote.
  - Founder promote: 15% above the 8% return, 20% above 2.0x, 25% above 3.0x. No closing fee to the founder. Management fee to the founder's company capped at $400K a year.
  - Board: two of five seats plus a jointly chosen independent. Consent over debt, acquisitions, the annual budget including technology, and the chief executive.
  - An operator with billing experience hired and holding equity before closing.
  - Broken-deal costs ($300-600K each) are the founder's.
  - Right to fund its share of later rounds; drag-along after year five; removal of the founder as manager for cause or after two covenant defaults.
  - Technology spending before closing is not funded. After closing it is released in tranches against measured touch-time reduction.
- **Reasons:** as the larger cheque it will not be a passive minority. It has seen roll-ups where the second deal was done to make a revenue target. The $40M and $100M revenue targets are the founder's, not the family's; it would rather own one well-run $18M firm earning $4M in year three.
- **Refuses:** funding two closings at once; any commitment that the vehicle will buy $40M by a date.
- **What would change it:** a prior exit by the founder in services; a seller rolling 20%+.

## Persona 7: Skeptical growth investor, asked to fund the later purchases

- **Incentives:** fund return; will not pay a technology multiple for outsourced billing.
- **Value created after paying for acquisitions:** see the bridge. $79M buys $9.7M of EBITDA. Three years later the company earns $5-8M, because $9.6M of revenue left and platform and technology overhead of $6.5M a year arrived before the savings did. Enterprise value $45-87M against $98M spent.
- **Exit multiple:** 9-11x EBITDA, about 1.0-1.3x revenue, to a sponsor-backed platform or a revenue-cycle outsourcer. Reference points: scaled or technology-led assets traded at 1.6x revenue and 15.7x EBITDA, 17-18x, and 4.0x revenue in 2025-26 [source: https://hitconsultant.net/2026/09/29/capstone-partners-report-ai-enabled-healthcare-it-ma/; https://pitchbook.brightspotcdn.com/c9/f2/09b3d6a646eca66150f81dea1663/h2-2025-healthcare-it-pe-update.pdf, via search summaries]; a $2.25bn-revenue outsourcer went private at 3.9x revenue and 14.5x EBITDA on a 27% margin [notes, C3 capital]. A $65M-revenue physician billing roll-up at a 10% margin is none of those. A buyer will pay for clients and nothing for the founder's agents.
- **Decision:** refuses common equity at a growth valuation. Refuses anything before the first firm shows two quarters of post-close results.
- **Terms offered:** $20-25M of preferred, 12% accruing, 1.5x minimum return, converting into 30-40%; approval of each acquisition and the technology budget. Conditions: dollar retention at or above 92% six months after closing; cost to serve down at least 4 points on the first book, audited; seller still employed.
- **What the founder owns:** $8M of $57.7M of cash equity is 14% by capital, perhaps 18-20% with promote. In the central case the preferred accretes to about $50M by month 36 on about $40M invested, which is more than the $39M of total equity value. The founder's $8M is worth zero to $8M at month 36. If year five works (below), the founder holds about $25-35M.

## What accumulates, and what does not

## Probabilities (this version)

- A first acquisition of at least $15M of revenue closes within 6 months: **10%**. Within 12 months: **45%**.
- First paid commercial delivery within 9 months: **42%**. This is the chance that any acquisition, including one under $15M, has closed and is billing clients by month 9 (about 40%), plus a small chance of a directly signed client.
- $40M of gross recognized revenue in a trailing twelve months by month 18: **2%**. On an annualized run-rate basis at month 18: 8%.
- $100M by month 36: **4%**.
- Year five if it works: revenue about $110M, roughly $90M of it bought; gross margin 42-46% (from about 35% at purchase); EBITDA 19-22%, $21-24M; enterprise value $210-265M at 10-11x; equity $145-200M after about $65M of debt, on $85-95M of cumulative cash equity. Revenue is recurring but not contracted: a percentage of clients' collections under agreements that end on 30-90 days' notice, re-earned every month.
