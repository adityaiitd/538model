# F4 v1: Hospital payment recovery, entered by buying a small firm (acquisition-led)

## What is sold
Recovery of money that insurers owe hospitals and have underpaid or denied. The company reviews every remittance against the payer contract, finds underpayments and wrongly denied claims, and files appeals. Paid a share of what is recovered.

## Standard unit
One dollar recovered.

## Price and contract
18-25% of recoveries (research notes give vendor fees of 20-35%). One-to-three-year contracts. The hospital pays nothing unless money is recovered.

## Entry step
Buy a small underpayment and denial recovery firm with $8-15M of revenue and 20-40 hospital clients, at 3-8 times EBITDA (notes), so roughly $15-40M. Funding: the founder's $5-10M, a seller note, and acquisition debt.

## Plan after closing
1. Use AI agents to work every claim, including small balances that are not worth an analyst's time today. Target: three times the claims per analyst.
2. Sell the added services to the acquired clients first, then use them as references for new health systems.
3. Buy two to three more books in 36 months.

## Founder's assumptions
- $40M by month 18: about $12M acquired, organic growth to $20M, and a second acquisition of $15-20M.
- $100M by month 36: three to four acquisitions plus organic growth.
- Precedents in the notes: Cloudmed sold for $4.1bn in 2022. R1's contingency revenue rose from $119M in 2021 to $690M in 2023.
- The first study's panels said a new vendor's sales cycle is 6-12 months and that buyers readily sign contingency contracts for aged denials at 10-15%.

## Known weak points the founder wants tested
- Are such firms for sale, and at what price, with private-equity roll-ups already buying?
- Contracts are often terminable at will. Does revenue survive a change of owner?
- Does AI raise recoveries, or do payers' own systems cancel the gain?
- Is acquisition debt available for a contingency-revenue business?
- Is this a product company or an outsourcer?
