# F3 v1 demand panel, round 1: customs duty recovery paid on results

Date of simulation: 24 November 2026. Everything below is a simulated hypothesis built from archetypes. It is not customer validation. No real person, quote, interview or order is represented.

Evidence labels: **[notes]** = contingency_recovery.md Part A. **[source: URL]** = checked in this session. **[DK]** = domain knowledge, unverified. **[sim]** = my simulated estimate for an archetype, built from the labelled rates.

Web checks used: 6 of 6 (3 searches, 3 fetches). What they added:
- Since 6 April 2026 Section 232 duty on steel, aluminum and copper derivatives is charged on the **full customs value**, not on metal content. Rates: 50% (Annex I-A), 25% (Annex I-B), a 15% all-in cap for Annex III goods to end-2027, 10% for US-melted metal. Goods with under 15% covered metal by weight are generally excluded (not in Chapters 72, 73, 74, 76). The same article warns of CF-28 requests, audits and higher False Claims Act and whistleblower exposure. [source: https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/05/what-every-multinational-should-know-about-the-new-rules-for-section-232-tariffs-on-steel-aluminum-and-copper-derivatives/] One other firm describes the 15% test as by value, so the basis is contested. [source: https://www.kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/04/united-states-new-rules-calculating-section-232-tariffs-steel-aluminum-copper.html, search snippet only]
- For exports to Canada or Mexico, drawback is the **lower of** US duties paid and the duties paid on the exported good when it enters Canada or Mexico, tested good by good. The page also refers to shipments that are "not an exportation under 1313(j)(4)". [source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/182.44] My reading [DK]: 1313(j)(4) bars substitution unused-merchandise drawback on exports to Canada and Mexico, and goods exported in the same condition under direct identification escape the lower-of cap.
- Penalties for false drawback claims: up to 3 times the revenue loss for fraud; for negligence up to 20% of the loss, then 50%, then 100% on repeats. A valid prior disclosure cuts negligence exposure to interest. [source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1593a]
- CBP moved all new drawback claims to one Center of Excellence from 22 April 2026. No current volume or backlog figure was found. [source: https://info.expeditors.com/newsflash/cbp-transitions-drawback-to-petroleum-natural-gas-and-minerals-cee]

## Persona 1. Chief financial officer, $2bn-revenue consumer-goods importer, 15% of volume re-exported to Canada and Mexico

**Who.** Reports to the board on margin and cash. Has a treasury team, a logistics director and one or two trade compliance staff. Imports about $800M of customs value a year, half from China [sim].

**Duties by authority [sim, rates from notes].**
| Authority | Basis | Annual |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary duty | 4% of $800M | $32M |
| Section 301 China | about 15% blended on $400M | $60M |
| Section 301 forced labor (from 24 Jul 2026) | 10-12.5% on covered half | $60M |
| Section 232 derivatives | housewares with metal | $10M |
| Section 122 (24 Feb-24 Jul 2026, one-off) | 10% on five months | $33M |
| IEEPA (Feb 2025-Feb 2026) | already refunded through CAPE by the existing broker at no contingency fee [notes] | $120-180M |

**Refundable share.** Theoretical drawback is 15% of eligible duties: about $63M over the five-year lookback and $22M a year going forward. Three things cut it:
- Exports to Canada and Mexico cannot use substitution. Each export must be traced to an import entry by lot or by an approved inventory method. [source: Cornell 182.44; DK for 1313(j)(4)]
- Where a Canadian affiliate or distributor is exporter of record, the importer needs that party's waiver to claim. [DK]
- Since 2025 the company has moved about a third of its Canadian volume to direct shipment or a bonded facility, so the duty is never paid. Any importer of this size with tariffs at three to four times 2024 levels has had the same advice. [DK]

**Already claiming?** Most likely yes (my estimate: 70% of companies with this profile). Section 301 at 25% has been in place since 2018 and a 15% re-export stream is the textbook case. Assumed state: a programme since 2019 through a drawback specialist or its forwarder's drawback desk at 8-12%, capturing 65-75% on Canadian lanes and little on Mexican lanes. The new Section 122 and forced-labor layers sit on the same entries, so the incumbent files them with no new sale.

**Alternatives.** The incumbent; the forwarder's own desk; Flexport, which already advances 70% of claim value within a day [notes]; a Big Four trade practice.

**Decision.** Refuses the three-year, 20% engagement. Uses the proposal to cut the incumbent's fee by two or three points. Would sign a 12-month, non-exclusive second pass limited to entries the incumbent confirms in writing it will not claim, at 12% of refunds.

**Advance.** Refused. With accelerated payment the refund arrives 3-6 weeks after filing [notes]. Five points of refund for receiving 70% a month early is 7.1% of the money advanced for about one month, which is above 60% a year. His revolver costs about 6% [DK]. He has also just received a nine-figure IEEPA refund.

**Approval time.** 3-5 months: CFO, general counsel, compliance manager, information security review.

**Data.** Would share entry data only after a security review and a data agreement. Export records for Mexico are with a second forwarder.

**18-month refund for a new vendor.** $2-6M, central $3M. In the 30% case where no programme exists the realisable lookback is $15-25M, but then he runs a tender that includes the established specialists, and a one-year-old firm wins perhaps one in six.

**What would change it.** Written evidence from the incumbent that a defined block of entries above $10M will go unclaimed and expire, plus three referenceable clients of similar size and an insurer-backed indemnity.

## Persona 2. Vice president of trade compliance, $15bn industrial manufacturer, existing drawback programme with a specialist

**Who.** Owns the customs compliance record, the drawback rulings and the relationship with CBP. Paid for zero penalties, not for refunds. Team of 15-25.

**Duties by authority [sim].**
| Authority | Annual |
|---|---|
| Ordinary duty (2.5% of $3bn) | $75M |
| Section 301 China (25% of $400M) | $100M |
| Section 232 metals, derivatives, parts (full value since 6 Apr 2026) | $250M |
| Section 301 forced labor (does not stack with 232 [notes]) | $150M |
| Section 122 one-off | $90M |
| IEEPA, already refunded | about $400M |

**Refundable share.** Manufacturing substitution drawback on roughly 25% of the $325M eligible base: about $80M a year at 2027 rates, up from about $35M a year in 2022-2024. Section 232 is closed except manufacturing drawback on qualifying derivatives since Proclamation 11021 [notes]. Exports to Canada and Mexico fall under the lower-of rule [source: Cornell 182.44].

**Already claiming.** Yes: 75-85% of the theoretical amount through a specialist with more than a decade of history on the account, at an effective 3-6%. Large programmes are advertised at "2-3% on paper" plus set-up, data and audit fees [notes]. The specialist holds the manufacturing rulings, the accelerated-payment privilege, the bond history and the claim files CBP audits against.

**Gap.** $12-20M a year in theory. Perhaps $5-8M a year is documentable, mostly in two acquired divisions on separate systems where bills of materials at production date are poor.

**Decision.** Refuses. No purchase order, no pilot on core entities. A wrong claim lands on her certification; a recovered dollar earns her nothing. A second filer on the same entries creates double-designation risk, which CBP treats as an over-claim.

**Fee she would accept.** At the contract tender in 2028: 5% or less. Before then: 10% on items the incumbent has declined in writing, for the two acquired divisions only.

**Advance.** No value. Investment-grade issuer; treasury would not permit a third party to finance a government receivable.

**Approval time.** 9-12 months: procurement tender, security review, legal, power of attorney approved by the corporate secretary.

**18-month refund for a new vendor.** $0-3M, central $0.5M.

**What would change it.** A CBP audit finding against the incumbent, or the incumbent being acquired and losing staff. Otherwise nothing before the tender.

## Persona 3. Controller, $300M-revenue apparel importer, no trade staff

**Who.** Runs accounting, cash and the bank facility. Customs is handled by the forwarder. Chief executive signs anything over $100K.

**Duties by authority [sim, apparel case].**
| Authority | Annual |
|---|---|
| Ordinary duty (about 14% of $110M; apparel rates are high) | $15M |
| Section 301 China (7.5% on a quarter of volume) | $2M |
| Section 301 forced labor | $12M |
| Section 232 | nil |
| Section 122 one-off | $4.5M |
| IEEPA, refunded mid-2026 through the forwarder | about $20M |

An electronics importer of the same size pays almost no ordinary duty and far more Section 301; totals are similar.

**Refundable share.** Exports are about 4% of volume (Canadian and European wholesale and online orders). Lookback: about $3.1M in theory, $1.5M realisable. Ongoing: $1.2M a year in theory. Destroyed stock (about 1% of volume) cannot be claimed retroactively because CBP must be notified before destruction [DK]. Corrections add about $0.2M.

**Already claiming.** Nothing. This is the profile the specialists skip: many traditional providers deprioritise accounts under $500K of annual recovery [notes].

**Alternatives.** He has had cold approaches from at least three venture-backed drawback firms and his forwarder's refund product, all at no upfront cost [notes]. He gets three quotes.

**Decision.** **Signs a contingency engagement.** Terms: 15% of refunds on the lookback and on ongoing claims, 12 months with renewal, 60 days' termination notice, no exclusivity on corrections, fee payable when cash arrives. He rejects 20% because a competing quote is lower, and rejects three years because nobody else asks for it.

**Advance.** Refused. His asset-based lender holds a lien on all receivables including customs refunds, and the credit agreement bars other secured borrowing [DK]. Lender consent would take longer than the refund. The advance would have value only during the 3-6 month wait for the accelerated-payment privilege, and he will not pay five points for that.

**Approval time.** 4-8 weeks.

**Data.** Yes. But he has no ACE portal account, the forwarder holds the commercial invoices, and nobody on his staff can pull export records. Data collection takes 6-10 weeks.

**18-month refund for a new vendor.** $1.5-2.5M, central $1.8M, of which about $1.2M is cash received by month 18. Fee: about $270K over 18 months and about $100K a year afterwards.

**What would change it.** Nothing needed to sign. To pay 20% he would need the competing quotes to disappear.

## Persona 5. Finance director, automotive and machinery parts maker exposed to Section 232

**Who.** $1.5bn revenue, imports about $450M of castings, forgings, fasteners and sub-assemblies. One compliance manager. Margins under pressure from customers who refuse tariff pass-through.

**Duties by authority [sim].**
| Authority | Annual |
|---|---|
| Section 232 derivatives and auto parts | $100M |
| Ordinary duty | $11M |
| Section 301 China | $20M |
| Section 301 forced labor (non-stacking) | $6M |
| Section 122 one-off | $6M |

Three quarters of his duty is the authority the brief itself says is mostly not drawback-eligible.

**Refundable share.**
- Drawback: 85% of his exports go to customer plants in Canada and Mexico. Under the lower-of rule a part that enters those countries duty-free returns nothing. [source: Cornell 182.44; application is my reading] Exports elsewhere are about 3% of output: about $2.6M a year in theory, and Section 232 manufacturing drawback needs a manufacturing ruling first [notes].
- Metal-content corrections: real but closing. Before 6 April 2026 many importers paid on full value because suppliers would not certify metal content [DK]. His pool is $3-8M. The 300-day correction window for the last such entry ends about 30 January 2027; protests on the latest liquidations run to about August 2027 [my arithmetic from notes]. From 6 April 2026 the duty is on full value, so metal-content valuation errors no longer arise. [source: Foley]
- New-rule corrections: annex placement (50% against 25%), the 15% weight exclusion and the US-melt rate. Worth $2-6M a year to him, but each is a legal position under heavy CBP scrutiny.

**Already claiming.** His broker and trade counsel have been writing to him about Section 232 refunds since early 2026. They bill hourly.

**Decision.** Refuses the three-year, 20% engagement. Would sign a one-off for pre-April metal-content corrections at 10% with a $400K fee cap, only if filings are made within 30 days and trade counsel signs each position. In practice he asks his existing broker first.

**Advance.** No value at five points; refunds on corrections arrive at liquidation and no privilege exists to accelerate them.

**Approval time.** 6-10 weeks.

**18-month refund for a new vendor.** $0.5-3M, central $1M. A firm starting now cannot be licensed, engaged and filing before most of the windows shut.

**What would change it.** A vendor already filing today with a named trade law firm attached and supplier metal-content evidence in hand.

## Persona 6. General counsel or compliance lead

**Incentives.** Avoid penalties, False Claims Act exposure and data loss. Gets no credit for refunds.

**Objections.**
1. **Incentive conflict.** The vendor is paid when accelerated payment arrives, 3-6 weeks after filing. The claim is not final until liquidation, which can take more than three years, and any excess must be repaid within 30 days. [notes] The vendor profits from aggressive claims; the company carries the penalty of 20-100% of the loss for negligence. [source: Cornell 1593a]
2. **Adverse findings.** A system that reads every entry line finds underpayments too. Once the company knows, it must correct or make a prior disclosure. The fee covers refunds only; the company pays the duty owed. [DK]
3. **Data.** Entry lines and bills of materials reveal suppliers, prices and designs.
4. **Balance sheet.** A start-up with $5-10M cannot back an indemnity.

**Decision.** Blocks the standard contract. Approves only with:
- 25-30% of each fee held back until the claim liquidates, with clawback of fees on any repaid amount.
- Indemnity for penalties and interest, backed by errors-and-omissions cover of at least $10M naming the company.
- Review run through outside counsel so adverse findings are privileged, with a written protocol for them.
- Power of attorney limited to drawback entries, revocable at will; corrections on consumption entries authorised case by case.
- Independent security audit, no use of company data to train models, deletion on exit.
- Termination for convenience; fee tail only on claims already filed.

**Effect on the founder.** Adds 6-10 weeks at a mid-sized company and 3-6 months at a large one. A clawback term may force the fee to be recognised later than cash under the revenue-constraint rule for variable fees [DK].

**What would change it.** A rated insurer or parent guarantee behind the indemnity, and a named law firm running the review.

## Persona 7. Existing customs broker or freight forwarder account manager

**Incentives.** Earns $30-150 per entry [DK]. A refund fee of 10-20% is the best margin on the account. A third party filing corrections on his entries implies his firm made errors and invites a claim against it.

**What he controls.** The importer owns its data and can pull entry summaries from ACE, so he cannot legally block. He does control the commercial invoices, packing lists and speed of response, and at a client with no trade staff he is the only person who knows where anything is.

**Decision.**
- **Top accounts (recovery above about $500K a year): does it himself.** Every large forwarder has a drawback desk or a partner specialist. He quotes 12-15% and tells the client that one filer for entries and refunds is safer.
- **Corrections on his own entries: blocks.** He files them himself at a per-entry charge.
- **Small accounts: refers.** He will introduce the start-up for 25% of its fee, with no exclusivity and the right to take the account back.

**Effect on the founder.** The broker channel is available only for the smallest refunds and takes a quarter of the fee. Regional brokers without a drawback desk (several hundred firms [DK]) are the realistic partners.

**What would change it.** A white-label arrangement where his firm's name is on the service and he keeps 30-40% of the fee.

## Persona 8. Trade analyst who must supply bills of materials and export records

**Incentives.** Close the quarter, finish the IEEPA refund reconciliation, keep up with the Section 232 and forced-labor rule changes. No bonus for refunds. Fears that a full entry review exposes his past classification work.

**Workload.** 60-150 hours over three months for a lookback [DK]: entry data from ACE, export filings from a different forwarder or from the customer, item-to-tariff-code mapping, bills of materials as they stood on each production date, inventory records good enough to trace or to support an accounting method. At persona 3's company this person does not exist; the work falls to a logistics coordinator.

**Decision.** Cannot sign and does not refuse. Delivers data in 8-16 weeks, not two. First claim lands 6-10 months after signature, against the brief's 4-8.

**What would change it.** The vendor extracts the data itself through read-only access (which needs IT approval, 4-8 weeks), asks for under 10 hours of his time, and an executive makes the project one of his stated objectives.

## Does anything accumulate?

No, on this panel's evidence. The accelerated-payment privilege, the bond and the manufacturing rulings belong to the claimant. The power of attorney is revocable. The lookback, which is the largest claim per client [notes], is paid once. What stays is an annual claim that any licensed competitor can take over, in a market where the largest incumbent is estimated at $25-75M of revenue after decades [notes, inference].

## Panel estimates for this version

- Probability of a first paid commercial delivery within 9 months: **60%** (a small fee from a mid-market lookback or a correction; above 90% if a licensed drawback broker is bought rather than built).
- Probability of $40M of gross recognised revenue in a trailing twelve months by month 18: **1%**. Central case $3-8M.
- Probability of $100M by month 36: **under 1%**. At a 12% fee this needs about $830M of refunds a year, more than the estimated volume of the largest incumbent [notes].
- Year five if it works: $25-40M of revenue, gross margin 55-65% after broker review and referral shares, equity value $60-120M. Revenue is repeat business, not contracted: the lookback is one-off, ongoing claims recur only while tariffs stay and the client does not restructure its supply chain, and every contract is terminable.
