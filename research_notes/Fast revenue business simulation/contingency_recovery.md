## A1. US customs duties collected by month, 2025-2026, and by legal authority

### Takeaway
Gross customs duties ran at $7-9B a month before April 2025, peaked at $33.1B in October 2025, and have settled at $22-26B a month since the IEEPA tariffs ended in February 2026. Section 232 (about $105B assessed in FY2026, mostly not drawback-eligible) is now the largest authority; Section 301 (China plus the new forced-labor action) is the largest drawback-eligible layer.

### Inferences
- Calendar 2025 gross customs duties sum to about $273.8B; Jan-Oct 2026 to about $249.6B (my addition of the Treasury monthly figures).
- Section 232 assessed in FY2026 sums to about $105.1B and in FY2025 to about $44.0B (my addition of CBP lines). With Section 301 (about $48B in FY2026 including ten weeks of the forced-labor action) these two authorities now account for roughly half of gross duties.
- Customs refunds from May to October 2026 total about $141B (my addition), against a pre-2026 baseline of $0.4-1.8B a month that contains all ordinary refunds and drawback.
- The forced-labor Section 301 action is running at roughly $8-9B a month (FY2027 to 8 Nov: $11.67B in about 5.5 weeks). Because sources describe it as drawback-eligible, it is the largest new addition to the drawback base since 2018.

### Gaps
- No primary monthly series by legal authority; CBP publishes fiscal-year totals only. Section 122 collections (24 Feb-24 Jul 2026) are not on the CBP page and were not found.
- What, if anything, formally replaced Section 122 on 24 Jul 2026 beyond the Section 301 forced-labor action was not confirmed in a primary document.

## A2. Supreme Court IEEPA case and refunds: status, mechanics, amounts, deadlines, claim trading

### Takeaway
The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs 6-3 on 20 Feb 2026 (Learning Resources v. Trump); CBP has since certified $128.4B of roughly $166B for refund through a free CSV upload in its ACE portal (CAPE). What remains is the hard residue: about $11.4B of finally liquidated entries open only to importers who sued, about $18.9B tied up in drawback claims, protests and AD/CVD entries, and under $1B stuck for missing bank details.

### Inferences
- About 77% of the $166B principal has been certified (128.4/166, slightly overstated because the certified figure includes interest). Roughly $28B has not yet been accepted into CAPE (166 minus 138.3), which matches the sum of Phase 3 ($11.4B) and the drawback/protest/AD-CVD bucket ($18.9B).
- The easy money has been paid out, mostly through importers' existing brokers at no government fee. A new entrant starting now would be chasing (a) finally liquidated entries, which need a lawsuit and therefore a law firm, (b) small or foreign importers who never filed or lack ACH enrolment, and (c) parties who paid tariffs but are not the importer of record (delivered-duty-paid shipments), who have no direct claim against CBP.
- A fee pool exists only on the residue. Even 10% on a $10B residue is $1B of fees, but it is split among thousands of brokers and law firms and is a one-off that largely ends in 2027.
- The discount market has compressed: at 50-90 cents with payment in 60-90 days, the remaining spread is a legal-risk premium on finally liquidated entries, not an operational arbitrage.

### Gaps
- No source gave contingency rates charged by brokers or law firms for IEEPA filings. No volume data for claims actually traded.
- Status of the Federal Circuit appeal and of any class-wide relief after 11 Nov 2026 is unknown. The count of importers who have not filed at all was not found.
- Whether Section 122 duties are being litigated for refund was not established.

## A3. Duty drawback (19 U.S.C. 1313): pool size, rules, eligibility, timing

### Takeaway
The last official figure is GAO's: about $1B of drawback a year (2019), with claims between $632M and $1.4B a year in 2009-2018. CBP has not published a clean drawback figure since; all refunds and drawbacks together were $7.1B in FY2023 and $7.8B in FY2024. Every "80% unclaimed" or "$10-15B unclaimed" figure traces to vendors, not CBP data. Most Section 232 duties and all AD/CVD are excluded, so the eligible base is Section 301, ordinary duties, Section 122 (Feb-Jul 2026) and the new forced-labor Section 301 duties.

Rules
- Five-year limit from date of import (19 U.S.C. 1313(r)); substitution requires the same 8-digit HTS subheading (manufacturing, 1313(b); unused merchandise, 1313(j), with a 10-digit test where the 8-digit description begins "Other"); "lesser of" rule caps substitution claims; notice of intent to export at least five working days ahead for unused goods unless waived; records kept three years after liquidation of the claim; manufacturing claims need a general or specific manufacturing ruling — [GingerControl matrix citing statute and 19 CFR 190](https://gingercontrol.com/resources/duty-drawback); [GAO-20-182](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-182.pdf)
- Eligibility by tariff (secondary sources citing proclamations and CSMS messages; verify before use) **[R]** — [GingerControl, verified Jul 2026 with later updates](https://gingercontrol.com/resources/duty-drawback); [Alliance Drawback, July 2026](https://alliancechb.com/duty-drawback/tariff-refunds/); [C.H. Robinson timeline](https://www.chrobinson.com/en-us/resources/insights-and-advisories/trade-tariff-insights/tariff-timeline/); [Flexport](https://www.flexport.com/products/duty-drawback/index.md):
  - Eligible: ordinary (MFN) duties, MPF and HMF; Section 301 China (CSMS 18-000419); Section 301 forced labor from 24 Jul 2026 (CSMS 69567203, confirmed 18 Aug 2026 after an ACE rejection was corrected); Section 301 Brazil; Section 201; Section 122 surcharge for entries 24 Feb-23/24 Jul 2026 (Proclamation 11012, CSMS 67844987); Section 232 pharmaceuticals and Section 232 timber/lumber (proclamations state "drawback shall be available").
  - Limited: Section 232 steel/aluminum/copper derivatives and auto/heavy-vehicle parts, manufacturing drawback only and only for qualifying goods (Proclamation 11021, 9 Apr 2026; CSMS 68253075). Unused-merchandise and substitution drawback stay barred.
  - Not eligible: Section 232 automobiles; Section 232 semiconductors (proclamation of 14 Jan 2026: "no drawback shall be available"); AD/CVD (19 CFR 190.3(b)); IEEPA fentanyl/border duties (EO 14193 sec. 3(g)), now refunded through CAPE instead.
  - Conflict: Flexport and LightSource list IEEPA reciprocal duties as drawback-eligible; an entry in a pending drawback claim is kept out of CAPE, so the same dollar cannot be recovered twice.
- Accelerated payment (19 CFR 190.92): the claimant applies in writing to the drawback office, describing bond coverage and estimated drawback for the next 12 months; CBP must respond within 90 days; a bond covering the estimated drawback is required; accelerated payment is not liquidation; excess must be repaid within 30 days of liquidation **[V]** — [19 CFR 190.92 via Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.92)
- Timing in practice: with accelerated payment claimants "generally receive their refunds within 21 days of claim acceptance" (14 days before 2018); CBP missed the 90-day deadline on about 60% of roughly 600 privilege applications received Feb 2018-Jul 2019; full desk review "could take more than 3 years"; claims auto-liquidate one year after filing if all designated import entries are final, unless extended **[V, dated 2019]** — [GAO-20-182](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-182.pdf)
- Vendor timing claims (2026) **[E]**: accelerated payment in 30-45 days or 4-6 weeks; without it 1-3 years, up to 4; privilege applications 3-6 months; first claim 4-8 months after starting; "9-12 months" legacy processing — [GingerControl provider guide, updated 12 Oct 2026](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/duty-drawback-service-how-to-choose-provider); [LightSource](https://lightsource.ai/blog/duty-drawback-practical-guide-recovering-import-duties-2026); [Flexport](https://www.flexport.com/solutions/claim-tariff-refunds/index.md); [Zollback](https://www.zollback.com/blog/tariff-refunds-for-smbs)
- Protest of a denied drawback claim within 180 days of liquidation — [GAO-20-182](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-182.pdf)

### Inferences
- The primary data bound the pool. Drawback paid cannot exceed total customs-duty refunds of $6-7.5B a year (FY2020-FY2025), and the last official figure was about $1B. A figure of $2-4B a year for FY2023-FY2025 is plausible but unverified. "$15B unclaimed" would imply eligible drawback of roughly $19B a year, more than 20% of all FY2024 duties, which is not credible for that period.
- The eligible base did grow in 2025-2026: Section 301 China runs at about $30B a year and the forced-labor action at $8-9B a month. Section 232, the largest authority, is mostly closed to the unused-merchandise and substitution claims that made up most pre-2019 volume.
- Fee arithmetic: at a 10-20% success fee, $40M of fees needs $200-400M of drawback paid to clients, which is 20-40% of the 2019 national total or perhaps 5-15% of a $3-4B pool. Charter Brokerage's claimed $4.1B recovered since 2018 (about $0.5B a year, see A4) shows the scale an incumbent leader reached over decades.
- Cash timing per new client is slow at the start. Privileges and bonds are applied for per claimant, so a first cheque realistically arrives 4-8 months after signing even when the filing itself is automated. The five-year lookback means the first claim is the largest; a new firm's revenue is front-loaded per client and then falls to an annual run rate.
- Section 122 duties (10% on most imports for five months) are a dated one-off drawback pool: entries from Feb-Jul 2026 can be claimed until 2031.

### Gaps
- No official drawback paid figure or claim count for FY2022-FY2026 was found. The DHS FY2025 report was too large to retrieve; CBP's trade statistics page does not show drawback.
- No primary estimate of unclaimed drawback exists in the sources reviewed.
- Current CBP processing times for privilege applications and liquidation (2025-2026) rest on vendor statements only.
- Bond pricing was not found.

## A4. Who files drawback: incumbents, large firms, start-ups; fees and scale

Drawback is filed by licensed customs brokers on power of attorney. Incumbents quote custom contingency fees; published ranges are 10-20% or 15-30% of the refund, falling for large programmes. The venture-backed entrants (Pax, Caspian, Zollback) have each raised about $5M and the only disclosed traction is Zollback's $10M of refunds processed and $1M+ of bookings, so no AI-native drawback firm has shown eight-figure revenue.

## A5. Other customs recovery work paid on results

### Takeaway
Post-summary corrections and protests are the mechanical routes for classification, valuation and exclusion refunds, with hard time limits (300 days from entry; 180 days from liquidation). No sourced pool sizes or fee norms were found for these lines.

### Cited Findings
- Post-summary correction: must be filed within 300 days of entry or 15 days before scheduled liquidation, whichever is first; entry must be paid and unliquidated; another authorised filer may correct an entry with the importer's authorisation; after liquidation the routes are protest or prior disclosure **[V]** — [CBP, Post Summary Correction](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/entry-summary/post-summary-correction)
- Liquidation normally about 314 days after entry; protest window 180 days after liquidation **[R]** — [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/tax/update-on-cbp-ieepa-refund-progress-and-new-orders-from-the-us-court-of-international-trade)
- Flexport sells four refund programmes together: drawback, post-summary corrections, protests and IEEPA claims — [Flexport](https://www.flexport.com/solutions/claim-tariff-refunds/index.md)
- Foreign-trade zones are permitted under privileged foreign status for the Section 301 Brazil and Section 338 Canada actions; the Section 301 forced-labor action has product and country exclusions — [C.H. Robinson timeline](https://www.chrobinson.com/en-us/resources/insights-and-advisories/trade-tariff-insights/tariff-timeline/)
- Total non-IEEPA customs refunds of all kinds (drawback, protests, corrections, exclusions) were $6-7.5B a year in FY2020-FY2025 **[V]** — [Treasury MTS Table 4](https://api.fiscaldata.treasury.gov/services/api/fiscal_service/v1/accounting/mts/mts_table_4?filter=record_calendar_month:eq:09,classification_desc:eq:Customs%20Duties,record_date:gte:2019-09-01&page%5Bsize%5D=100&sort=record_date)

### Inferences
- With duties at $290-320B a year instead of $85B, every percentage point of misclassification or overvaluation is worth three to four times what it was in 2024, and the Section 232 derivative rules (duty on metal content, clause-13 conditions) create correctable errors at scale. This is the largest unsized pool in Part A.
- The 300-day correction window forces continuous engagement rather than a five-year lookback, so this work behaves like a subscription audit, not a one-off recovery.

### Gaps
- No data found on first-sale valuation savings, FTZ savings, exclusion-request success rates, or fees for classification/valuation recovery. Not searched for lack of budget.

## A6. What a new entrant needs: licences, CBP approvals, time

### Takeaway
A corporate customs broker licence needs one individually licensed officer; the exam runs twice a year with 12-22% pass rates, so hiring a licensed broker is the practical route. Drawback documents may be signed by the claimant's own officers or by a licensed broker with power of attorney, and at least one start-up prepares claims while a partner broker files, so operating under another broker's licence is possible.

### Cited Findings
- Individual licence: US citizen, 21 or older, not a federal employee, pass the Customs Broker License Examination, apply and be approved. Corporate licence: at least one licensed officer, partner or associate; lacking one for more than 120 days revokes the licence **[V]** — [CBP, Becoming a Customs Broker](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/becoming-customs-broker)
- Exam: 80 questions, fourth Wednesday of April and October; pass rates before appeals 12% (Oct 2025), 22% (Apr 2026), 13% (listed for Oct 2026); scores within 2-3 weeks **[V]** — [CBP, exam notice](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/license-examination-notice-examination)
- Who may sign drawback entries: company officers, partners, employees with power of attorney, or "a licensed customs broker with a power of attorney" (19 CFR 190.6) **[V]** — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.6)
- Filing channel: claims go through ABI into ACE as type 47 entries; providers are either "direct filers" with their own ABI filer code or "handoff filers" whose partner broker files; GingerControl uses an unnamed filing partner **[R]** — [GingerControl provider guide](https://gingercontrol.com/blog/duty-drawback-service-how-to-choose-provider)
- Privileges: accelerated payment and waiver of prior notice are applied for on paper and reviewed manually; CBP has 90 days and has often missed it; a bond at least equal to the claims is needed for automatic accelerated payment **[V, 2019]** — [GAO-20-182](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-182.pdf); [19 CFR 190.92](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/190.92)
- Caspian is described as both a licensed broker and a CBP-approved software vendor within about a year of founding **[R]** — [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250729878632/en/Caspian-Launches-AI-Powered-Global-Trade-Advisory-Platform-with-5.4M-Seed-Funding-Led-by-Primary-Venture-Partners/)

### Inferences
- Licensing is a hiring problem, not a multi-year barrier: Pax, Caspian and Zollback were all filing within 12-18 months of founding. The slow steps are per-client: privilege applications, bonds, manufacturing rulings and export-record collection.
- Acquiring a small licensed drawback broker with existing privileges, rulings and client history would shorten time to first cash more than building.

### Gaps
- Time from passing the exam to licence issuance, broker application fees, ABI certification time, and bond cost were not retrieved.
