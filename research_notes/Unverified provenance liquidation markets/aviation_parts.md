# Aviation: used parts, engines and aircraft whose value depends on documentary provenance (as of 23 Nov 2026)

Verification key used throughout: **(read)** = I opened the page or document this session. **(snippet)** = taken from a search-engine summary; the page itself was not opened, so treat wording and numbers as provisional.

Headline for the thesis: demand is acute and records gaps do destroy value, but the largest value loss (life-limited parts with broken back-to-birth trace) is mostly not curable by inspection or AI. It is curable only by finding the missing document. No source gave a dollar or percentage estimate of value destroyed by records gaps. The bottleneck traders report in 2025-2026 is feedstock supply, not unverifiable inventory.

## 1. Size: USM market, aftermarket, teardown volumes, 2025-2026 shortage

### Takeaway
USM is roughly a $6-9bn a year market (syndicated estimates, wide spread), sitting inside a supply crisis that IATA and Oliver Wyman priced at more than $11bn of extra airline cost in 2025. Demand is very strong; the scarce thing is aircraft and engines to tear down, and the listed traders say so in their results.

### Inferences

- The spread of USM estimates ($2.9bn to $8.6bn) means the "market size" figure should be quoted as a range. None of the publishers is an aviation specialist such as Oliver Wyman, Alton, IBA or Naveo.

## 2. The discount: full records versus incomplete or missing records

### Takeaway
The direction is well documented and extreme for life-limited parts (a broken trace can take a disk to scrap value and an engine to "worthless"), but I found no published price schedule, percentage discount, share of parts scrapped for records reasons, or dollar estimate of value destroyed. Every quantified claim on this point would be an assumption.

### Cited Findings
- IBA (2018, dated): back-to-birth traceability is "of paramount importance for owners, lessors and lessees"; "any gaps or issues in documentation can have a significant impact on the residual value of the engine" and "can render the engine worthless". Lessees who cannot account for parts may have to "pay a premium to replace any parts that are unaccounted for". No percentages given. (read) — [IBA](https://www.iba.aero/resources/articles/what-to-look-for-when-valuing-an-engine-march-2018/)
- A records-review firm states a CFM56 life-limited part with a broken trace is "discounted to scrap value or held out of the sale" until the missing document is recovered; if a disk sheet cannot be found and cycles cannot be substantiated, the owner bears the cost of the life the records will not support. Vendor page, no figures. (read) — [ee-aero](https://ee-aero.com/aircraft-records/aircraft/cfm56-llp-trace-records-review/)
- Coalition report (Oct 2024): regulations require operators to document the current life status of installed life-limited parts, but "most LLP transactions today will require documentation of back-to-birth (BtB) traceability for the entirety of the lifespan of the LLP". (read) — [Aviation Supply Chain Integrity Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- IATA's life-limited parts guidance (2020, dated): most transactions feature back-to-birth traceability; lessors prefer new life-limited parts because they present no trace issue. (snippet) — [IATA LLP traceability guidance](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/b94a0e7f14694efe8b72ca1b73052f05/llp-traceability-1st-ed-2020.pdf)
- A 2019 industry presentation hosted by the Aviation Suppliers Association says that without proper documentation value "plummets"; no percentages. (snippet) — [ASA conference presentation](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/ASA/files/ccLibraryFiles/Filename/000000001166/Mon%20GS%20-%20Weinberg.pdf)
- A maintenance-software vendor states that if back-to-birth traceability cannot be established the part must be treated as having zero life remaining. Marketing material, not regulation. (snippet) — [Oxmaint](https://oxmaint.com/industries/aviation-management/life-limited-parts-llp-tracking-back-to-birth-cmms)
- Norton Rose Fulbright: provenance and documentation strongly affect value; owners with well-kept records get higher prices; there is a trend toward back-to-birth ownership trace for major parts beyond life-limited parts. (read) — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/c4f83013/endof-life-consignment-agreements-key-considerations)
- Non-records causes of discount in consigned airline stock (ASA, 2016, dated): airline-specific engineering orders the market does not recognise, which may force re-overhaul or de-modification; airline serviceability declarations outside buyers will not accept, leading to downgraded condition; untracked shelf life; operator-produced parts mixed into stock; "questionable trace documentation". (read) — [ASA, Consignment Refinement](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/consignment-refinement)
- FAA defines scrap to include used parts with no maintenance history. (snippet) — [FAA Order 8120.11](https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/order/8120.11.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two different discounts exist and should not be merged. For life-limited parts the discount is close to binary (full value or scrap). For non-life-limited rotables and expendables the discount comes from condition and tag status ("as removed" versus overhauled with a fresh release certificate), which is a normal repair-and-certify margin that incumbents already capture.
- The absence of any published discount table after targeted searching suggests pricing is bilateral and opaque. That is a diligence finding in itself: the founder would need broker quotes or transaction data to size the spread.

### Gaps
- No documented price pairs (same part number, with and without trace) were found.
- No estimate of the share of parts or engines scrapped or discounted for records reasons, and no estimate of dollars destroyed. I looked; none surfaced.
- "As removed" versus overhauled price ratios: not found in an openable source.

## 3. Are the records digital? Cost and duration of records reviews

### Takeaway
Release certificates are still mostly paper or flat PDF, eighteen years after the FAA allowed electronic forms, and the industry's own post-scandal report asks for OCR of legacy records. Hard numbers on the cost and duration of records reviews are thin and mostly from vendors.

### Cited Findings
- FAA has authorised electronic Form 8130-3 since 2009, but "adoption of digital ARCs across the industry remains slow, with most ARCs being paper". Brokers and sales offices use digital certificates "primarily in PDF form", which often does not reach receiving inspection or records retention. Use of digital verification tools "remains very limited". (read, report dated Oct 2024) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- GE Aerospace began digitising more than 18 million MRO records going back to 2015 in 2023, and uses AI to check certificate fields against other records; it planned a digital-signature pilot from late 2024. Boeing is piloting a digitally signed 8130-3 with a large airline customer. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Bluetail (business and general aviation records) said in August 2026 it had digitised more than 30 million aircraft records. (snippet) — [EIN Presswire](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/936718401/bluetail-surpasses-30-million-aircraft-records-digitized-marking-milestone-year-for-ai-powered-aviation-platform)
- Lease returns: sources say gathering and accepting technical records takes from a few weeks to several months, with planning advised 6-9 or even 18-24 months before lease end; one says most lease-return cost overruns come from the accuracy and completeness of technical records. Attribution among the sources is from search summaries and should be rechecked. (snippet) — [Sofema](https://sassofia.com/blog/aircraft-lease-return-records-review-best-practice-considerations/); [Acumen Aviation](https://www.acumen.aero/blogs/aircraft-lease-redelivery-conditions-explained-why-end-of-lease-disputes-happen)
- One vendor anecdote: rebuilding records across three MRO vendors took 19 working days, cost $46,000 in extended reserves and triggered a $28,000 return-condition penalty. Single marketing example. (snippet) — [Oxmaint](https://oxmaint.com/industries/aviation-management/aircraft-lease-return-insurance-documentation-cmms)
- An academic study applied lean methods to Airbus A320 pre-redelivery records review; quantitative results not retrieved. (snippet) — [University of Limerick](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/from-turbulence-to-efficiency-optimizing-the-airbus-a320-pre-rede/)

### Inferences
- The raw material for an AI records product exists in volume (paper and scanned PDF), and the industry has formally asked for it (recommendations 5, 7 and 8 of the coalition report).
- The same report shows the engine makers building this in-house and proposing an industry database they would feed, which is a competitive threat to a start-up as much as a tailwind.

### Gaps
- No percentage split of paper, scanned PDF and structured digital records was found.
- No independent benchmark for the price of an engine records audit or lease-return records project (per engine, per aircraft, or per page).

## 4. Fraud and trust: AOG Technics and the response

### Takeaway
AOG Technics sold about £6.9m of parts on forged certificates and caused about £39.3m of losses, so bad paper cost the industry roughly 5.7 times the value of the parts. The response made buyers more cautious about small unknown brokers, including accredited ones, and pushed the industry toward digital certificates and vendor identity checks.

### Cited Findings
- UK Serious Fraud Office, 23 Feb 2026: Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala, 38, sentenced to 4 years 8 months after pleading guilty to fraudulent trading in December 2025. AOG Technics sold more than 60,000 engine parts worth £6.9m with forged release certificates between January 2019 and July 2023, mostly for the CFM56. Company revenue was more than £7.7m over four years, about 90% from fraud. Estimated losses to airlines and manufacturers exceeded £39.3m. Named victims include American Airlines and Ethiopian Airlines. He ran it from a home office in Surrey, altered genuine certificates, created false shipment memos and invented fake staff. (read) — [SFO press release](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sfo-secures-4-year-prison-sentence-for-aircraft-parts-fraud)
- American Airlines did not buy directly from AOG but found 28 engines affected and lost about £21m. (snippet) — [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/aircraft-propulsion/aog-technics-fraudster-gets-prison-sentence)
- Detection: in June 2023 TAP Air Portugal contacted Safran about CFM56 parts that looked older than the paperwork said. Fewer than 1% of CFM engines in service were affected. Forged certificates carried fake signatures of real Safran employees and fake LinkedIn profiles with stock photos. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- AOG Technics was accredited. In April 2024 the FAA removed Transonic Aviation Consultants, the body that accredited AOG, from its accreditation programme after observing two Transonic audits in January 2024, and withdrew the TAC-2000 standard. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf); [FAA programme page](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/safety/programs/AC00-56)
- Coalition (formed 22 Feb 2024; Airbus, Boeing, GE Aerospace, Safran, airlines; co-chairs Robert Sumwalt and John Porcari) issued 13 unanimous, voluntary recommendations on 9 Oct 2024: (1) buy primarily from distributors accredited under FAA AC 00-56B or EASA 2019/009/R; (2) feedback loop between installers and accreditors; (3) industry oversight body for accreditors; (4) database of accredited vendors with know-your-customer style identity verification; (5) expand digital release certificates and authentication tools; (6) standard for required document fields; (7) digitise existing and past parts documents with OCR; (8) software to verify certificate fields against manufacturer and airline databases through APIs; (9) voluntary industry database of back-to-birth documentation, including for non-serialised parts; (10) training on unapproved parts; (11) verification and auditing of scrap and recycling vendors; (12) faster industry sharing of suspected unapproved parts; (13) new technology: digital unique identifiers, digital twins, blockchain. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf); [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/supply-chain/industry-coalition-recommends-13-actions-avoid-unapproved-parts)
- The report warns that scrap parts not properly mutilated "may be reworked or camouflaged to appear as used serviceable material (USM) by bad actors". (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- 2025-2026 regulator notices: FAA unapproved parts notifications on 3D-printed cabin items sold online (Aug 2025), Superior Panel Technology parts, and a 2026 notice on products sold without production approval; a 2026 Special Airworthiness Information Bulletin (SAIB 2026-11) refers to stolen turbofan engine parts. (snippet) — [FAA notifications index](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/safety/programs/sups/upn); [SAIB 2026-11 copy](https://www.aviation.govt.nz/assets/aircraft/airworthiness-directives/other-authorities/SAIB-2026-11.pdf)
- Follow-up: working groups continue the coalition's work. (snippet) — [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/supply-chain/working-groups-advance-parts-integrity-work)

### Inferences
- A start-up whose pitch is "we take material with weak paperwork and make it saleable" has the same outline, from a buyer's quality department's point of view, as the fraud the industry just spent three years cleaning up. The trust hurdle for that specific pitch is higher after 2023 than before.
- The same events create demand for certificate verification as a service to buyers (recommendation 8). That is a software sale to receiving-inspection teams, not a trading margin.

### Gaps
- Details of SAIB 2026-11 (which engine parts, how many) were not opened.
- No count of implementations of the 13 recommendations as of late 2026, and no confirmation that the proposed vendor database or back-to-birth database exists yet.
- The 2024 titanium falsified-documents case (Boeing and Airbus supply chain) did not surface in searches this session.

## 6. Business model norms: consignment, margins, capital

### Takeaway
Consignment is a standard structure and lets a consignee trade without buying inventory; quoted commissions run from about 5% on high-value parts to 30% on low-value parts, with the consignee usually recovering teardown, storage and repair costs first. Winning consignments requires warehouse, repair capability, sales reach and credibility, so "no inventory capital" is not the same as "no capital".

### Cited Findings
- One consignment provider (TurbineWorks) quotes sales commission from 5% for high-value parts to 30% for low-value parts, with a worked example at 20%. (snippet) — [TurbineWorks](https://turbineworks.com/consignments.html)
- ASA commentary (2016, dated) poses "Is a simple 70/30 split OK?" as a negotiating question (70% to the owner), and lists terms to settle: freight, insurance, who pays for overhaul before sale, scrapping, reporting and audit rights, recall of parts, end-of-term handling. It suggests the owner file a UCC lien in case the consignee goes bankrupt. (read) — [ASA, Consignment Refinement](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/consignment-refinement)
- Norton Rose Fulbright: the consignee earns a commission on sale or lease revenue; it typically recovers teardown, storage and repair costs before the owner is paid; commission level depends on scope from full turnkey to specific component sales; title usually passes owner to consignee to end user; owners should check the consignee's export-control safeguards. (read) — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/c4f83013/endof-life-consignment-agreements-key-considerations)
- An older trade article describes splits "front weighted in favor of the consignor until further balanced as revenue milestones are achieved". (snippet, 2015) — [Jetrader, Autumn 2015](https://nxtbook.com/naylor/ISTS/ISTQ0315/index.php?startid=49)
- Three structures in use: outright purchase of the aircraft, disassembly for a fixed fee with the owner keeping the parts, or consignment with shared proceeds. (snippet) — [VREF](https://vref.com/news/why-aircraft-tear-downs-require-strategic-partnerships/)
- Suppliers weigh benefits and challenges of consignment stock (Aviation Week; article not opened). Duncan Aviation runs a long-standing business-aviation parts consignment programme. (snippet) — [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/supply-chain/aftermarket-suppliers-weigh-benefits-challenges-consignment-stock); [Duncan Aviation](https://www.duncanaviation.aero/debrief/2023/Spring/making-money-with-aircraft-parts-consignment)
- Scale reference: AerSale holds about $364m of inventory against $335m of revenue, an inventory turn below 1 on a cost basis. (snippet) — [AerSale FY2025 release](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/593410/aersale-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025-results)

### Inferences
- At a 20-30% commission, a consignee handling $10m of sales a year earns $2-3m before warehouse, quality staff and repair costs. The model supports a small business but the "much higher resale price" in the thesis accrues mostly to the owner under a consignment split, unless the intermediary buys the asset outright and takes the records risk itself.
- Slow turns are normal. Consigned stock that does not move goes to scrap after a period, per the ASA commentary.

### Gaps
- No survey of consignment splits, sell-through rates, time to sell, payment terms or typical ticket sizes was found. The 5-30% range rests on one provider's website.
- Broker margins on back-to-back trades: not found.

## 7. Legal start: certificates, accreditation, who can re-certify, and whether lost trace can be restored

### Takeaway
A parts trader needs no FAA certificate, and accreditation is voluntary on paper but close to mandatory in practice. A Part 145 repair station can issue a fresh release certificate for work it performs, but that certificate does not recreate a life-limited part's history. Where the missing item is the life history of a life-limited part, the loss is permanent unless the original document is found.

### Cited Findings
- The FAA chose voluntary oversight of distributors in 1993 and published Advisory Circular 00-56 in 1996. A distributor is "any person selling or transferring parts for installation in appliances or type-certificated aircraft, aircraft engines, or propellers". Accepted standards as of April 2024: ASA-100, ISO 9001, AS9100/AS9110/AS9120. A draft AC 00-56C exists. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf); (snippet) [FAA draft AC 00-56C](https://www.faa.gov/aircraft/draft_docs/afs_ac/AC_00-56C_Coord_Copy.pdf)
- Accredited suppliers are audited twice every three years (report graphic); ASA-100 certificates mostly run three years with a yearly self-audit; accreditation takes effect only once listed in the programme database, which ASA updates within up to 10 business days. An ASA fee schedule shows $8,200 plus travel per audit, tier unclear. (read for audit cadence; snippet for fees) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf); [ASA accreditation steps](https://aviationsuppliers.org/?bid=26); [ASA fee schedule](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/ASA/files/ccLibraryFiles/Filename/000000000877/rev%204.1%20ASAAP%20Contract.pdf)
- Europe: EASA Decision 2019/009/R recognises ASA-100, EN/AS9120, EASO 2012 and FAA AC 00-56 as supplier quality systems for Part 145 organisations, which must have component acceptance procedures. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Accreditation standards require that parts be "traceable to a prior source (or if LLP, include back-to-birth (BtB) traceability)". (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Who may issue release certificates for new, used or repaired parts: a Part 145 repair station, a Part 121 or 135 air carrier with an approved continued airworthiness programme, or a production approval holder. A distributor cannot. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Back-to-birth traceability is not an FAA legal requirement; FAA Chief Counsel opinions from 1992 to 2009 say so. It is "a commercial norm for life-limited parts" and has not been the norm for expendables. Regulations "do not limit the forms of evidence that may be used" to establish airworthiness; invoices, shipping tickets and markings can evidence production by an approved manufacturer (citing AC 20-62E), and the installer must still assess condition. (read; ASA bulletin dated Aug 2019) — [ASA member bulletin](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/ASA-Member-Bulletin---August-2019---Back-to-Birth-Traceability)
- FAA in the 2002 final rule on life-limited parts: regulations do not require back-to-birth records to determine life status; a trail back to origin would be needed only where the operator's records are too incomplete to make an accurate determination. (snippet) — [Federal Register, 15 Jan 2002](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/01/15/02-492/safe-disposition-of-life-limited-aircraft-parts)
- A used life-limited part without the status record required by 14 CFR 91.417(a)(2)(ii) or the transfer record required by 43.10 has "no evidentiary basis" to be treated as within limits; a repair station signature on Form 8130-3 certifies only the work described on the form, not earlier or remaining life. Secondary vendor source; regulation text not opened this session. (snippet) — [FileFlo](https://www.getfileflo.com/blog/life-limited-parts-records-requirements)
- Workarounds exist but cost life: "conservative calculations approved for some types to work around gaps in records which are at the expense of component total life and consequently residual value". (snippet; LinkedIn article) — [Ravi Kumar, LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/llp-back-to-birth-traceability-ravi-kumar)
- For non-life-limited parts with incomplete records, a blog describes the common route: a repair station inspects and tests the part and, if it meets standards, issues a new Form 8130-3. Secondary source; the page returned a 404 when opened. (snippet) — [Flying411](https://flying411.com/blog/how-to-verify-traceability-and-certification-of-used-aircraft-parts/246)
- Even for new parts, weak paperwork causes friction: US repair stations holding both FAA and EASA approval have had the E100 inspection form (used when a new part arrives without an 8130-3) rejected by FAA inspectors and EASA representatives, despite a September 2016 FAA letter accepting it. (read; article dated May 2022) — [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/safety-ops-regulation/us-repair-stations-face-new-challenge-parts-traceability)

### Inferences
- Legal answer to the critical question, in three cases. (a) Non-life-limited rotable or repairable part with missing or stale tag: curable. A Part 145 shop with the right rating inspects, tests or overhauls and issues a fresh certificate; this is ordinary business for every USM trader. (b) Life-limited part with a gap in cycle history: not curable by inspection, AI or a new certificate, because physical inspection cannot count past cycles. It is curable only by locating the missing record, or partly by a manufacturer-approved conservative life assumption that sacrifices value. (c) Part of unknown origin (cannot be shown to come from an approved manufacturer): legally possible to evidence through other documents, commercially very hard, and after AOG Technics most buyers will refuse.
- A new entrant can lawfully trade from day one and can contract a Part 145 shop for re-certification. Nothing found shows how long such a partnership takes to set up, and engine shop capacity is 93-95% utilised in 2026, so shop slots are themselves scarce.
- Law is more permissive than the market. The discount comes from buyer and lessor contract standards, which a start-up cannot change by being legally correct.

### Gaps
- I could not open 14 CFR 43.10 on eCFR (redirected) or FAA AC 20-62E (403). The regulatory statements above rest on the ASA bulletin, the coalition report and secondary summaries; confirm against primary text.
- No source on time or cost to obtain ASA-100 or AS9120 from scratch (fee snippet only), nor on whether accreditors require a trading history.
- No source on how quickly a new trader can sign a Part 145 partner, or on manufacturer programmes for re-lifing parts with record gaps (GE's TRUEngine programme surfaced by title only).
- Status of AC 00-56C (draft or final) as of November 2026 not confirmed.

## 8. What AI on records plus mobile inspection can and cannot fix

### Takeaway
AI can find gaps, reconcile cycle counts, detect forged or inconsistent certificates and speed up document recovery. It cannot manufacture a missing historical record, and a mobile inspection cannot establish accumulated cycles on a life-limited part. The work that restores value is document retrieval from former operators and shops, which is a relationships-and-persistence task with a closing time window.

### Cited Findings
- Typical breaks in a CFM56 life-limited-part trace: trace ends at an early shop visit with no surviving disk sheet; status-sheet cycles do not reconcile with module build records; release paperwork names a different serial number; an installation record has no matching removal record; remaining cycles disagree with accumulated cycles. (read) — [ee-aero](https://ee-aero.com/aircraft-records/aircraft/cfm56-llp-trace-records-review/)
- Curable, per the same source: recovering missing disk sheets, birth records or release documents from the shops that built the engine; matching removal and installation entries across two shop packages; reconciling cycle counts per module build. Often the birth record sits at a shop the current owner has never dealt with. (read) — [ee-aero](https://ee-aero.com/aircraft-records/aircraft/cfm56-llp-trace-records-review/)
- Not curable: a disk sheet that cannot be found where cycles cannot be substantiated. Recovery gets harder each year as build-shop record retention lapses. A records review does not physically inspect disks, re-life parts or make airworthiness determinations. (read) — [ee-aero](https://ee-aero.com/aircraft-records/aircraft/cfm56-llp-trace-records-review/)
- AI detection of bad certificates is already in production at GE Aerospace: it examines certificate fields and flags mismatches for extra scrutiny. The AOG forgeries were caught because a part looked older than its paperwork and Safran confirmed the certificate was fake; signatures and formatting were inconsistent. (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Non-serialised parts are the hardest to trace because proving a document belongs to an unserialised article is difficult. (read) — [ASA member bulletin](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/ASA-Member-Bulletin---August-2019---Back-to-Birth-Traceability)
- Airline-specific modifications and serviceability declarations not recognised by the wider market can be fixed by re-overhaul or de-modification, at a cost. (read) — [ASA, Consignment Refinement](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/consignment-refinement)

### Inferences
- Curable by software plus a repair-station partner: disorganised but complete records (paper, scans, multiple shops); arithmetic and serial-number inconsistencies that resolve once all documents are matched; missing or expired release tags on non-life-limited parts; airline-specific status that needs re-certification to a manufacturer standard.
- Not curable: lost cycle history on life-limited parts; missing incident or accident clearance where the prior operator will not or cannot sign; parts with no evidence of approved manufacture; anything where the only fix is a document that no longer exists.
- The "curable" bucket is where incumbents already operate, so the value uplift there is shared with competitors. The "not curable" bucket is where the scrap-price discount is largest. The thesis needs a third bucket, "recoverable but nobody bothered", and no source quantifies it.

### Gaps
- No data on what fraction of broken traces are eventually recovered, or how long recovery takes.
- No source on whether lessors or insurers accept AI-assembled trace packages without human sign-off.

## 9. Buyer behaviour: will airlines, lessors and MROs buy from a new intermediary?

### Takeaway
After AOG Technics the stated industry direction is to buy from accredited distributors, verify vendor identity and add airline on-site audits. I found no source giving the time a new vendor needs to get onto approved-vendor lists, so the "a year or more" question stays open, but every signal points toward more friction for newcomers, not less.

### Cited Findings
- Recommendation 1: airlines, MROs and engine makers should "primarily utilize" distributors accredited under FAA AC 00-56B or EASA 2019/009/R. Recommendation 4: a vendor database with identity verification modelled on know-your-customer rules in finance, because "bad actors, as in the AOG Technics case, utilize publicly available information about vendors and employees to fool buyers". (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- American Airlines case study in the report: proposes an organisation of air carriers that performs on-site audits of parts suppliers and lists those that pass, reinforcing "know your supplier". "Today, many aviation industry organizations have established their own internal vendor screening processes." (read) — [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- ASA itself says a listing on the ASA-100 database "should not be the only factor" in approving a company. (snippet) — [ASA-100 documents](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/ASA-100-Documents)
- AOG Technics held accreditation and still sold forged-paper parts for four and a half years, and American Airlines was hit without buying from AOG directly. (read) — [SFO](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sfo-secures-4-year-prison-sentence-for-aircraft-parts-fraud); [Coalition report](https://www.aviationsuppliers.org/asa/files/cclibraryfiles/filename/000000005402/Aviation%20Supply%20Chain%20Integrity%20Coalition%20-%20Report%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- Counter-signal: shortage pushes buyers to widen sourcing. IATA asks for more access to USM from teardowns, and teardown firms report much longer lead times for USM. (read) — [IATA, 24 June 2026](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/06-24-urgent-action-needed-to-ease-engine-mro-bottlenecks/); [Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/mro/supply-chain/what-next-aviation-teardown-segment)

### Inferences
- A new intermediary probably sells first to other traders and brokers (who then sell to airlines), giving up margin, until it has accreditation and an audit history. That is an inference from the accreditation structure, not a sourced fact.
- An unknown company offering material whose paperwork it "repaired" faces the worst possible buyer reception in the current climate. Selling verification to buyers, or partnering with an established accredited trader as the sales channel, would face less resistance.

### Gaps
- No source on approved-vendor-list onboarding time at airlines, lessors or MROs, or on minimum trading history. This needs primary interviews with airline supplier-quality managers.
- No evidence found of buyers accepting or rejecting AI-verified trace packages from third parties.
- Lessor behaviour specifically (acceptance of engines with reconstructed trace at lease return) not found.
