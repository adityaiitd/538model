## 1. Which companies hit $100M within 12-18 months and $1B within 2-3 years; equity used; gross vs net

### Takeaway
The $100M-in-under-18-months club is real but small, and nearly every member either raised $60M-$600M+ of equity before the milestone or runs a net-fee exchange/protocol with near-zero marginal cost; the only sourced cases that did it on roughly $0-10M of outside equity are Hyperliquid, Midjourney and Surge AI (plus Tether-style float businesses), none of which is a gross-revenue physical intermediary. No sourced case was found of a capital-light principal trader of physical goods reaching $1B of revenue in 2-3 years on $10M of equity.

- The $1B-in-2-3-years milestone on small equity has only been achieved where (a) marginal cost is near zero and demand is pulled by a speculative or AI wave (Hyperliquid, Midjourney-class), or (b) the company is a contractor to a handful of AI labs with customers effectively prepaying (Surge AI). The implication for a back-to-back principal trader: hitting $100M of gross revenue is arithmetically easy (a few dozen $2-5M equipment trades), but the equivalent economic milestone is gross profit, and at 3-10% margins $100M of gross revenue is $3-10M of gross profit.
- Net-fee exchanges show the opposite optics: Polymarket's about 0.3% rate means $375M of revenue sits on more than $100B of annualized notional. A principal trader booking notional as revenue would show 100-300x the "revenue" for the same economics.

## 2. Accounting (ASC 606 principal vs agent) and how investors value gross-revenue, low-margin intermediaries

### Takeaway
Gross recognition requires control of the specified good before transfer; momentary "flash" title in a back-to-back or drop-ship trade does not by itself establish control, so the trader needs real fulfillment responsibility, inventory or performance risk, and price-setting to book notional. Even when gross is allowed, markets pay roughly 0.35-0.75x revenue for traders and distributors versus 9-16x for exchanges and data businesses (20-45x gap), so $100B of EV needs about $130-290B of trader revenue or about $6-11B of exchange/data revenue.

### Cited Findings

ASC 606 rules
- An entity is a principal if it controls the specified good or service before it is transferred to the customer (ASC 606-10-55-37); control means the ability to direct the use of, and obtain substantially all remaining benefits from, the asset (ASC 606-10-25-25). — [RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/principalagent-considerations-gross-vs-net)
- A principal can obtain control of (1) a good from another party that it then transfers, (2) a right to a service performed by another party that it directs, or (3) a good or service it combines with others into the specified item (ASC 606-10-55-37A); momentary control before transfer to the customer may not qualify. — [RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/principalagent-considerations-gross-vs-net)
- The three indicators (ASC 606-10-55-39) are primary responsibility for fulfillment, inventory risk before or after transfer (including return risk), and discretion in setting price; none is weighted above another, and agents sometimes have pricing flexibility too. — [RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/principalagent-considerations-gross-vs-net)
- The SEC staff has cautioned that the indicators are neither a checklist nor a substitute for the control assessment; inventory risk is only one possible indicator. — [Deloitte Roadmap 10.2](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/revenue/asc606-10/roadmap-revenue-recognition/chapter-10-principal-versus-agent-considerations/10-2-determining-whether-an-entity)
- The indicators do not override the control definition; the unit of account is each specified good or service, not the contract, so a reseller can be principal for some items and agent for others (ASU 2016-08). — [PwC Viewpoint 10.2](https://viewpoint.pwc.com/dt/us/en/pwc/accounting_guides/revenue_from_contrac/revenue_from_contrac_US/chapter_10_principa_US/10_2_principal_versus.html); [Deloitte Roadmap 10.4](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/revenue/asc606-10/roadmap-revenue-recognition/chapter-10-principal-versus-agent-considerations/10-4-contracts-in-which-entity)
- Deloitte's drop-ship style example concludes agent where the supplier sets the price and the entity lacks inventory risk; a principal may hold inventory risk because it "obtained control from a third party before transfer of the good to the customer". — [Deloitte Roadmap 10.3](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/revenue/asc606-10/roadmap-revenue-recognition/chapter-10-principal-versus-agent-considerations/10-3-determining-whether-an-entity)
- SEC comment-letter outcomes: Echo Global Logistics (freight broker) is principal/gross because it sets prices, directs carriers, handles claims and bears carrier-payment risk (2019); Bsquare (software reseller) reports over 95% of revenue gross on fulfillment responsibility, return risk and price-setting; Kontoor concessions gross because it owns inventory; McDonald's ad co-ops net because agencies perform and McDonald's has no unilateral control. — [RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/principalagent-considerations-gross-vs-net)
- Flexport's reporting shows the practical spread: about $3.2-3.3B gross revenue against about $660M "net revenue" in 2021. — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/flexport-at-3-3b-revenue/)

Public multiples (StockAnalysis, prices at 20 Nov 2026 close)

### Inferences
- Revenue needed for $100B EV at today's multiples: Glencore-type 0.35x implies about $286B; WESCO-type 0.75x implies about $133B; Copart-type 5.2x implies about $19B; Verisk 9.4x implies about $10.6B; ICE 10.8x implies about $9.2B; MSCI 14.7x implies about $6.8B; CME 16x implies about $6.2B.
- On EV/gross profit the gap narrows but persists: WESCO about 3.5x gross profit (EV 19.66 / (26.04 x 21.5%)), Copart about 11.5x, Verisk about 13.4x, MSCI about 17.7x, CME about 16x. Investors effectively price intermediaries on gross profit and EBITDA; booking notional as revenue changes the headline, not the valuation.
- For a back-to-back equipment trader to defend gross recognition under audit it needs evidence resembling Echo's fact pattern: its own contract with the customer for delivery, price set independently of the supplier, liability for non-performance, damage, warranty or returns, and supplier-payment obligation regardless of customer payment. A pass-through with flash title and supplier-set price points to net.
- A sophisticated Series B+ investor will restate a gross-revenue intermediary to net revenue or gross profit (as Sacra and CB Insights do for Flexport). "$1B revenue" at 5% gross margin is comparable to a $50M-gross-profit company.

### Gaps

The codification wording on legal title held "only momentarily" (ASC 606-10-55-37) should be quoted from the source before publication.
- No sourced example specific to back-to-back GPU, transformer or commodity trades under ASC 606 was found; IFRS 15 treatment of commodity traders (and the ASC 815/IFRS 9 treatment of physically settled commodity contracts as derivatives, which can force net presentation) was not researched.
- S&P Global and exchange peers outside the US were not pulled. One aggregator figure showing Deutsche Boerse at 20.2x EV/Revenue ([multiples.vc](https://multiples.vc/public-comps/deutsche-borse-valuation-multiples)) looks inconsistent with CME/ICE and should not be used without checking.
- Glencore's EV on StockAnalysis is shown without an explicit currency, and its multiple blends mining assets with trading; a pure-play listed trader comp was not found.

## 3. Working-capital structures that let intermediaries scale, and the failure cases

### Takeaway
The structures that actually work move the asset off the intermediary's balance sheet at the moment of origination (non-recourse forward flow, consignment, investor-financed contracts that customers sublease) or keep it self-liquidating and matched (trade finance against 20%+ equity). The failures share one pattern: the intermediary held duration, price or credit risk funded by short-term or confidence-sensitive capital, and a single input (insurance, freight rates, house prices, venture funding) moved.

### Cited Findings

Working examples
- Carvana/Ally forward flow: since December 2016 Carvana sells qualifying finance receivables to Ally under a committed forward-flow arrangement "without recourse to the Company for their post-sale performance"; it sold $3.6B (2023), $3.0B (2024) and $4.7B (2025) of principal; the commitment was raised to $6.0B (Oct 2025 to Oct 2026) and then to $8.0B (July 2026 to July 2027). — [Carvana FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1690820/000169082026000009/cvna-20251231.htm); [Carvana Q2 2026 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001690820/000169082026000055/cvna-20260630.htm)
- Copart consignment: salvage yards are paid either by consignment (flat unit fee) or by outright purchase and resale; by fiscal 1997 about 61% of Copart's sales were fixed-fee consignment and about one-third went through its purchase-type PIP program; today over 80% of revenue is service fees. Result: 45% gross margin and 35% operating margin with no vehicle inventory risk on most volume. — [FundingUniverse](https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/copart-inc-history); [Wikipedia](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Copart); [StockAnalysis CPRT](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/cprt/statistics/)
- SF Compute: long-term compute contracts are financed by outside investors and buyers can sublease spare capacity; the company takes about 10% and says it manages more than $100M of hardware after raising about $40M. — [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/sf-compute-ai-funding/)
- Trafigura (scaled principal trader): total assets $79.5B on group equity of $16.2B (just over 20%) at September 2025; adjusted debt to equity of minus 0.40x (inventories and trade finance treated as self-liquidating); in 2016-2018 it ran on $5.8-6.7B of equity against $41-53B of assets with adjusted net debt/equity of 1.14-1.48x. — [Trafigura FY2025 financial review](https://www.trafigura.com/news-and-insights/insights/2025-trafigura-annual-results/financial-review/); [Trafigura 2018 annual report](https://lib.nus.edu.sg/nus_hlc/annrep/trafint2018.pdf)
- Negative cash conversion cycle: exists when days payable exceed days inventory plus days receivable; marketplaces such as Amazon and eBay hold buyer cash for days before paying sellers. — [GoCardless](https://gocardless.com/en-us/guides/posts/what-is-a-negative-cash-conversion-cycle); [Resolve on Dell](https://resolvepay.com/blog/dells-negative-cash-conversion-cycle)

Failure cases
- Greensill (collapsed March 2021): trigger was loss of trade-credit insurance, which led Credit Suisse to suspend Greensill-linked funds and cut off funding; growth had depended on unusually concentrated exposures across insurers, funds and obligors that were not visible to fund investors or regulators. — [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/insights/trade-finance-lessons-from-recent-insolvencies/); [TXF](https://www.txfnews.com/articles/7154/Greensill-debacle-lessons-for-the-SCF-industry); [Berne Union](https://berneunion.org/Articles/Details/543/Greensill-Lessons-impact-and-opportunities-for-insurers)
- Opendoor 2022: revenue $15.57B, gross margin fell from 9.1% to 4.3%, $458M inventory write-downs, net loss $1.35B when house prices turned while it held inventory. — [Opendoor IR](https://investor.opendoor.com/news-releases/news-release-details/opendoor-announces-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2022-financial)
- Convoy (digital freight broker, shut October 2023): raised more than $800M of venture funding, valued at $3.8B after a $260M 2022 round that included $100M of venture debt from Hercules Capital; Hercules took control of the company at the end; management blamed a "massive freight recession" plus the funding drought. — [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/?p=88356); [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/the-markets-crushed-us-in-tearful-farewell-to-employees-convoy-execs-reflect-on-shutdown/); [FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/death-from-overfunding-an-obituary-for-convoy)
- Flexport: gross revenue fell from about $4-5B (2022) to about $1.6B (2023) purely on freight-rate normalization, illustrating that a principal's revenue is price times volume and not recurring. — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/flexport/); [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/flexport/financials)
- Zume: raised about $445M ($375M from SoftBank in 2018), dropped robot pizza in 2020, pivoted to packaging, shut down June 2023. — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2023/06/12/softbank-pizza-robot-shuts-down-zume-445-million); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-06-03/fallen-pizza-startup-zume-shuts-down-after-raising-millions)

### Inferences
- The durable pattern in the working examples is "originate, do not hold": Carvana originates loans and sells them under a committed, non-recourse line; Copart sells cars it never owns; SF Compute arranges investor-funded hardware. Each still controls the customer relationship and the fee.
- A thinly capitalized principal trader cannot replicate Trafigura's model at scale: even today's most conservative large trader carries equity of about 20% of assets, and it ran at 11-14% of assets in 2016-2018. On $10M of equity that supports roughly $50-90M of open trade positions at any moment unless positions are fully customer-prefunded and back-to-back.
- Every failure case had a single exogenous price or confidence variable: Greensill (insurance renewal), Opendoor (house prices over a 3-6 month hold), Convoy and Flexport (freight spot rates), and venture-debt covenants accelerating the end at Convoy. For an AI-hardware intermediary the equivalent variables are GPU/equipment spot prices, delivery slippage and AI-credit availability (see section 6).

### Gaps
- No sourced real-world examples were retrieved for customer deposits funding supplier deposits, back-to-back letters of credit, warehouse lines or escrow in equipment trading. Unverified recollection: Dell's build-to-order model ran a cash conversion cycle around minus 30 to minus 40 days in the 2000s; Tesla funded Model 3 tooling partly with customer reservations; transferable and back-to-back LCs are the standard thin-equity tool in commodity trading but collapsed for several Singapore traders (Hin Leong, Agritrade, 2020) when banks found the same cargo pledged multiple times.
- Pipe (revenue-financing marketplace, 2022 leadership change) and WeWork (long lease liabilities against short memberships, Chapter 11 in November 2023) were searched but no usable results were returned; treat as unverified.
- Details of how neoclouds use customer prepayments and take-or-pay contracts as collateral for GPU-backed debt (CoreWeave DDTL structures) were not retrieved.

## 4. The arc "market-maker first, then standardized product/owned IP, then data and financial rail"

### Takeaway
The arc is real (ICE and Copart are the cleanest sourced cases) but slow: ICE took about 5 years from formation to IPO and about 25 years to roughly $100B EV, and Copart took about 20 years to become an all-online, fee-based platform. In both, margin expansion came when the firm stopped being a risk-taking counterparty and became the standardized venue (clearing, online auction) that others had to use.

### Cited Findings
- ICE: Jeffrey Sprecher bought the failing Continental Power Exchange in 1996/1997 for $1 (other accounts say $1,000) plus assumed debt; ICE was formed in 2000 as an internet platform for OTC energy trading, backed by Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, BP, Total, Shell, Deutsche Bank and Societe Generale; it acquired the International Petroleum Exchange in 2001, listed on the NYSE in 2005, bought NYBOT (2007) and Creditex (2008), and reported $9.9B revenue in 2023 with dedicated Fixed Income and Data Services and Mortgage Technology segments. — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/Articles/2632366/Jeffrey-Sprecher.html); [FIA Hall of Fame](https://www.fia.org/hall-fame/articles/jeff-sprecher); [ICE interview](https://www.ice.com/insights/conversations/inside-the-ice-house/ice-founder-and-ceo-jeff-sprecher-two-decades-of-disrupting-markets)
- ICE today: EV $118B, revenue $10.9B, operating margin 53%. — [StockAnalysis ICE](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/ice/statistics/)
- ICE's 2025 commitment of up to $2B to Polymarket shows the incumbent rail buying into the next market-maker rather than the reverse. — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/polymarket-at-375m-year-revenue/)
- Copart: founded 1982; mixed consignment and purchase (PIP) programs through the 1990s; converted all salvage facilities to the internet-only VB2 auction in December 2003, eliminating live auctions; later patented the platform; now over 80% of revenue is platform service fees, with 45% gross and 35% operating margins. — [Copart history](https://copart.com/Content/US/EN/About-Copart/Our-History); [Automotive Fleet](https://www.automotive-fleet.com/news/copart-converts-all-salvage-facilities-to-virtual-bidding-second-generation-vb2-system-v2); [FundingUniverse](https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/copart-inc-history); [StockAnalysis CPRT](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/cprt/statistics/)
- CME: exchange model at 66% operating margin and 16x EV/Sales on $6.96B revenue. — [StockAnalysis CME](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/cme/statistics/)
- Carvana: retail principal that layered a financing rail on top, selling $4.7B of originated receivables to Ally in 2025 under forward flow. — [Carvana FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1690820/000169082026000009/cvna-20251231.htm)
- Flexport: after 10+ years and about $2.5B raised, still about 20% net-revenue margin and about 1% net margin at its 2021 peak; the "freight forwarder to data/finance platform" transition has not produced exchange-like margins in the sourced data. — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/flexport-at-3-3b-revenue/)
- Shopify monetized payments volume and later added Shopify Capital merchant financing, cited as the canonical "platform then financial rail" sequence. — [NFX](https://nfx.com/post/new-operating-manual-fintech-enabled-marketplaces)
- Prediction markets compress the arc: Kalshi went from about $735M estimated annualized revenue at end 2025 to a company-stated $1.5B+ by May 2026 as a regulated exchange (venue from day one, not a principal). — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/kalshi-at-3b-year/); [The Block](https://theblock.co/post/400413/kalshi-hits-22-billion-valuation-after-1-billion-raise-led-by-coatue)

### Inferences
- Timeline base rate from the two cleanest cases: 4-8 years from first trades to a standardized venue that customers treat as the default (ICE 1997-2001/2005; Copart 1982-2003 for the full online conversion), and decades to $25-120B EV. Nothing sourced supports completing all three stages inside 3 years.
- What made the transitions work in the sourced cases: (1) ICE enlisted the largest liquidity providers as backers, so the venue launched with flow; (2) acquisition of regulated infrastructure (IPE, NYBOT, clearing) rather than organic build; (3) Copart replaced a physical process with a proprietary online format that widened the buyer pool, then moved revenue from spread on purchased cars to fees.
- Margin expansion coincides with exiting principal risk: Copart at 45% gross margin on fees versus Opendoor at 4-9% as a principal in a comparable "buy/sell a big-ticket asset" category; Flexport stuck near 20% net-revenue margin while remaining a forwarder.

### Gaps
- Not researched: Bloomberg, Stripe, Coupang, Tesla Energy, John Deere telemetry, Samsara, and CME's own history. No sourced facts are offered for them.
- Unverified recollection on ICE: the founding banks and energy majors received equity in exchange for committing order flow, and ICE's 2005 IPO-year revenue was in the $150M range; data and recurring revenue now make up roughly half of ICE's revenue. None of this was confirmed in-session.
- No historical gross-margin series for Copart or ICE was retrieved, so the exact year margins inflected is not established.

## 5. Disintermediation: when brokers and marketplaces get bypassed, and what prevents it

### Takeaway
The best causal evidence (a randomized trial) shows that building trust between the two sides increases bypass once trust is high enough, which is exactly the situation after a first successful high-ticket trade; the practitioner literature says high-value, low-frequency B2B deals are the worst case for take rates. The claimed defenses (escrow, financing, embedded services) are documented as monetization and trust tools, but I found no rigorous evidence quantifying how much leakage they prevent.

### Cited Findings
- Gu and Zhu (Management Science 67(2), 2021), randomized controlled trial on a large online freelance marketplace: enhanced trust raises hiring of high-quality freelancers, but when trust is sufficiently high it also increases disintermediation, offsetting the revenue gains; bypass was detected by scanning chats for phrases such as "my phone number", "avoid fees" and "Venmo". — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:67:y:2021:i:2:p:794-807); [HBS working paper](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hbs:wpaper:18-103)
- a16z: disintermediation is driven by price sensitivity, convenience or necessity and suppresses growth and monetization; founders of low-frequency marketplaces face elevated disintermediation and competition risk. — [a16z marketplace glossary](https://a16z.com/the-marketplace-glossary/); [a16z, four paths](https://a16z.com/four-paths-to-marketplace-success/)
- In high-value markets with infrequent orders, a take rate on purchase orders "will be perceived as unfair, especially when suppliers and buyers know each other already". — [TechCrunch, Nov 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/11/04/b2b-marketplaces-will-be-the-next-billion-dollar-e-commerce-startups/amp)
- Suppliers may see the take rate as an unfair tax; marketplaces respond by embedding banking, early-payment discounts, pre-approved financing and insurance so that they are a place to obtain financial services as well as to transact. — [Bain Capital Ventures interview via Bowery](https://bowerycap.com/blog/insights/investing-in-b2b-marketplaces-merritt-hummer-allison-xu-bain-capital-ventures)
- Escrow "increases service trust, increases speed of transaction and adds to take rate through commission" (NFX's Pete Flint, in a 2021 report that is consumer-leaning). — [Dealroom/Adevinta/Speedinvest](https://dealroom.co/uploaded/2021/09/Dealroom-adevinta-speedinvest-Fintech-enabled-Marketplaces-2021.pdf)
- Counter-view: a 2025 paper argues some disintermediation can raise platform value by expanding the user base and network effects. — [MPRA paper 126803](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/126803/)
- Structural counter-examples where intermediaries are not bypassed despite repeat counterparties: Copart (over 80% of revenue from fees; sellers are a few insurers, buyers are repeat dismantlers) and CME/ICE (66% and 53% operating margins) rely on title processing, storage, clearing and a proprietary auction or central counterparty rather than on introductions. — [Wikipedia: Copart](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Copart); [StockAnalysis CME](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/cme/statistics/)
- Echo Global Logistics is treated as principal precisely because it contracts with the shipper, directs the carrier, handles claims and bears payment risk; the broker that takes principal obligations is contractually in the middle of every load. — [RevenueHub](https://www.revenuehub.org/principalagent-considerations-gross-vs-net)

### Inferences
- A pure introducer in $1M+ equipment trades should expect the take rate on repeat business between the same two counterparties to trend toward zero; the Gu-Zhu result suggests a successful first trade makes this worse.
- The intermediaries that persist do one of: (a) stand in the middle as contractual principal or central counterparty (credit, delivery and warranty risk), (b) hold the money (escrow, clearing, financing), (c) own a standardized process or format the counterparties cannot replicate bilaterally (auction, contract spec, title/compliance handling), or (d) aggregate many-to-many liquidity so each trade is with a different counterparty.
- Acting as principal therefore serves two purposes at once: it supports gross recognition under ASC 606 and it is the main structural defense against bypass. Both come at the cost of real balance-sheet risk.

### Gaps
- No quantitative leakage rates for B2B high-ticket marketplaces (percentage of repeat volume going off-platform) were found.
- No direct evidence that escrow, financing, warranty or exclusivity reduce bypass; sources only assert it. Exclusivity and non-circumvention clause enforceability were not researched.
- Xie and Zhu's platform-leakage pricing experiment and Salikhov's home-cleaning study were identified but not read.

### Inferences
- The cycle is bifurcating by counterparty credit: top-four hyperscalers are still accelerating with spreads under 100bp, while BBB/high-yield and project-finance AI credit has repriced and issuance has dropped sharply. An intermediary selling into the build-out should segment its book by who ultimately pays.
- Delays are increasingly caused by physical bottlenecks (power, equipment, third-party capacity) rather than lack of intent, which sustains scarcity premiums for equipment brokers in the short run but raises the probability of buyer non-performance and order cancellation on long-lead back-to-back trades.
- A startup whose first $100M of revenue comes from this market in 2027 is implicitly long a cycle in which 2027 guidance from the largest buyers is still "more", but the marginal (neocloud/developer) buyer is already capital-constrained. Customer prepayment and credit quality matter more than volume.

### Gaps

- Q3 2026 earnings-call capex figures were not checked against company filings; Microsoft's calendar/fiscal definitions cause frequent confusion across sources.
- No confirmed report of a new Microsoft lease pause in 2026 was found. Nvidia guidance and GPU spot/rental price trends were not researched.

## 7. How many companies have reached $100B and $1T; how long it took; share that began as intermediaries

About 11-17 companies were worth $1T+ during 2026 (none existed before 2018) and well over 100 are above $100B; historically reaching $100B took 7-30 years, with only the 2023-26 AI labs doing it in under 5 (xAI 2.3 years, Anthropic 4.5), all on tens of billions of invested capital.
