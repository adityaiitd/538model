# Hundred billion company simulation

24 November 2026

## Verdict

No business I tested meets your requirements, and I would not commit $10 million and 20 years to any of them.

I screened 31 ideas, took seven businesses through independent adversarial panels in ten versions, and redesigned after each round. No version drew better than a 4% simulated chance of $1bn of revenue in seven years. No version kept a path to $100bn of equity value once the panels corrected the numbers.

The closest is contracted onsite power for AI data centers, entered by buying an operating generator fleet. Buyers in the simulation signed for it at $46,000-52,000 per MW-month. But it is a leveraged asset pool with a realistic ceiling of a $0.8-2.5bn sale, in which a sponsor would give you a 5-8% stake and the commercial role, not the chief executive's. It scored 38 out of 100.

My decision is to found none of these today. Spend about $350,000 and 30 days on the four tests in section 17. They decide whether the closest opportunity is worth pursuing as what it is, a private equity platform, and they check the three conditions that would reopen a larger one.

## How the work was done, and its limits

Six research agents built a fact base on compute, power, physical production, financial infrastructure, health and biotech, and AI-delivered services. Their notes, with a URL on every claim, are in [research_notes](<../research_notes/Hundred billion company simulation/>).

I then ran 26 panel sessions. Each panel was a separate agent that saw only a written founder brief and the research notes. Each played seven to eleven stakeholders, was told to pursue those stakeholders' interests and not to help the founder, and could run a few web checks of its own. Round one used three panels per candidate: buyers, delivery, and capital with competition. Redesigns went back to the same panels so they could not quietly drop an objection. New candidates and the last redesign also faced fresh panels.

Four limits matter.

1. Everything the panels said is simulated. There were no real interviews, orders, deposits or commitments.
2. The panels run on the same model that I do, so they are separate but not truly independent. Their probabilities are opinions. The useful output is the sourced facts they found that broke each brief.
3. The method is built to reject. It would probably have rejected OpenEvidence or Mercor before they worked. It protects against the wrong company better than it finds the rare right one.
4. The shared web-search budget ran out in every research stream and every panel. Gaps are listed in each notes file. Many cost figures are labelled "domain knowledge, unverified" in the transcripts. One vendor page fetched during research contained text addressed to AI assistants. The agent ignored it.

## What the simulation found

1. In every candidate the scarce input belonged to someone else, who kept the margin: the miner, the breaker maker, the utility, the lead insurer, the records vendor, the model lab, the fleet owner.
2. Buyers discounted a start-up's long-dated promise to zero. Guarantees, price floors, wraps and warranties from a thin balance sheet did not sell in any market.
3. Creditworthy buyers supplied themselves. The buyers who would sign were weak credits, and that blocked the financing.
4. Selling AI labor at a discount did not hold. Rivals have the same agents, and price falls to cost.
5. A commodity asset under contract is competed down to an 8-10% return.
6. In five of seven businesses the panels' own condition for "yes" was to buy a holder of the scarce input: a billing firm, a stake in a mine, a seat inside a rated insurer, a breaker source, a generator fleet. When I tested that directly, the price of buying in had already absorbed the gain.

Points 1 and 6 are the answer to your question. With $5-10M and no proprietary technology, captive distribution or licence, the paths to $100bn that exist today run through owning compute, power or models at a scale that needs billions early. Late 2026 is three years into that race. Assets and allocations are held and priced.

## What accumulates

Nothing that a competitor cannot also buy. After 100 blocks (2.5 GW) the company has pooled spares, trained crews, a record of measured availability and cheaper debt. After 1,000 blocks it would be the largest fleet in the country and would still be selling megawatt-months on the same engines as everyone else. There is no network effect: one customer's megawatt is worth nothing more to it because another customer exists. The operating data is real but every fleet owner has the same data on the same engine models. This is a scale business, not a compounding one.

## The 18 items, for the closest opportunity

I give all 18 for onsite power so you can see exactly what the closest case looks like. Items 15 and 18 say plainly where it falls short.

### 1. The business in one sentence

We rent small gas power plants to AI data centers that cannot get enough electricity from the grid yet.

### 2. The exact first product

A firm megawatt for a month. It is delivered as a 25-50 MW islanded plant at the customer's site: gas gensets with spare units, a battery that follows the swings of AI load and covers an engine trip, switchgear, fuel management and a crew on site around the clock. The first plants use units from the acquired rental fleet, refitted for continuous duty.

### 3. Who buys, why, and what they pay

In the simulation two kinds of buyer signed. A neocloud with GPUs on order and no power date took 40-50 MW for five years at $48,000-52,000 per MW-month with a $2-3M deposit. A colocation developer whose utility date had slipped took 40-60 MW as a 36-month bridge at $46,000-50,000. Both wanted speed. Hyperscalers refused: they pay incumbents $32,000-38,000 and want 200 MW minimum and a record. Fuel is passed through.

### 4. Why now and not five years ago

Five years ago grid power for a large load took one to two years and nobody paid a premium to avoid the wait. Today the wait is three to seven years, turbines are sold into the 2030s, and a month of delay costs an operator with idle GPUs an estimated $350,000-830,000 per megawatt (panel estimate). Ten-to-fifteen-year contracts for onsite power from investment-grade buyers did not exist before 2024.

### 5. The existing revenue pool

About 2 GW of onsite power was running for data centers in mid-2026, worth roughly $0.9bn a year at $455,000 per MW-year. Solaris alone holds 3.2 GW of contracts and a backlog over $16bn ([Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/417401/solaris-doubles-the-eps-consensus-and-raises-guidance-again-but-a-services-heavy-mix-thins-margins-and-the-stock-has-gone-nowhere)). Oracle reportedly pays VoltaGrid more than $1bn a year for one site ([Bloomberg via Energy Connects](https://www.energyconnects.com/news/gas-lng/2026/february/voltagrid-weighing-public-listing-or-sale-riding-the-ai-wave)). If a third of the 90-177 GW announced is built, the pool is $14-27bn a year. The market panel put a 10% share of the whole US market at $12-15bn of enterprise value.

### 7. Technology and intellectual property

None is proprietary. The engineering that matters is load-following control for AI workloads on an islanded plant, emissions catalysts, and fuel redundancy for 99.99% availability. All of it can be bought.

### 8. Competitors

VoltaGrid (4.3 GW planned, $5bn of debt, Blackstone equity), Solaris (3.2 GW contracted), Liberty, ProPetro, Williams, Bloom Energy, Caterpillar dealers, and rental majors. They have fleets, engine slots, crews, records and cheaper capital. The simulation found no way to beat them. The entrant can only serve the buyers they decline: sites under 100 MW, weak credit, terms under seven years. Those buyers pay more, and that is the whole business.

### 9. Main objections and how the business evolved

See the ledger table above. The business moved from batteries on a grid-flexibility thesis, to islanded gas engines, to buying a fleet. Each move answered the previous objections and each ended at the same place: the entrant owns nothing scarce.

### 13. Capital before the first commercial delivery

Buying 100 nameplate megawatts costs $150-210M. Refitting 30 firm megawatts costs $20-27M. The battery, switchgear, site work, fees and working capital add $25-40M. Total about $200-280M, of which lenders provide 40-47% of the purchase price and a sponsor provides the rest. Your own money at risk before closing is $1.5-2M for search and diligence, plus a hard deposit of $3-5M that the seller in the simulation required alongside an equity letter. Starting from nothing instead of buying a fleet costs $30-45M to a first operating block and takes 18-24 months.

### 15. Expansion path to $100bn

There is not a credible one. At $10bn of revenue the business needs about 20 GW and $42bn of capital, and the capital panel judged that unreachable for this entrant. Even there, asset equity is worth $13-35bn. Reaching $100bn would mean becoming a private utility for compute at 50 GW or more: owning generation, fuel supply, storage and retail power for large loads. Constellation and NextEra show such companies can be worth that much. They took decades and started with regulated assets. An entrant that begins with a 5-8% stake in a 100 MW fleet in 2027 is not on that path.

### 16. The three assumptions most likely to destroy the business

1. That a fleet with enough large units can be bought at $1.8M per megawatt or less, and that a sponsor will back you to run it. The panels put the chance of closing at 15-18%.
2. That the shortage and prices above $40,000 last past 2028, when incumbent engine deliveries and several gigawatts of rival capacity arrive. The market panel expected $30,000-34,000 by 2029. At $30,000 the equity is worth nothing.
3. That buyers accept a new operator running an islanded plant on one gas line. The field veteran put year-one availability at 98.5-99.5% against the 99.9% that contracts require.

### 17. Experiments that can settle these in 30 days

Budget about $350,000: an M&A adviser's retainer, counsel, an engineering consultant and travel.

| Test | What to do | Pass | Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Is there a fleet to buy? | Approach every private gas generator rental owner with 50-150 MW through an adviser. | Three confidentiality agreements and one indicative price at or below $1.8M per nameplate megawatt with at least 30 firm megawatts usable for compute | No owner engages, or prices sit at $2.1M or above |
| B. Will a buyer commit money? | Send a one-page term sheet to the ten targets in section 10. | One signed letter of intent for 25 MW or more at $42,000 or above with a $1M deposit into escrow | Interest without a deposit. That is not demand |
| C. Will a sponsor back you? | Meet six sponsors with tests A and B in hand. | One written indication to fund $130M or more with you as chief executive and a stake of 8% or more | Every sponsor offers the commercial role only |
| D. Is there equipment for growth? | Ask two dealers and one Korean maker for written allocation. | 100 MW deliverable by mid-2028 at a stated price | 2029 or later |

Run one more test in parallel, because it reopens a larger business. Ask three utilities with flexible-connection programs for a letter stating that a named site would be energized on a stated earlier date if the customer installs a battery or generator of a stated size. One such letter revives the first version of the power candidate, where the scarce input would be the utility agreement and you would be among the first to hold one.

### 18. Final investment decision

No. I would not commit $10 million and the next 20 years to this business or to any of the other six.

I would spend $350,000 and 30 days on the tests above. If tests A, B and C all pass, the honest description of what you would then own is a minority stake in a private equity platform with a five-year exit worth $20-100M to you in the base case. That may be worth doing. It is not the company you asked me to find.

## What would change this conclusion

The simulation rejected ten versions. Three developments would reopen the question, and each can be watched.

1. A regulator or utility puts in force a rule that grants a dated earlier energization in return for flexibility at 50 MW or more. FERC told six grid operators in June 2026 to justify or reform their large-load rules. When it rejected PJM's curtailment plan it pointed PJM toward a voluntary non-firm service that flexible loads could choose. If this happens the scarce input becomes a signed utility agreement, which a new entrant can obtain.
2. Forced selling of compute or power assets. The financing pause, 15% yields and customer concentration at the neoclouds make this plausible. A buyer with capital and no legacy debt would then acquire scarce inputs below cost. That is the opposite of today's auction prices.
3. Evidence that the method was wrong about a capital-light candidate. If a real asset manager signs a $250,000 annual sponsorship for adviser attention without named leads, or a lab signs a 12-month non-exclusive licence, the panels' central objection to C7 or C6 falls. Those tests cost little.

I did not test owning compute directly. I screened it out because it needs billions and chip allocation that you do not have. It is the one category in the research where a company founded in 2024 reached $140M of revenue in a half-year, and it did so after raising $3.7bn and while losing $1.02bn in that same half. If your access to institutional capital is at that scale before validation, not after, that screen should be rerun.
